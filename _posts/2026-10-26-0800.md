## 2. PaperPilot – Zero-Cloud, Pure-Rust PDF Engine with WASM and MCP {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/KrushnaVardhanReddy/PaperPilot) · 101 points · 25 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50238739)*

PaperPilot 是一个用 Rust 编写的开源 PDF/文档工具包（Apache 2.0），作者称其为一个核心引擎驱动五种界面：CLI、Tauri 2 + Svelte 5 桌面应用、浏览器内的 WASM 版本（usepaperpilot.com）、监听 localhost:7823 的本地 REST 网关，以及供 Claude Desktop、Cursor 等客户端调用的 MCP 服务器。它建立在 lopdf、pdf-writer、rten（纯 Rust PaddleOCR）和 image 等库之上，提供合并、拆分、旋转、N-up、文本/OCR 提取、水印、签名校验、PII 正则脱敏等 75 多项操作，宣称单次操作延迟约 20–130ms，且不向任何服务器上传文件。路线图列出 v0.7 的 PDF↔Markdown/HTML/JSON、v0.8 的 PDF→Office、v0.9 的加密/签名/涂黑/表单，以及单独的 Enterprise 版（SSO、RBAC、审计日志、DLP）。

**Discussion**: 最尖锐的质疑是作者身份与出处：有人问这个引擎是否由 AI 拼凑、并从 MuPDF 的 C 源码转写而来，认为若如此应改用 AGPLv3 而非 Apache 2.0；另一位评论者纠正说它用的是 Rust 的 hayro，作者则在帖中强调底层是 lopdf、pdf-writer 和 image。其次是一轮「这有什么必要」的争论：有评论者说现有命令行工具早已能做同样的事，把项目读成「只认网页、害怕终端」；反驳者指出它同时提供 CLI，而且 WASM 版能在没有命令行的场合（比如朋友的手机上）直接跑。也有不少具体需求：官方 GUI、已知密码的解锁、添加签名、替代 Apache Tika 接入 Open-WebUI、PDF 转 ePub 后用 Calibre 修。另有人挑刺说 Tauri + Svelte 并不算「原生」，是 LLM 写文案时的用词错误，并顺带问作者为何选用较少见的 Antigravity 工具链、体验如何。整体气氛是礼貌的兴趣多过认同，隐私卖点被接受，但差异化仍被反复追问。

**Worth reading**
- [colesantiago](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50239092): 质疑项目是否「vibe-coded」且由 MuPDF 源码从 C 改写而来，主张若属实必须披露并将许可证改为 AGPLv3。
- [ligarota](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50240082): 直接反驳上述指控，指出项目实际使用的是 Rust 的 hayro 库。
- [smartbit](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50239683): 称赞 COMPETITORS.md 里的竞品分析比帖中列表更好读，并追问作者为何选择少见的 Antigravity 工具链、用的什么 LLM、体验如何。

## 4. Show HN: A Truly Modular Keyboard {#story-4}
*#5 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/notaroomba/keybytes) · 116 points · 25 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50228115)*

A GitHub hardware project by NotARoomba (also the poster) for a keyboard where every key is its own 19.05mm square PCB: a CH32V003 RISC-V microcontroller per key, pogo pins carrying 3.3V, GND and I2C on all four sides, magnetic cases to hold the tiles together, and a two-board main unit with an STM32WB55RGV6 for Bluetooth/USB, 2MB QSPI flash, a BQ25895 charger and a TPS631000 buck-boost converter, all designed in KiCad. The stated goal is keyboards in arbitrary shapes — the author says he was annoyed that triangle- and circle-shaped keyboards don't exist. So far only two key modules have been assembled; the author is waiting on switches to build five.

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly enthusiastic but the practical questions go unanswered by hardware that exists yet. The author repeatedly concedes the limits: rigidity and drop behaviour are untested beyond two keys, staggered/non-ortholinear layouts and big keys like spacebar and shift aren't supported in this revision (both are on the list for the next one), there's no mouse module yet, and per-key cost is about $6 at higher volume with no retail price given. Engineers picked at the electrical design: the I2C-per-key scheme drew a 127-address-limit and bus-hang warning with CAN suggested as an alternative, and one commenter who read the PCB and schematic asked why there are four separate I2C buses — the author says it cuts parasitic capacitance and lets firmware infer physical position, with each key remembering its nearest-to-main-board 「parent」 and forwarding events hop by hop. The harshest reply argues the thing is non-viable as a product — cost, weight, whether a 104-key layout can be lifted without splitting, lack of redundancy across many pogo-pin joints, and no automatic function remapping after the tiles scatter. Recurring smaller asks: blank spacer modules to fill gaps, and a full-layout demo instead of two keys.

**Worth reading**
- [SV_BubbleTime](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50239179): A commenter who read the schematic and layouts calls the PCB work polished, not a first draft, but can't work out how address assignment across four I2C buses is supposed to function.
- [mrheosuper](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50240184): Warns that I2C caps out at 127 addresses and is prone to bus hangs with that many slaves, suggesting CAN as a more robust interconnect.
- [M95D](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50240378): A detailed rejection arguing the design fails as a product: unknown sell price, weight and lift-ability of a 104-key build, no redundancy across hundreds of pogo-pin contacts, no staggered layout, no big keys, and no way to recover key assignments after the tiles fall apart.

## 5. The Curse of Soldered RAM {#story-5}
*#7 on HN · [blog.hardill.me.uk](https://blog.hardill.me.uk/2026/10/24/the-curse-of-soldered-ram/) · 52 points · 40 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50221394)*

The post is a Fedora user's account of a 2021 Lenovo X1 Carbon whose soldered RAM went bad: BTRFS reported checksum failures and remounted the root filesystem read-only, and memtest86+ produced errors almost immediately. With no DIMM to swap, he quarantines the faulty addresses using the kernel's memmap= option, working around a grub2-mkconfig bug that mangled $0 by storing the range list in grubenv with grub2-editenv. His argument is that soldered RAM turns a cheap, user-replaceable repair into a whole-laptop replacement, especially painful during the current memory-price shortage, with Framework noted as a rare exception.

**Discussion**: Commenters largely agree that soldered RAM is a repairability and longevity problem, with several saying the pursuit of thinner laptops is not worth it and that vendors also use soldering to extract more money for memory upgrades. The counter-argument is that buyers demand thin, light and water-resistant machines and that sockets add height and cost; one commenter notes RAM prices are not falling anyway. LPCAMM2 is raised as the intended best-of-both compromise, but commenters say it has seen little adoption: one says no component supplier, not even Alibaba, lists LPCAMM2 sockets, another finds only about $30-per-socket pricing, and others note that Framework's new 13 Pro uses it and a recent ThinkPad T14 does too, while lamenting the lack of ECC. On repair, commenters say BGA RAM can be reworked with hot air or professional equipment, but sourcing chips, changing capacity straps or firmware, and preserving warranty make it impractical for most. Others point to Lenovo models that still mix soldered and SODIMM memory, and one describes moving a tiny resistor to make a laptop ignore its soldered RAM.

**Worth reading**
- [ACCount39](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50240331): Explains that soldered RAM is technically replaceable, but the real blockers are BGA rework skill and equipment, chip sourcing, and device-specific strap resistors or firmware that tell the machine how much RAM it has.
- [pseudohadamard](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50227696): A Lenovo E16 owner reports 8GB soldered plus a 32GB SODIMM for 40GB total and says it outperforms the X1, while criticizing Lenovo for replacing the Carbon's carbon-fiber case with plastic.
- [nicman23](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50239905): Describes moving a very small resistor so a laptop would ignore its soldered RAM and use only the SODIMM.

## 6. Overgrown, when plants invade your abandoned tabs {#story-6}
*#8 on HN · [charliegerard.dev](https://charliegerard.dev/blog/overgrown-extension/) · 250 points · 80 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50197108)*

Charlie Gerard built a small Chrome extension called Overgrown, based on a creative exercise around the word 「overgrowth」: each tab runs a neglect clock while it is out of view, and vines grow from the edges toward the middle. Once a tab is more than 5% overgrown, the page is walled off — you can scroll but not click, select or drag — until you slash the vines away by dragging the mouse; at 5% the remainder falls off and the jungle restarts if you ignore the tab again. Growth speed is configurable, you can pick a rusty machete, chainsaw or flamethrower, and Gerard says she considered blocking users from closing overgrown tabs but dropped it as too annoying. She wrote the post with 22 tabs open, 14 of them already fully overgrown.

**Discussion**: Commenters split into two camps: tab hoarders who defend the practice and people who close everything daily and treat it as a pathology. The hoarders argue tabs are effectively ephemeral bookmarks or history items — one reports roughly 12,000 open tabs across machines and profiles, another says YouTube alone breeds hundreds of half-watched tabs — while the clean-slate camp says anything unvisited for days belongs in bookmarks or browser history and that closing is therapeutic. That sparked a side argument about whether browser history is a reliable substitute: one commenter notes Chrome only keeps 90 days of it, and another says history is poorly searchable without manually stuffing keywords into bookmark titles. A long sub-thread on deliberately keeping no history turned into a privacy dispute, with one side citing border-crossing device searches as the reason and others calling it paranoia since anyone with access can see browsing anyway. Practical responses were mostly feature requests: make the coverage percentage adjustable, make the plant art translucent for a 「yellowing and decay」 effect, add the option to block closing overgrown tabs, port it to Firefox, and combine it with tab unloading to reclaim RAM; several people just called it useless-but-delightful skeuomorphism, and one asked for the same treatment for old ChatGPT chats.

**Worth reading**
- [jdprgm](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50239103): A commenter estimates being stuck at about 12,000 open tabs across machines, browsers and profiles, and says getting one profile under 2,000 felt like progress — the thread's most extreme data point.
- [zeroq](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50237543): Someone with ADHD explains the hoarding honestly: each idea spawns a rabbit hole of 10 tabs meant to kick off a side project, revisited months later, and says the hoarded information vastly exceeds their throughput.
- [kccqzy](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50237250): A key correction to the 「just use history」 advice: at work they are required to use Chrome, which keeps history only 90 days, while an open tab can outlive that and survive restarts.

## 7. Attention Is Not What You Need (2025) {#story-7}
*#9 on HN · [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.19428) · 86 points · 29 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50237224)*

An arXiv preprint (cs.LG, submitted 22 Dec 2025, 16 KB) by Zhang Chong arguing that multi-head self-attention is really just 「tensor lifting」 into an opaque pairwise-interaction space, and proposing an attention-free alternative built on 「Grassmann flows」: a Causal Grassmann layer that projects token states, encodes local token pairs as 2D subspaces on a Grassmann manifold via Plücker coordinates, and fuses them back with gated mixing. Results are modest: 13–18M-parameter Grassmann models reach Wikitext-2 validation perplexity within roughly 10–15% of size-matched Transformers, and a Grassmann-Plücker head on DistilBERT edges out a Transformer head on SNLI (0.8550/0.8538 vs 0.8545/0.8511). The paper claims linear scaling in sequence length at fixed rank and no code link.

**Discussion**: The sharpest thread is a suspected causality bug: mapontosevenths reads the paper as pairing (z_t, z_{t+Δ}) with positive Δ and then folding g_t = geometric_mix(z_t, z_{t+Δ}) into h_t at step t, i.e. leaking the next token into the current prediction. magicalhippo initially reads the excerpt as innocuous future-token neighbourhoods but concedes the point, and dig finds two third-party reproduction repos, Infatoshi's grassmann-flows and kanenorman's grassmann, that both reverse the pairing to (current, past) — the first reporting underwhelming results, the second roughly matching the paper, which nobody resolves. A second cluster attacks the evidence base: commenters say an 18M-parameter model on Wikitext-2 proves nothing about architectures, noting Transformers' real advantage is that they scale, while others point to Qwen 3.5's ~75% linear-attention layers as proof that attention-free designs already work in production and that architecture only matters when compute is constrained. A third argument is about rigor and provenance: one long comment calls the writing LLM-generated and demands figures, defined metrics and more completeness, prompting replies that the critique itself reads like LLM output, plus the standard reminder that arXiv is not peer-reviewed — countered by several reviewers-by-experience who say official peer review is often no better. Nobody defends the paper's experimental scale; the rough consensus is that the idea is interesting but unproven and possibly buggy.

**Worth reading**
- [mapontosevenths](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50239237): Claims the pairing (z_t, z_{t+Δ}) with positive Δ, combined with the gate mixing g_t into h_t, means the model uses the next token to predict the next token — i.e. it is either a sign error or cheating.
- [magicalhippo](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50240260): Locates two reproduction repos that both use (current, past) instead of (current, future), one getting underwhelming results and the other roughly matching the paper, leaving the causality question unresolved.
- [oofbey](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50239044): Argues an 18M-parameter model on an ancient dataset proves nothing, and that the key property of Transformers is enabling easy training of arbitrarily large models.

## 8. IBM CEO says open weight models are now good and cheap on-prem {#story-8}
*#10 on HN · [theregister.com](https://www.theregister.com/systems/2026/10/22/ibm-ceo-says-open-weight-models-are-now-irresistibly-good-and-cheap-on-prem/5304640) · 198 points · 103 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50238088)*

A Register write-up of IBM CEO Arvind Krishna's remarks on the Q3 2026 earnings call: the gap between closed frontier models and open weight ones has closed from roughly 70-vs-20/30 on broad benchmarks in 2024 to 70-vs-50 in late 2025, and open models now deliver about 90% of frontier effectiveness at 10–25% of the token cost. Krishna argues most enterprise tasks (summarizing email, reading contracts, customer calls) don't need the expensive tier, that useful AI infrastructure fits in two or three racks, and that the real barrier is staffing — kernel engineers, networking, GPU placement, caching — which he hopes IBM software will paper over, alongside sales of Spyre accelerators for z17. The piece also lists IBM's quarterly numbers (total revenue $17.1B, up 5%; infrastructure $3.7B, up 5%; software $7.8B, up 9%; Red Hat up 11%; mainframe revenue down 20%) and an unrelated severity-one IBM Cloud incident that left Classic Cloud provisioning down for 16+ hours.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people who already run local models and people who think the CEO is talking his book. The most detailed first-hand account comes from a commenter running an 8-GPU rig (about $25K, 240GB VRAM, 2.5kW limited to 1.5kW, 100–150 tok/s) who hasn't touched a paid API in months — but who concedes it is not cheaper than a frontier lab, and that independence and keeping client data under NDA is the actual reason. Others counter that a Claude Max subscription costs less than a decade of that hardware, though several reply that subscriptions are VC-subsidized and will be repriced after IPOs. A second camp argues $25K is the wrong number entirely: one commenter runs Qwen on 4 second-hand V100S cards bought at ~$750 each and calls it roughly Sonnet-level for agentic coding, and the most-repeated benefit across the thread is not price but unrestricted use — no refusals, no rate limits, no bans, no data leaving the building. On IBM itself the tone is skeptical: multiple commenters dismiss it as 「CEO said a thing」 journalism, note the obvious incentive to sell Spyre cards and consulting, and trade stories of overpriced IBM products that never shipped; a minority defends the mainframe/Telum II hardware and IBM's free Granite models. There is rough agreement that on-prem inference needs ongoing tuning and possibly dedicated inference engineers to be worth it.

**Worth reading**
- [jacquesm](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50238816): Concrete numbers from an 8-GPU owner: ~$25K build, ~2,500W (throttled to 1,500W), 100–150 tok/s, and an explicit admission that local is not cheaper than a frontier lab — independence is what he's paying for.
- [bitexploder](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50239074): Counter-data point that undercuts the $20K+ framing: 4×V100S at ~$750 each plus a 48GB MacBook runs Qwen at ~200 tok/s single-user and is called about as reliable as Sonnet for agentic coding.
- [collinmcnulty](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50238658): The strongest skeptic line: a CEO talking his book is no more evidence than a frontier lab saying the opposite, however plausible the claim.

## 9. Microsoft Decision-1: 9B Qwen Model Launch Explained {#story-9}
*#12 on HN · [shattered.io](https://shattered.io/microsoft-decision-1-qwen3-5-9b-model-2026/) · 36 points · 15 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50238620)*

The post describes Microsoft-Decision-1, announced October 9, 2026 and built by post-training Alibaba's open-weight Qwen3.5-9B, as a model that does not generate text at all: it takes supplied content plus a fixed list of answer options and returns a calibrated probability for each, aimed at routing, classification, prioritization, verification and agent workflow control. It cites Microsoft's claims of 83.5% accuracy across 36 benchmarks and roughly 150,000 held-out questions, about 35x lower P50 latency than GPT-6 Sol, 4.5x faster than Quyet-1.0-Large, 98.7% perturbation stability, and $0.042 per million input tokens with free output (OpenRouter pricing), available hosted-only via Microsoft Foundry and OpenRouter with a 32k-token input limit and no image or audio support. The piece itself concedes none of the benchmark or latency figures have been independently reproduced, and it is truncated before the section on internal trials.

**Discussion**: The top comment calls the piece an advertisement for Microsoft because it never mentions Jev or any other decision-scoring model, and the rest of the thread largely fills that gap: one commenter rates pplx-decider-v1.2 and its -flash variant well on MonitoringBench, another says respan/span-02 came closest to Jev in informal OpenRouter testing while being open source and cheaper, and a third recommends baselabs' mammoth-1/elephant-1, currently free on OpenRouter and working well in a small internal app. One commenter links a phishing-detection comparison of decision models on OpenRouter. Notably, nobody reports having evaluated Microsoft-Decision-1 itself, so the thread is about the alternatives rather than the launch. The other half of the thread is a pelican-on-a-bicycle tangent: some dismiss that meme test as useless for decision systems while others explain it began with Simon Willison as a deliberately ungameable sanity check, and complain that models are now being trained to pass it.

**Worth reading**
