## 1. Outages reported at Bank of America, Chase, U.S. Bank, Wells Fargo and Zelle {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [apnews.com](https://apnews.com/article/bank-outage-chase-wells-fargo-truist-50fee2693c22f597fa6ca51a97ef2cda) · 265 points · 158 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521045)*

An AP News report that customers of several major U.S. banks — Bank of America, Chase, U.S. Bank, Wells Fargo — plus the Zelle payments network reported outages on the same day. The article itself could not be fetched (HTTP 403), so its contents are known only second-hand: commenters note it cites DownDetector data and quotes a Chase representative confirming an outage, with no equivalent confirmation from the other banks. It lands days after a large Microsoft 365 outage and an FAA ground stop, which is the frame most of the thread argues about.

*Article not read: HTTP 403*

**Discussion**: The thread splits on whether this is a pattern or a coincidence. One camp points to three major outages in three business days and argues the shared root cause is systemic: over-centralisation on a handful of clouds, complexity optimised for speed and cost rather than robustness, and outsourcing of basic operational competence. The other camp calls that an omnicause, noting no common cause is known for any of the three, that AI, layoffs and Iranian hackers are all speculation, and that rare events look frequent precisely because they make the news — while pointing out that bank and FAA uptime is far above 99%. **Worth reading**

## 2. Grazing sheep in fields with solar arrays {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [news.cornell.edu](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/10/sheep-grazing-solar-fields-offers-farmers-economic-ecological-gains) · 117 points · 101 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50520182)*

A Cornell news piece covering two new papers. One, 'Low solar-grazing densities support beneficial insects in agrivoltaic systems' (Environmental Research Letters, Oct. 7, first author Leana Zoller, senior author Steve Grodsky), ran a three-year trial at the Cascadilla Community Solar Farm in Dryden, NY: at two sheep per hectare, wild bees, honey bees and lady beetles were 38% more abundant than on a mowed control plot, an effect driven mostly by an 83% rise in honey bees; at four sheep per hectare insect numbers matched mowing while vegetation control improved; at eight sheep per hectare vegetation control peaked and flock health stayed high, but honey bee abundance fell 45%. The second, 'Flock Management for Solar Grazing' (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, Sept. 28), is a spreadsheet-based model from Cornell business and engineering researchers (Vincent Slaugh, Linwei Xin, Shane Henderson) that helps farmers size flocks against contract revenue and site vegetation, already used by Ithaca's United Agrivoltaics in onboarding.

**Discussion**: The thread mostly wandered away from the papers: the largest cluster debates why Permian Basin operators flare stranded gas instead of selling it (pipeline capacity, Waha Hub prices near zero, Oklahoma's anti-waste rules) and whether crypto miners or data centers can absorb it. A second big branch argues over why solar goes on grassland instead of desert — the answers given are transmission cost and permitting delays (the SunZia line took 20 years from planning to operation), plus the point that 'barren' land is often land degraded by grazing. On the actual topic, practitioners converge on sheep being the least destructive option: goats climb onto panels and chew cables, and cattle — 1,000–1,500 lb animals — rub against and break racking, which is exactly the blocker one cattle owner describes hitting. Several commenters push back on the study's framing, noting the comparison is against mowing and that mowing around hundreds of support struts is itself a costly nuisance, so the bar is low. A sheep-specific correction from one commenter: sheep shear grass off at the crown down to bare soil, so reseeding with clover or native flowering plants may be needed — which would actually help the biodiversity claim.

**Worth reading**
- [_heimdall](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521557): A cattle owner with ~20 head says he wants shade for his field but is blocked by the lack of affordable racking that survives 1,000 lb steers rubbing on it, and by a local utility that makes back-feeding the grid pointless.
- [fred69](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521875): Notes that sheep bite grass down to the crown at soil level rather than cropping it higher, so a clover/native-flower mix may need periodic reseeding — good for biodiversity, but a real cost.
- [scblock](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521882): Industry detail correcting a sibling comment: utility-scale arrays are designed for 1500 VDC (strings of 20-25 modules, not 12-18), and cables often run unprotected straight into the combiner box and underground, with above-ground hangers rare.

## 3. Jiff 1.0: A datetime library for Rust {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [burntsushi.net](https://burntsushi.net/jiff/) · 185 points · 43 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50519744)*

Andrew Gallant (burntsushi) announces Jiff 1.0, a Rust datetime library built around making DST and time zone handling the center of the API rather than an afterthought, with locale-sensitive formatting via ICU4X. The post walks through the type onion — civil::Date, civil::Time, civil::DateTime, Timestamp, TimeZone, ZonedDateTime, plus Span and SignedDuration — and shows how ambiguous civil times in DST folds and gaps are resolved with 'compatible' (default), 'earlier', 'later' and error-on-ambiguity strategies. Gallant notes existing production use in uv, Cargo, Starship, fd, Nushell, mise, Jujutsu, uutils/coreutils and an AWS LLRT Temporal implementation, and commits to API stability at 1.0 indefinitely because Jiff is meant to appear in other libraries' public APIs. Jiff deliberately supports only the Unix time scale (no TAI or leap seconds).

**Discussion**: The thread is largely a tribute to burntsushi's library design and temperament, with several people noting his other projects and one contributor describing him as easy to work with. The most substantive testimony comes from chrono's former maintainer, who says he took the job hoping to fix its API, hit resistance to every improvement, burned out, and now calls chrono stable but semi-deprecated — others link the chrono issue confirming that status and say the switch to Jiff was easy. A scientist simulating asteroid orbits asks about non-Unix time scales like UT1; burntsushi points him to hifitime, and the scientist replies that hifitime's integer representation adds numerical noise to his long-baseline simulations. A long side debate argues whether a language should ship datetime handling in its standard library: one camp cites supply-chain safety and sane ecosystem integration and points to java.time, the other argues a deficient built-in API crowds out better libraries and teaches bad habits, with Go's time package offered as the cautionary tale. A smaller thread corrects the record that the Temporal model is recent and descends from Joda/java.time, and the remainder is jokes about pronouncing Jiff like 'gif' with a soft g.

**Worth reading**
- [quodlibetor](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521423): chrono's maintainer of roughly five years explains he tried and failed to fix chrono's API, burned out on the resistance, and now calls chrono stable but semi-deprecated while praising Jiff.
- [burntsushi](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50520811): burntsushi states plainly that Jiff only supports the Unix time scale and recommends hifitime for TAI/UT1-style astronomical work — with the requester replying in C10 that hifitime's internal representation hurts his long-term orbital simulations.
- [masklinn](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50520954): Corrects the idea that Temporal invented the good model: it follows Joda and java.time, and links Colebourne's objections to Temporal making non-ISO calendars first-class.

## 4. Software is breaking down at a systemic level {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [breck-mckye.com](https://www.breck-mckye.com/blog/2026/11/software-is-breaking-down-at-a-systemic-level/) · 132 points · 95 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50520866)*

A blog post by Breck McKye arguing that 2026's run of reliability and security disasters — a two-day global Microsoft outage, an FAA ground stop, the PHP2Shell remote code execution bug in a runtime powering roughly 70% of websites, and thousands of SSL certificates compromised by a weak private key at eMudhra — have different immediate causes but a common shape: highly complex systems assembled from low-quality parts that nobody understands end to end. He traces this to cloud consolidation, dependency-heavy open source since the mid-2010s, and the loss of specialist knowledge, then argues the real AI danger is not bad model output but 'decoherence' — citing Kristen Maevyn's essay 'The Decoherence Trap' on locally valid but mutually incompatible models accumulating until change becomes unaffordable — plus ex-Microsoft engineer Axel Rischin's critique of Azure's quick-fix reliability culture. (The fetched article was truncated partway through the decoherence section.)

**Discussion**: The dominant reaction is that the essay's history is wrong and its mood is nostalgic: commenters point out AWS is ~20 years old, Maven Central 25, CPAN 31, left-pad a decade past, and rattle off log4j, Heartbleed, CrowdStrike and Okta as proof that software was never in a golden age — failure counts are up mainly because there is vastly more software and detection is now nearly free. Several still endorse the core claim that complexity and centralisation have made failures correlated and industry-wide, and a large subthread argues the fix is documentation: write down the rules, specs and tests so agents have a harness to follow. A rival camp calls that naive, saying the hard problem is evaluating output when you can no longer hold the system in your head and AI doesn't learn from its mistakes. First-hand reports split both ways — faster shipping and fewer per-task failures on one hand, rubber-stamped reviews of 500-line diffs and more incidents per month on the other — with rough agreement only that AI compresses the timescale rather than inventing a new failure mode.

**Worth reading**
- [tflinton](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521220): A practitioner automating infrastructure upgrades reports AI agents fail half as often as humans (1/20 vs 1/10) yet produce 5x more incidents per month because they run at 10x the speed — safer per action, less reliable per unit of human time.
- [dotdi](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521329): Describes 70 people laid off leaving 20 to absorb the work, 30-second LGTM reviews on 500-line diffs, and a DevOps lead about to disable the JVM JIT everywhere on Opus 5.5's advice when the real fix was a one-line library change.
- [titzer](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521192): Counter-example from the maintainer of the Virgil compiler: Claude produced a working arm64 backend in about 3 days and a complete RISC-V backend in 3 hours, both passing the full test suite — evidence AI can harden core infrastructure, not just generate slop.

## 6. The Secret ThinkPad Powerbutton Code to Bring Dead Laptops Back to Life (2007) {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [masnick.com](https://masnick.com/posts/the-secret-thinkpad-powerbutton-code-to-bring-dead-laptops-back-to-life/) · 257 points · 102 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50516473)*

A 2008 blog post by Mike Masnick recounting how his new ThinkPad z61t went completely dead (no LEDs, hot after sleeping) an hour before a talk at the Office 2.0 conference, and how IBM support talked him through a revival ritual: unplug the AC adapter, remove the battery, press the power button 10 times at one-second intervals, then hold it for 30 seconds, then reinsert the battery and power on. The machine came back, and support attributed it vaguely to 'static discharge.' The post then collects years of reader comments reporting the same trick reviving T40/T41/T42/T43/T60/T61/X41/X200/R61 and even non-ThinkPad machines, plus a few failures.

**Discussion**: The thread's dominant correction is that 'static discharge' is the wrong term: what the procedure actually does is drain residual charge from capacitors and reset the embedded controller (EC), which boots before the BIOS and can latch into a bad state. Several commenters argue the number and timing of the presses is theater — a single long hold plus a delay before reconnecting power is what matters — and former call-center and PC-shop staff supply the same pattern from other domains (telling customers to hold a router reset for 10 rather than 3 seconds, to 'reverse the plug', 'reseat the power cable', or 'blow the dust out of the RJ11 ports'), because direct questions like 'is it plugged in?' get indignant denials rather than checks. Counter-evidence is well represented: people whose laptops stayed dead because of a clogged fan and cooked motherboard, a T60p fixed instead by reseating RAM, and one machine cured by removing the CD drive, so the consensus is 'worth trying, not magic.' Useful side threads note that newer ThinkPads (X280, T15p) replace the ritual with a pinhole battery-disconnect/EC reset, that the same symptom affects Dells, MSIs, Asus and Legion laptops, and that the underlying failure is often a Windows machine waking in a bag and overheating.

**Worth reading**
- [legacynl](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50517185): A former call-center operator explains the 10-push instruction as an anti-underholding trick: customers told to hold a router reset for 3 seconds often hold 1-2, and bumping the script to 10 seconds made most 'broken' routers work.
- [YeGoblynQueenne](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50519128): First-hand counterexample: the reset worked once on a T15p with a paperclip-in-the-hole reset, then the laptop died permanently when a clogged fan cooked the board — proof the trick is not general magic.
- [dlgeek](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50518069): Explains what the pinhole on modern ThinkPads actually is: a battery disconnect that fully cuts power to the embedded controller, held so its capacitors discharge.

## 7. Aristotle Expands into Rust {#story-7}
*#7 on HN · [harmonic.fun](https://www.harmonic.fun/news/aristotle-rust-verification/) · 50 points · 26 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50520270)*

Harmonic announced that Aristotle, its AI-driven formal verification system previously aimed at C and Verilog, now verifies Rust — claiming it can prove absence of undefined behavior even inside unsafe blocks, and prove that Rust rewritten from C behaves identically. The post cites two Solana case studies: on Manifest, an open-source order book, it proved 28 results including that a valid swap goes through, and found a rounding bug the team has since fixed; on Project 0, a prime broker, it proved the books stay consistent across deposits, withdrawals, borrows, repayments and liquidations in any order. Harmonic is partnering with the Solana Foundation and selling Aristotle self-serve on consumption-based pricing (the company previously raised a $120M Series C led by Ribbit Capital at a $1.45B valuation).

**Discussion**: The thread splits in two. The first cluster is technical curiosity: how specs get written, whether a human or an LLM produces them from natural-language intent, whether there is an open-source equivalent, and general fatigue at the number of Rust verification tools appearing. One commenter is struck by the claim of proving no undefined behavior inside unsafe blocks, saying they have not seen another system attempt it, and is answered with a list of adjacent projects — cargo-anneal, VeriFast, RefinedRust, safety-tags and Verus — which use very different mechanisms and are still an active research area. The larger argument is a Rust-vs-garbage-collection tangent: one side says Rust is a poor fit for many tasks, that the borrow checker forces endless refactors and that GC only gets more attractive as hardware improves; the other says LLM-written code shifts that friction away from individual developers, that Moore's Law-style gains are ending so GC's headroom is not guaranteed, and that Rust's type system buys correctness that matters more than raw throughput for most software. Nobody in the thread reports hands-on use of Aristotle itself, so the discussion stays at the level of the claims rather than validating them.

**Worth reading**
- [bryanlarsen](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521076): Lists five existing Rust verification tools (cargo-anneal, VeriFast, RefinedRust, safety-tags, Verus) that work by very different mechanisms, framing the space as still-active research.
- [bryanlarsen](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521166): Points readers who actually write unsafe Rust at Google's public unsafe-code review skill document rather than a verification tool.
- [darksaints](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50522122): A practitioner who has built high-throughput systems on Java argues most GCs are mediocre, most software is nowhere near the performance frontier, and correctness is what matters — so Rust's type system wins.

## 9. Jolt – Clojure that speaks C {#story-9}
*#10 on HN · [jolt-lang.net](https://jolt-lang.net/) · 42 points · 11 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50519345)*

Jolt is a Clojure dialect that targets Scheme instead of the JVM: the compiler reads Clojure, lowers it to a host-neutral IR, emits Scheme, and runs it natively on Chez Scheme (or on Gambit compiled to JavaScript), and it compiles itself. It aims at near-superset Clojure semantics C-style persistent collections, numeric tower, transducers, multimethods, protocols, core.async, agents/pmap on OS threads — while swapping Java interop for a C FFI (ffi/load-library, ffi/defcfn, shown binding sqlite3_libversion on libsqlite3), and `jolt build` AOT-compiles app plus deps and statically linked C libraries into one self-contained binary. Stated divergences are no general Java interop/reflection/gen-class, no BigDecimal, and regex via irregex rather than java.util.regex; install is via brew or a curl script, with deps.edn resolution and an nREPL server for CIDER/Calva/Cursive.

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly a fight about how the project was built rather than about the language. The first comparison raised was jank (Clojure on C/C++); Jeaye, jank's author, corrected the claim that jank isn't a full Clojure dialect, and then dropped the thread's bombshell: jolt is 'very much AI coded' and doesn't disclose this on its site or README, which he says matters to a lot of people. skydhash argued the AI-written README, unreadable Makefile and the project's AI policy (that no one can hold a large codebase in their head, so new code is 'always done with limited understanding') are red flags, and that specs, modules and tests are how engineers handle that. Author yogthos defended the policy as basic reality, pointed to jolt's large specification and conformance test suite as his answer to cognitive complexity, and was accused of a personal attack for telling the critic he seemed new to the profession; a self-described 27-year veteran agreed with the critic. Others noted jolt's upside — FFI access to the native ecosystem, TCO, fibers, and running existing Clojure libraries unchanged — and one commenter simply tallied the other projects already named Jolt.

**Worth reading**
- [Jeaye](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50522232): Jank's author Jeaye corrects the mischaracterization of jank as not a full Clojure dialect, frames the real difference as C++/native dependency management, and says jolt is heavily AI-coded without disclosing it.
- [skydhash](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521183): A detailed critique: the README is LLM-generated and the Makefile unreadable, and the project's AI policy that code is 'always added with limited understanding' is an excuse — specs, modular boundaries and tests are how engineering actually copes.
- [yogthos](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521602): Author yogthos answers that the policy is not an excuse, links jolt's conformance harness as his mitigation, and argues nobody can know every edge-case interaction in a large codebase — though the reply turns personal.
