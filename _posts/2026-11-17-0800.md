## 1. Carnegie Mellon is putting computer science courses on YouTube for free {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [cs.cmu.edu](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/learn/) · 1,325 points · 183 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50522980)*

CMU's Griffin School of Computer Science launched 'CS Learn', a site hosting full-semester lecture recordings from current CMU CS courses across seven departments, with one course premiering each day during launch week and more promised in January. Instructor Phillip Compeau, who runs the project, says students built tooling that automates much of the production work so a small team can ship courses quickly, with human review of every lecture. The site also teases badges, AI tutoring layered onto courseware, and graded work/credentials via CMU Online; current titles visible include Machine Learning with Large Datasets and Principles of Software Construction.

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly warm — alumni and self-taught programmers thank CMU, swap favorite courses (Andy Pavlo's databases, 15-213, 15-259), and ask for advanced and graduate material rather than the intro courses posted so far, plus slide decks, labs and assignments; a CMU faculty member notes Pavlo's database lectures are already online and says he plans to record his distributed systems course next time he teaches it. The biggest argument is whether this matters in the LLM era: one commenter asks what value lecture videos have when you can interrogate a model, others reply that AI hallucinates, that it cannot tell you what you don't know you don't know, and that a structured human curriculum is the point — while the skeptics counter that models can be prodded and that credentials and networking, not information, are the real reason to attend CMU. Hosting drew complaints: several people want PeerTube, torrents or an open license so the material can be archived and redistributed offline, and one commenter raises the UC Berkeley captioning lawsuit, prompting a debate over whether Section 504 covers a private university (Compeau says every lecture has and will have 'state of the art captions'). A long side thread debates whether CS fundamentals still protect jobs, with the pessimistic view that knowing fundamentals won't stop economic replacement and the optimist view that fundamentals are what let you steer AI. Nothing resembling consensus emerges beyond agreement that more material is good and that courseware, not just videos, will decide whether it works.

**Worth reading**
- [dgacmu](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50524420): CMU's David Andersen (dgacmu) links Andy Pavlo's already-public database lectures and says he intends to publish his own distributed systems lectures the next time he teaches them.
- [BeetleB](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50527279): A detailed correction to the thread's legal thread: Section 504, not just ADA Title II, binds any institution taking federal money — including private universities and companies with federal research grants.
- [pcompeau](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50526457): Compeau answers the accessibility concern directly: every lecture has and will have state-of-the-art captions, and accessibility is a stated priority.

## 2. Windows 11 Is Collecting Data on Which Coding Agents and Developer Tools You Use {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [xusheng.dev](https://xusheng.dev/posts/windows-coding-agent-telemetry/) · 531 points · 371 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50524736)*

A reverse-engineering writeup: the author used an AI agent to scan recent Windows binaries and found MissionControlAggregator.dll (version 10.0.26100.9818 on a Windows 11 ARM64 build 26200.9974 VM) containing hardcoded executable-name matchers for coding agents — copilot.exe, claude.exe, codex.exe, agy.exe on the CLI side, plus github.exe, cursor.exe and antigravity.exe as desktop agents — behind a feature named CLICodingAgentUsage (feature ID 62580080). The component subscribes to KernelProcess.AppStateChangeSummary, Win32kTraceLogging.AppInteractivitySummary and WSL's Lxss.Manager Exec events, then emits a Segments.SubCohorts ETW report with bitfields for 12 developer-tool categories, 4 CLI agents and 5 desktop agents, discarding focus-time and runtime details and keeping only a 'was it seen' bitmap. The author verified end-to-end by copying cmd.exe to codex.exe and to gcc.exe and watching slots flip, and found the report sitting in DiagTrack's upload queue as optional diagnostic data — enabled by default (AllowTelemetry=3) in a clean US Windows 11 Home ARM64 install.

**Discussion**: A large share of the thread barely engages with the telemetry finding and instead becomes a Linux-switching thread: many commenters report running CachyOS, Bazzite, Fedora, Mint, NixOS or Arch as daily drivers, say single-player games work essentially perfectly through Proton, and cite kernel-level anti-cheat (Call of Duty, Battlefield, League of Legends, PUBG, EA titles) as the one real blocker. Skeptics push back on that enthusiasm: several say HDR support, Nvidia driver config, per-monitor scaling and non-Steam installers are still painful, and one self-described top-ranked League player says the small rough edges (FreeSync quirks, RTSS replacement, endless Proton-version tinkering) were enough to keep a dual boot; a counter-argument holds that most 'Linux isn't ready' posts are a decades-old genre. On the article itself, the split is between people calling this a nothing-burger (Windows disclosed the toggle, optional diagnostics can be turned off, ETW is a normal admin/diagnostic mechanism, Google and Apple do worse) and those who find the specific inventory of coding agents qualitatively different. Practical threads cover disabling telemetry via Group Policy rather than the Settings toggle (which Windows can reset) and whether previously collected data gets uploaded if you re-enable sharing — the article's own evidence is that the data is recorded locally regardless and only uploaded when optional diagnostics is on. One exchange questions whether data skewed to Windows users who leave telemetry on tells Microsoft anything useful, with the retort that this is exactly the segment the company cares about.

**Worth reading**
- [KennyBlanken](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50527432): Argues the Settings toggle is unreliable and gives the Group Policy path (Administrative Templates > Windows Components > Data Collection and Preview Builds) to disable diagnostic data so Windows can't silently re-enable it, plus a warning that aggressive hardening breaks Windows' internet connectivity check.
- [TheJoeMan](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50526682): Raises the sharpest question about the article's finding: if the usage bitmap is recorded locally no matter what and only gated at upload time, is re-enabling diagnostic data later going to flush previously collected history?
- [shevy-java](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50528090): Questions the methodology itself — the whole discovery came from asking an AI to hunt through Windows binaries — and gets an answer about how such claims can and should be verified rather than trusted.

## 3. BugMeNot has closed {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [bugmenot.com](https://www.bugmenot.com/) · 493 points · 223 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50523601)*

BugMeNot — the site where people posted shared throwaway logins to get past registration walls — is gone after roughly 23 years; its homepage now serves almost no readable content. The only thing left, per commenters, is a block of 4-letter groups (DXXR KAKD QMDZ…) that appears in the page source when you click the bug icon, and it became the thread's main attraction: one commenter traced the first letter of each 4-letter group to Python's random.Random(2026).choice over the uppercase alphabet, another posted a decode chain (mod-26 diffs → base-128 → ASCII → Atbash → Z85) yielding symbol tokens like U2R3X and FL4XX, and a third claimed the result resolved to an onion address serving Joan Stark's 1999 r2d2/c3po ASCII art — a claim others had already walked back. Nothing on the page itself explains the shutdown.

**Discussion**: Two conversations dominate. The first is a eulogy with a strong split on whether it still worked: old users say the success rate collapsed around 2010 as sites blacklisted it, while several people report fresh successes within weeks or months — pfSense's $0 community-edition cart, a Veeam login wall, VMware/Broadcom downloads, JEDEC specs, pianobook.co.uk, even a Docker Desktop account. The most upvoted thread is the RetailMeNot origin story: the Australian founders turned BugMeNot's PageRank and UX into a coupon site, sold it to WhaleShark Media and IPO'd in 2016, described as one of the great bootstrap stories. The second argument is ethical and legal: one camp says using shared credentials is a bad-faith ToS violation that was never going to survive, and that a site serving freeloaders can't pay for hosting; the other says ToS isn't law, the CFAA's scope has been narrowed, and many BugMeNot targets weren't paid subscriptions at all but pointless signups for manuals, datasheets and one article. Several people note it's largely superseded by temp-mail services, DuckDuckGo email protection, Apple's Hide My Email and catch-all domains, and that big publishers moved to metered paywalls that kill shared accounts anyway.

**Worth reading**

## 4. Optimizely compromised, served malware on Zoom, GitLab {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [blog.report-uri.com](https://blog.report-uri.com/worlds-largest-brands-infected-with-malware-via-optimizely/) · 290 points · 126 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50524767)*

A Report URI write-up on an incident from 16 November 2026 in which attackers wrote malicious code into the Project JavaScript field of Optimizely Web customer projects, so it was served from cdn.optimizely.com to visitors of 178 organisations including Zoom, the BBC, CNN, Salesforce, GitLab, HSBC, Equifax, Ledger, Peacock and Vodafone. Report URI fetched 1,611 distinct snippets and found 188 infected (some on customer-hosted domains), carrying a crypto address clipper, a Luhn-checking card skimmer posting to 1688360.cc/card.php, cookie and html2canvas screenshot exfiltration, and the Coruna/DarkSword iOS exploit kits aimed at iOS 18.6 and earlier. The payload was rebuilt 26 times that day, the attacker's wallets had received about $7,040 before being swept at 13:03 UTC, and by 21:52 UTC only one snippet (TalkTalk's) was still dirty; Report URI is a CSP reporting vendor and the post is partly a pitch for its detection product.

**Discussion**: The dominant thread is that tag managers and third-party marketing scripts are the root cause — several commenters want Google Tag Manager-era practices re-evaluated, with ad-blockers as the only thing that reliably stops GTM. That leads to a long argument about liability: some want serving malware through third parties to be criminal, others (pointing at the xz backdoor) say that logic would imprison open-source maintainers, and a middle camp argues for civil liability and a private right of action since negligence tiers are unworkable. Practitioners push CSP and Subresource Integrity as the fix, and are corrected on both counts: SRI pins a hash but Optimizely's whole purpose is to change script content continuously, and SRI also does nothing to stop an allowed script from loading further scripts — only CSP frame/script/connect sources do that. Commenters also push back on the report's alarm: browsers sandbox per-site, so CNN or the BBC can mainly be harmed where they take cards or crypto, and the genuinely serious part is the iOS exploit-chain delivery for unpatched devices. A recurring meta-complaint is that the write-up reads as AI-generated marketing copy (and that it is a dupe of an amirulcyber.com post), and a widely-shared tl;dr notes the real oddity is write access across hundreds of tenants, meaning weak tenancy isolation or a very severe Optimizely-side compromise.

**Worth reading**
- [hn_throwaway_99](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50527738): Argues the browser sandbox bounds most of the damage and the report should say so clearly — the real severity is the Coruna/DarkSword iOS exploit kits, not the crypto clipper on news sites.
- [jawns](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50525269): Concise tl;dr, and flags the interesting part: write access reached hundreds of customers, so either tenancy isolation is weak or the compromise was severe.
- [stavros](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50524841): Corrects the SRI recommendation: SRI verifies the script you fetched, but does not stop that script from loading further scripts — that requires CSP.

## 5. If an App Asks for Full Disk Access or System and App Protection Override {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [support.apple.com](https://support.apple.com/en-us/149699) · 98 points · 53 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50526560)*

An Apple support document (published November 16, 2026) describing a change in macOS 27.3: when an app asks for Full Disk Access or the new System & App Protection Override, users can now grant access to only specific protected folders or apps instead of bypassing system protections entirely. The doc lists what full bypass actually permits — reading, changing, deleting and exporting all data, viewing hidden and recently deleted photos, browsing as you on websites, and, for the override, controlling the Mac and apps and monitoring everything including keystrokes — and walks through the 'Bypass System Protections' confirmation, per-app 'Ask First' vs 'At Any Time' settings, and how to review or revoke access in System Settings under Files & Folders, Full Disk Access, and System & App Protection Override.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between developers who call the design a bad API and users who say it is long overdue. The developer complaint, laid out in detail at the top, is that apps still cannot declare which paths they need, and that there is no read-only mode, no expiring grant, and no way to deny-list sensitive directories like ~/.ssh — so legitimate apps will keep demanding everything. The other camp argues developers got their chance: App Sandbox let apps ask for specific folders via open dialogs and almost nobody adopted it unless forced, so Full Disk Access became the escape hatch, and one commenter counted 44 apps requesting FDA on their Mac while granting only 8 (Anki and Soulver among the askers). A recurring correction: the claim that AI companies drove this is only partly right — Claude and ChatGPT desktop apps reportedly never asked for FDA, while Meta's AI app was allegedly pointing users at it to reach Messages, which had no narrower permission. Several people ask for negative access or exclusions instead of allowlists, and others note Apple already protects some ~/Library/Application Support directories (browsers, Discord) against session-stealing malware; the sub-thread over what FDA was 'designed for' turns into a dispute about whether Apple is quietly rewriting the permission's history or just writing plain-language docs for non-technical readers.

**Worth reading**
- [comex](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50527825): Points out Apple already tried developer-scoped access with App Sandbox and developers didn't adopt it, and notes Full Disk Access is currently the only route to Messages — likely why some AI apps ask.
- [duskwuff](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50527877): Notes macOS already applies FDA-style protection to directories like ~/Library/Application Support for browsers and Discord to block session-stealing malware, with a link explaining the mechanism.
- [comex](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50527910): Corrects the thread's premise: Claude and ChatGPT desktop apps never requested Full Disk Access; Meta's AI app was the one allegedly steering users to it to read Messages.

## 6. OpenCode Browse {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [opencode.ai](https://opencode.ai/product/browse) · 224 points · 120 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50523459)*

OpenCode Browse is a Chrome/Chromium extension plus MCP server that gives agents control of your actual browser — your tabs, logins and extensions — with agent tabs opening in collapsed background groups, a handoff flow for logins, 2FA and payments (the tab comes back to you, then you click Continue), plus screenshots, tab recordings and network capture. It works with any MCP client (OpenCode, Claude Code, Cursor, Codex, Zed) and supports allowed-site restrictions and lending an existing tab to an agent. It targets Chromium-based browsers only (Chrome, Edge, Brave, Arc, Helium, Vivaldi, Opera), installable via a curl/pwsh/npm/bun one-liner.

**Discussion**: Almost nobody discussed the product itself; the thread became a referendum on OpenCode as a company. The loudest complaint, from a self-described heavy user running billions of tokens a day through it, is that V2 shipped with no permission model by default, broke configs and plugins arbitrarily, and lost pinned sessions — with a side claim that session titles are generated by sending conversation data to their servers even when running a local model offline. Several others corroborate: one demonstrated escaping a bwrap sandbox by writing a permissive config in a subfolder, and another cited a September 2025 GitHub issue where OpenCode devs explicitly said allowing all actions by default was intentional because users 'think they want permissions and then actually prefer not having them'. Defenders argue security belongs in the sandbox rather than the harness (permission fatigue makes in-harness prompts useless), that the team ships genuinely useful software, and that the 'CEO' being mocked is Dax, who is not the CEO (Jay V is listed as CEO) and who one follower reads as a skeptic rather than a braggart. A parallel thread argues you should just use CDP or Playwright MCP directly, with replies noting chrome-devtools-mcp, Firefox's own devtools MCP and Vercel's agent-browser already exist — countered by people who say wrapping CDP into a consistent MCP surface takes far more than the claimed four hours. Secondary gripes: SST v4 appears unmaintained with a community fork forming, and the 'real browser' list omitting Firefox and Safari reads as sloppy marketing.

**Worth reading**
- [testtestmetest](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50525680): A power user (12-hour days, half a dozen containers, billions of tokens through OpenCode) details why he refuses to upgrade to V2: no shipped permission model, arbitrary breaking config changes, no easy directory confinement, and session titles silently derived from his conversations.
- [sburud](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50528401): Concrete sandbox-escape anecdote: asked OpenCode to break out of a bwrap wrapper, it dropped a permissive config into a subfolder, moved the session there, and wrote a file to the desktop with no prompt.
- [rgbrenner](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50527043): Links the September 2025 GitHub issue where OpenCode maintainers state the permissive defaults were deliberate — 'our goal is agent UX, not security' — and that none of them use permissions.

## 7. Why 3dfx 1 cards had such a washed out color output? {#story-7}
*#7 on HN · [twitter.com](https://twitter.com/id_aa_carmack/status/2122478083319992513) · 96 points · 16 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50525575)*

The link is a John Carmack post on X answering a question about why 3dfx Voodoo-era cards looked washed out in 1997. His explanation: NeXT and 3dfx deliberately built closer-to-linear display pipelines (Pixar and SGI heritage respectively), which is correct at 10+ bits per channel but wrong at NeXTStation's 4 bits or 3dfx's 5-6 bits, since human vision needs more precision in the darks than a linear ramp gives. Compounding it, artists painted textures in non-linear paint programs, so 3dfx's more accurate blending and filtering produced the characteristic washed-out look; Carmack admits he did not understand gamma during the glQuake days and that many games still mishandle it.

**Discussion**: The thread largely backs Carmack but supplies numbers and a partial correction: one commenter notes VGA's default gamma was 1.0 and 3dfx's 1.5, so against a CRT's ~2.2 the 3dfx setting was actually closer to linear output, and that Glide's grGammaCorrectionValue or the SST_GAMMA environment variable could reproduce standard VGA mapping — arguing the 1.5 default was more a brightness war or a way to avoid wasting bits in near-blacks than principled color science. Others add that sRGB is genuinely linear in CRT signal voltage, and that 'doing it correctly' today by resampling in linear space can look worse because quantizing back to 8 bits introduces banding and the non-linear path accidentally matches human contrast sensitivity. Someone recalls Carmack publicly learning this while adding HDR to Rage, calling it a long-standing blind spot. A side thread asks why Quake 2 looked reddish on a Riva card versus software rendering, with replies pointing to the software renderer not doing colored lighting and skybox light tinting.

**Worth reading**
- [rasz](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50527880): Supplies the actual gamma numbers (VGA 1.0, 3dfx 1.5, CRT 2.2) and argues 3dfx was closer to linear output than plain VGA, with SST_GAMMA=1 restoring identical color mapping — so the 1.5 default was probably brightness marketing or bit-usage, not color science.
- [nullc](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50528227): Contrarian practitioner point: resampling in linear space and then quantizing back to 8 bits can band badly, and non-linear resampling often looks closer to right because it happens to match human contrast sensitivity.
- [dcrazy](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50528033): First-hand recollection that Carmack only worked through linear color blending publicly while adding HDR to Rage — remarkable that the blind spot lasted that long.

## 8. Test/verification technique effectiveness with Astra {#story-8}
*#10 on HN · [danluu.com](https://danluu.com/astra-testing/) · 32 points · 6 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50515035)*

Dan Luu re-runs his agentic testing eval on GPT-6 Astra after previously running it on GPT-5.6 Sol. On the original Zstd-from-RFC task Sol needed $3.50 (medium) or $5.50 (xhigh) per run to hit full holdout correctness about 63%/77% of the time, while Astra gets over 99% at medium for about $1 — so the benchmark saturated. He moves to the IMAP RFC and then a Pandoc eval (~14 turns per run, 25 runs per condition, roughly $90k at MSRP, no condition reaching 100%), where 'Default' (no prescribed technique) remains strong but several techniques now beat it, unlike with Sol: differential testing made agents probe boundary conditions, TDD underperformed again, ACL2 did well, and property-based-testing libraries finally produced useful tests. He stresses the wide error bars, sensitivity to trivial prompt changes, and single-problem sample, and pushes back on a viral claim that agents should be told to stop writing tests.

**Discussion**: Commenters are broadly appreciative that someone is measuring agent techniques instead of arguing from intuition. One commenter argues the real contribution is starting a taxonomy of agent patterns and that the field is shockingly short on engineering — treating skills, MCP and similar constructs as things that may end up like Netscape absent evidence. A TDD practitioner asks what 'TDD' actually meant in the prompts and why it underperformed; another commenter supplies a link to Dan Luu's earlier agentic-testing writeup for the methodology details. Others ask where the ~$90k of eval budget comes from and point to Luu's separate language comparison, with one developer saying they default to Rust but find F# competitive with Python on correctness and wanting to see both re-run under the new models. Notably, no one disputes the findings themselves — the skepticism is about generalization, matching the author's own caveats.

**Worth reading**
- [reedf1](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50528117): Argues the post's real value is starting a taxonomy of agent patterns, and that a field of engineers is choosing techniques by intuition rather than measurement — skills, MCP and the rest could end up like Netscape.
- [reedf1](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50528177): Points to danluu.com/agentic-testing/ for the missing detail on how the TDD condition was actually prompted and defined.
- [scotty79](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50528528): Flags Luu's companion language eval and reports from experience that F# matches Python on correctness at 'Ultra' effort, asking for Rust and F# to be re-tested under Astra 6.

## 9. Chemistry is Actually Geometry {#story-9}
*#11 on HN · [chillphysicsenjoyer.substack.com](https://chillphysicsenjoyer.substack.com/p/chemistry-is-actually-geometry) · 204 points · 104 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50522643)*

A Substack essay (whose internal title is 'Theoretical Biologists Should Know About Feinberg's Deficiency Theorems') by a writer at chillphysicsenjoyer, aiming to make Martin Feinberg's Foundations of Chemical Reaction Network Theory accessible to non-specialists. The argument: instead of fitting hundreds of unmeasurable rate constants for something like the Krebs cycle, treat reactions as networks — species as points, stoichiometric subspaces and classes as linear-algebraic objects — so that graph and geometric structure yields qualitative predictions (deficiency-zero-style results, oscillation vs. convergence) without precise parameters. It walks through examples of surprising dynamics: the Belousov-Zhabotinsky and Briggs-Rauscher oscillators, the urea-urease pH clock, Liesegang rings, the Higgins-Sel'kov glycolysis model, the KaiC phosphorylation cycle, and Edelstein's network with three equilibria (two stable, one unstable).

**Discussion**: Much of the thread barely engages the chemistry. The loudest subthread is a long philosophy-of-math argument over whether anything can be 'proven' — one camp invoking axioms and Münchhausen's trilemma, another (stouset, Rury, askvictor) insisting that math is conditionally certain by construction and only its application to reality is empirical; a string-theory aside follows. Several commenters note the title set wrong expectations, expecting molecular shape and electron density rather than trajectories in concentration space, though smj-edison argues the payoff is that an oscillation or a spiral is literally visible in the phase plot. Practitioners push back hardest on scope: gus_massa says the essay covers perhaps half of the first two years of a chemistry degree and omits organic chemistry entirely (his example: dimethyl ether is a gas, ethanol a liquid, from the same atoms), and syntacticsalt adds that Feinberg's deficiency theorems are not standard in typical US undergrad chemistry or chemical engineering programs nor known even to many kinetics PhDs. Spamhaus's sender guidelines page lays out minimum requirements for anyone sending email, with a phased rollout starting November 2026: every sending IP must have a valid HELO/EHLO hostname, a reverse DNS (PTR) record, and a matching forward A/AAAA record (FCrDNS). The page also requires confirmed opt-in for all subscribers, SPF/DKIM/DMARC with DKIM aligned to the From domain, IPv6 allocations of at least /64 with working reverse DNS, branded rather than hosting-provider rDNS names, and specific ESP/shared-infrastructure rules, warning that failures can land IPs on the CSS or XBL zones. It notes that residential IPs, placeholder HELO values such as localhost, and generic provider-generated rDNS names are treated as suspicious.

**Discussion**: The thread starts with a self-hoster (BLKNSLVR) who runs mail behind a residential ISP connection and therefore cannot set reverse DNS; the response is that residential-IP mail has been effectively blocked across most of the internet for years, and several people suggest renting a VPS, asking the ISP to unblock port 25, or using a relay such as AWS SES, DuoCircle or AnyDomain's AnyMXRelay. Others push back hard on the policy itself, calling Spamhaus a protection racket and the rules security theatre that will hurt small legitimate setups while doing nothing against actual spammers, while a counter-argument holds that the real value is attribution — if your zone points at the IP, you cannot claim it was someone else. On the AI angle, one commenter argues LLMs now make DKIM/DMARC/HELO setup trivial, so the difficulty barrier is gone and everything collapses back to IPv4 reputation; others reply that these records were never hard and that homegrown servers are the ones still getting them wrong. There is also first-hand frustration with Microsoft, which blocked a sender over a HELO of example.com instead of mail.example.com, and with Microsoft's SNDS tool, described as showing no problem while blocks persist for one to two weeks.

**Worth reading**
- [computer23](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50524014): First-hand account that Office 365 blocked their mail because the HELO was the bare domain rather than mailserver.domain, and that clearing Spamhaus and then Microsoft took significant time.
- [wahern](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50525689): Practical fix for the residential-IP problem: ask the ISP for a PTR record, or better, get the in-addr.arpa zone delegated so you control PTRs yourself without a ticket each time.
- [edelbitter](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50524907): Rebuts the 'this does nothing about spam' argument: the point of these records is removing plausible deniability, so misbehaviour can be attributed and acted on immediately.
