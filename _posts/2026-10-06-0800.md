## 1. Beam: Reflection's 501B open-weight model {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [reflection.ai](https://reflection.ai/blog/introducing-beam) · 406 points · 124 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49969183)*

Reflection AI's launch post for Beam, its first open-weight model: a sparse Mixture-of-Experts LLM with 501B total parameters and 23B active, pretrained on 23.8T tokens and RL-trained with 100M+ rollouts on 10.5K NVIDIA GB300 GPUs over four weeks, using ~1.3B sandboxes and up to 170K concurrent sandboxes. The post claims coding/agentic performance competitive with GLM 5.2 and approaching Qwen 3.8-Max while using 3–4× less inference compute than comparable reasoners. No weights yet: the model is in final red-teaming, and weights, technical report, model card and developer artifacts are promised 「later this month」 with signups for early access.

**Discussion**: The dominant reaction was 「talk is cheap, show me the weights」 — an announcement with no weights, no API and no technical details was widely called premature, and several commenters compared Beam unfavorably with DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM 5.3, arguing it is bigger, costlier to run and worse on nearly every benchmark. The strongest evidence came from a commenter who posted a side-by-side spec table (501B vs 552B total params, 23B vs 8–16B active, 28T vs 45T pretrain tokens, Apache 2.0 vs MIT, no vision, weights 「this month」 vs launch day). A competing camp defended the release as a legitimate Western entrant — a first model rarely beats incumbents, and a US-based lab is explicitly valuable for buyers whose contracts exclude Chinese models — while others countered that new entrants should at least win one meaningful dimension. Two side threads drew scrutiny: one correcting the article's claim that its 180×90 globe-map generalization demo is a few days old (commenters traced the puzzle to an August 2025 LessWrong post), and one on where training data comes from, with claims that the real moat is proprietary RL environments and contractor-built synthetic tasks, not scraped web text. Commenters also noted the benchmark charts omit GLM 5.3 and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, which appear only in the table, and that Reflection raised over $4B at a ~$25B valuation (backers reportedly include Nvidia, Eric Schmidt and Sequoia) with no prior shipped product.

## 3. Find the flattest route between any two points in SF {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [flattensf.com](https://flattensf.com/) · 167 points · 57 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49971230)*

flattensf.com is a browser-based route planner for San Francisco that computes flattest-path routes across roughly 160,000 street segments, using USGS 1 m lidar elevation data and the Overture/OpenStreetMap street graph. A slider runs the full trade-off between distance and cumulative climbing, from the shortest path to the flattest one worth walking (a foot of climb is priced at 200 ft of walking). Climbing means cumulative gain, not the net elevation change between endpoints; stairways are allowed on foot and excluded on bike, and place search runs offline against data embedded in the page.

**Discussion**: Commenters mostly praised the live elevation profile and the distance-vs-climb slider, but the thread turned into a bug hunt: several riders reported that at the slider's flattest setting the tool still routes them up very steep streets. A commenter riding a cargo e-bike gave a reproducible Mission Bernal-to-Diamond Heights trip where the tool picks a 22.7% grade to save about a foot of climbing; the author acknowledged the objective currently trades only distance against total climbing, and that steepness is reported but not optimized. Another rider's report about Cabrillo St in the Outer Richmond led the author to find a real parser bug: the Street's Slow Streets 「destination-only」 tag had been read as closed to pedestrians, hiding the street entirely, which the author fixed and deployed. The author also conceded the router doesn't yet know about bike lanes after being told it happily routes cyclists down Geary and Divisadero. A separate subthread accused the project of being LLM-generated slop, citing Claude Code listed as a contributor in the repo; a veteran routing-engine author pushed back that parser bugs like this are endemic to combining traffic rules, messy map data and user preferences, and several commenters named alternatives: bikehopper.org (maintained by an HN commenter, 1 m DTM over SF), Valhalla, and OpenTripPlanner's distance/time/slope triangle.

**Worth reading**
- [almstimplmntd](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49971926): The author diagnoses and fixes a concrete parser bug: Cabrillo St is a Slow Street whose 「destination-only」 tag was read as closed to pedestrians, so the router couldn't see the street at all.
- [laurencerowe](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49973119): A cargo e-bike rider gives a reproducible route where the tool picks a 22.7% grade over a 12% alternative to save roughly 1 ft of climbing, tying the complaint to the same issue seen in Google Maps.

## 4. Resurrecting iChat Audio and Video Conferencing {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [blog.pipetogrep.org](https://blog.pipetogrep.org/2026/09/11/resurrecting-ichat-audio-and-video-conferencing/) · 33 points · 6 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49973878)*

A blog post by Chris Jones describing how he and a friend got iChat AV audio/video calls working again on OS X Leopard and Snow Leopard by reimplementing Apple's dead SNATMAP server. The old setup broke because iChat fetches its public IP from configuration.apple.com's snatmap.txt, which now redirects to HTTPS that legacy OS X TLS can't negotiate, and Apple's snatmap.apple.com:5678 server no longer responds — so the two Macs fell back to exchanging private LAN IPs. Using packet captures, iChat's -errorLogLevel 7 debug mode and LLM help, he reverse-engineered the UDP/5678 protocol (16-byte request and response packets carrying a nonce plus the caller's external IP and port), wrote a Python server, and now runs a public one; users add 「157.230.2.213 configuration.apple.com」 to /etc/hosts. He tested on a Proxmox/KVM lab with two OpenWrt NAT VMs and a Logitech C920 webcam.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between nostalgia and a FaceTime usability argument: one commenter says modern FaceTime releases are 「avant-garde art experiments」 and that the recents/contacts redesign caused accidental calls, while another insists FaceTime is nearly too simple (preview, recent contacts, green New call button) and was optimized for 「calls from grandma」 — a third then points out that if a basic-skills user can't find the 16x16 missed-calls dropdown, that itself is confusing. On the technical side, a commenter situates SNATMAP as Apple's 2003-era equivalent of STUN (RFC 3489): learn your public IP and port from an outside server, swap them over chat, then both punch through NAT simultaneously — which is exactly why port-preserving NAT matters. That commenter warns that carrier-grade NAT (common on mobile and some ISPs), which assigns a new port per destination, will still break calls after the hosts fix, and notes iChat had no TURN relay fallback unlike modern WebRTC. The original author's work was enthusiastically received, and one commenter recalled the over-engineered iSight camera working through adapters until the M1 dropped its driver.

**Worth reading**
- [mulanroo](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49974410): Explains SNATMAP as Apple's 2003 predecessor to STUN (RFC 3489), why port-preserving NAT is required, and flags CGNAT as the likely cause of remaining failures since iChat has no TURN fallback.
- [jd3](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49974950): First-hand note that the iSight camera kept working through multiple adapters on Intel Macs until Apple removed the driver with the M1.
- [penskymaterial](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49974588): Argues the resurrection is a time warp to when video conferencing actually worked, contrasting it with an increasingly confusing FaceTime UI.

## 5. Opus 5.5 agents discover two room-temperature magnetic semiconductor candidates {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [vals.ai](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/room-temperature-magnetic-semiconductors) · 303 points · 198 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49970667)*

A post on the vals.ai blog (author goes by spicylemonade) reports that a team of Claude Opus 5.5 agents used density functional theory (PBE+U and HSE06) to propose two candidates for Luttinger-compensated antiferromagnetic semiconductors — zero net moment but spin-sorted band edges. The first, a never-synthesized YBaMnFeO₅, is predicted to have a 2.35 eV band gap with 1.0 eV hole and 1.4 eV electron spin windows, but the required Mn/Fe checkerboard ordering breaks down around 950 K, so standard 900–1300 °C synthesis would likely scramble it. The second, KV[Cr(CN)₆] (a Prussian-blue relative first made in 1999), is predicted to have a ~2.1 eV gap, 2.6/1.6 eV spin windows and an ordering temperature of 376 K measured in the 1999 powder — but the only sample contains water and shows a 0.125 μB residual moment, and the two simulation methods disagree on whether the hole window survives hydration. Full inputs, outputs and caveats are on GitHub (compensated-magnet-ledger).

**Discussion**: The thread is dominated by credentialed skepticism. A commenter with a PhD in magnetic materials objects strongly to the article's opening framing of magnetism as a binary of ferromagnets vs. antiferromagnets and to its claim that a fridge magnet's atomic magnets all point one way (domains, Halbach arrays, and ferrimagnetism all contradict it); several others note they expected paramagnets to be the second type. Materials/science commenters argue the work is routine: running Quantum Espresso on a literature compound and finding a promising combination is undergraduate-level work, 'not state of the art' for DFT, and would normally get shredded at a lab meeting rather than blasted worldwide. Many read 'room temperature' as invoking superconductors and reverting to LK-99 comparisons, and the HN title is called out as editorialized versus the actual headline, which emphasizes antiferromagnetic rather than semiconductor. Others note only one candidate already exists at all (a 1999 powder, not remeasured), that the other is likely impossible to synthesize with current thermodynamics, and that vals.ai is an evals company with an incentive to post eye-catching LLM results. A minority defends the work as a genuinely good use of LLMs — concrete, falsifiable, and checkable — while conceding nothing has been measured or re-made yet.

**Worth reading**
- [tedsanders](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49971175): A commenter with a PhD in magnetic materials says the article's ferromagnet-vs-antiferromagnet binary is wrong (diamagnets and paramagnets are far more common) and asks what property set is actually wanted for room-temperature magnetic semiconductors.
- [tedsanders](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49971315): Same commenter corrects the 'atomic magnets all point the same way' explanation: ferromagnets have domains, fridge magnets are usually Halbach arrays, and most are actually ferrimagnetic.
- [contemporary343](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49971124): A working computational scientist says the agents just ran Quantum Espresso, which is not state-of-the-art DFT, and that DFT results of this kind need heavy skepticism before excitement.

## 6. Dust: Pretraining Transformers Without Backpropagation {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [qlabs.sh](https://qlabs.sh/research/dust) · 153 points · 36 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49970871)*

A research post from qlabs.sh presenting Dust, a zeroth-order (derivative-free) training method for pretraining transformer language models. Instead of perturbing weights, Dust adds Gaussian noise to each linear layer's output independently at every token — node perturbation — so every token acts as a separate virtual population member evaluated in a single forward pass, with reward-weighted noise forming the gradient estimate. The authors claim Dust approaches or exceeds backprop as population grows, is roughly 10^3–10^4x more efficient than a transformer implementation of the weight-space ES method EGGROLL from 1M tokens up (by extrapolation), and that larger models are more population-efficient rather than less — a 243M-parameter model beats one 120x smaller at most population sizes, with alignment holding up to 1B tokens. They concede it is not yet compute-efficient enough to replace backprop today.

**Discussion**: The dominant reaction is skepticism that derivative-free methods will ever matter: the top commenter argues neural objectives are smooth, the gradient is cheap and increasingly useful as parameter count grows, a complexity gap between gradient-based and derivative-free Lipschitz optimization has recently been proved, and gradient-based structural optimizers like Muon are the better bet. Others attack the Bitter Lesson framing, noting the paper never discusses convexity and that Dust's smoothing plus a first-order method would likely erase its advantages, while conceding it does scale better than prior zeroth-order work. A parallel thread debates parallelism: one commenter says backprop is already just matrix multiplies and points to asynchronous neural predictive coding, which converges to backprop in a certain regime and needs no global coordination; another counters that coordination is essentially free on GPUs and that the brain's lack of backprop is a consequence of its wiring constraints, not a goal worth imitating. Several people suggest hybrids — using Dust to generate teaching targets or fine-tune an already backpropped checkpoint — and one notes Dust's smaller memory footprint for continual learning, though it does not solve catastrophic forgetting. A minority (including one commenter who dismisses it outright as unoriginal Monte Carlo gradient estimation) aside, the thread is unconvinced Dust beats backprop on efficiency but interested in the virtual-population trick and the larger-models-are-more-population-efficient result.

**Worth reading**
- [blt](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49974026): Argues derivative-free methods are structurally doomed: the objectives are smooth, gradients are more valuable as parameter counts grow, and a proved complexity gap favors first-order methods — with Muon as the better direction.
- [vatsachak](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49971640): Notes backprop is itself highly parallel, and points to neural predictive coding as a genuinely asynchronous alternative whose gradients provably converge to backprop within a regime, while doubting any replacement wins without dedicated hardware.
- [ACCount39](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49972786): Detailed counter-argument that coordination and bandwidth are cheap for GPUs and expensive for brains, so the brain's non-backprop learning is a limitation of its substrate rather than evidence zeroth-order methods are superior.

## 7. Friendship ended with Deno, now Node is my best friend {#story-7}
*#8 on HN · [dbushell.com](https://dbushell.com/2026/10/03/deno-to-node/) · 132 points · 65 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49971719)*

A blog post by David Bushell describing his migration from Deno back to Node.js after using Node heavily on a SvelteKit client project. He set up fnm for version management and pnpm (with a one-day minimum release age and post-install script blocking) to limit supply-chain risk, praises native TypeScript support, complains that Node refuses type stripping for files under node_modules (ERR_UNSUPPORTED_NODE_MODULES_TYPE_STRIPPING), settles on tsdown for bundling, self-hosts Forgejo on his own GitHub replacement, and reports that porting his static site generator to Node v26.10.0 needed only node:fs, node:path and Hono's node adapter — and ran 15% faster. **Discussion**: A large camp agrees Deno has lost momentum: a former contractor who worked on Deno's Standard Library says there is no roadmap or communication after the layoffs, and others argue an acquisition might have been the only viable path. But several regular Deno users push back hard — one points to deno test/lint/check and JSR's free TypeScript library publishing as still-unmatched conveniences, and another calls the 'no reason to use Deno' line laughable, citing built-in TypeScript, permissions and single-executable compilation. **Worth reading**
- [Naitronbomb](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49973527): Corrects the article's claim that Node blocks TypeScript in node_modules out of anti-Microsoft philosophy: the real reason is that the library's tsconfig may not match yours, and a .d.ts declaration build from tsc already solves publishing TS to npm.
- [benburton](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49975003): Says Deno remains the pick over Bun when sandboxing matters, noting no other runtime enforces the same network-level gating with an address allowlist by default.
- [nananana9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49974538): First-hand report of a Bun soundness bug — under HMR, re-exported module variables were snapshotted and importers saw stale values — with a hastily merged patch, offered as caution about the Rust rewrite.

## 8. Why Common Lisp is now the best programming language {#story-8}
*#9 on HN · [vivienhenz.com](https://www.vivienhenz.com/common-lisp) · 120 points · 165 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49973598)*

A blog post by Vivien Henz arguing that Common Lisp wins the LLM era because its image-based, live system removes the read/compile/runtime distinction (new function definitions replace old ones without restart), and its condition system opens a debugger with the full stack and live restarts instead of crashing, so an LLM can patch and resume rather than rerun. It also argues homoiconicity makes macros easy, letting teams build opinionated domain languages (his ERP example) that keep LLM edits on-model, claims his CL apps come out six to seven times shorter than the Python versions, praises the ANSI standard frozen since 1994, and shrugs off Quicklisp's few-thousand-project ecosystem and the hiring problem by saying LLMs can port libraries and strong learners can pick the language up in interviews.

**Discussion**: The dominant thread is that every language community has a version of this essay: commenters point out one can make equally plausible LLM-era cases for Python/JS (training data, no compile step), Rust (compiler feedback, borrow checker), and Go (fast builds, simple types), and the 2019–2012 HN threads on Lisp brevity get linked as evidence this argument is decades old. Many practitioners argue the opposite of the article's thesis — that strong static types are disproportionately valuable for LLMs because the type checker enforces correctness and acts as machine-checkable context, though others counter that CL is already strongly typed (no implicit coercions, no duck typing) and can bolt on ML/Haskell-style types via Coalton. Several push back on specific claims: resumable debuggers and hot reload exist in Python, JS, Erlang/Elixir and other Schemes; SBCL is slower and hungrier than C in the Benchmarks Game; and the macro thesis cuts both ways, with critics calling Lisp macros an ad-hoc, undocumented maintenance hazard. A recurring meta-criticism is that the post reads like post-hoc rationalization by a Lisp fan, or outright LLM slop with 404ing links. Against that, real users report LLM-driven CL/Clojure work going well: agents driving a REPL via MCP, LLMs cheerfully generating gnarly macros, and even Arc — HN's own long-tail Lisp — being handled fine.

**Worth reading**
- [dang](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49974250): dang says he was surprised at how reliably LLMs produce Lisp macros, including the backtick-comma arithmetic he always found tedious, freeing him to design macros while the model implements them.
- [varjag](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49974822): varjag reports using agents heavily on Common Lisp in production projects, with Codex plus a vibe-patched Tron MCP, and says REPL-based agent development does feel faster — though he never benchmarked it.
- [Jach](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49974948): Jach corrects the common claim that CL is dynamically/duck typed: CL is strongly typed with optional declarations and compile-time warnings, SBCL catches many errors, and Coalton adds a full ML/Haskell-style type system as a library.

## 9. An algorithmic failure beneath the secret ballot {#story-9}
*#10 on HN · [blog.citp.princeton.edu](https://blog.citp.princeton.edu/2026/08/03/an-algorithmic-failure-beneath-the-secret-ballot/) · 69 points · 35 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49945588)*

A Princeton Center for Information Technology Policy blog post by postdoctoral fellow Max Springer claiming that a 2022-disclosed flaw in Dominion ballot scanners — a deterministic, reversible algorithm for the "random" number assigned to each electronic ballot record — is still unpatched in many places. Using only public records (county early-voting lists and cast-vote record files) plus a $20 coding-agent subscription, he says he recovered the cast order of 1.52 million ballots (98.9% of in-person ballots) across 114 of 139 Georgia counties in the May 2026 primary, and that with check-in logs or CCTV he could match specific voters to specific ballots in small counties. The certified fix, Dominion software version 5.17, dates to March 2023 but saw slow uptake; he argues AI tools have collapsed the expertise moat that kept such exploits theoretical.

**Discussion**: The top thread supplies precedent and a correction: one commenter notes Diego Aranha's 2017 Brazilian exploit, where a CSPRNG was seeded with the machine's power-on time rounded to the second, requiring only brute-forcing 3600 orderings — the same failure mode, oddly, given Diebold's involvement. Several people assumed this meant paperless touchscreen voting, but two commenters point out Georgia has used hand-marked paper ballots since 2020, and that the flaw lives in the way the tabular cast-vote record and audit ledger are generated and published — a problem hand counting with risk-limiting audits could share. Practical proposals include real CSPRNGs, omitting the random number entirely, canonical lexicographic sorting of ballots, and Texas's post-2024 practice of sequentially numbered ballot packs that presiding judges shuffle before voters pick one. A large subthread debates why the list of who voted is public at all, with a polling-station operator defending it as an anti-fraud and public-assurance measure, and a voter-fraud anecdote drawing a sharp rebuttal that it is third-hand, unverifiable and contradicted by years of failed fraud investigations. A separate strand argues for abolishing the secret ballot outright and is firmly rejected on vote-buying and retaliation grounds, while a few commenters criticize the post's prose ("load-bearing", perceived LLM style) and say it is hazy about whether it deanonymized early-voting or election-day ballots.

## 16. Ephemeral Testing {#story-16}

## 19. The first fully implanted cochlear implant reaches patients {#story-19}
*#29 on HN · [spectrum.ieee.org](https://spectrum.ieee.org/fully-implantable-cochlear-implant) · 83 points · 64 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49920785)*

IEEE Spectrum 报道，奥地利公司 Med-El 发布了 TICI Unico——全球首款商用全植入式人工耳蜗（Totally Implantable Cochlear Implant）。麦克风、声音处理器、可充电锂电池和接收线圈全部植入头皮之下，整机重 20 克，皮下麦克风约镍币大小、位于头皮下方约 6 毫米，电池单次充电 1–1.5 小时可支撑最长 40 小时，公司称使用 15 年后仍能保证每充一次至少 24 小时。该设备已获欧洲监管批准，宣布当天上午完成了前四例商业植入，但尚未在美国上市；一项 30 人的临床试验称其听力表现与 Med-El 传统植入体「相当」，并让用户能在睡眠、游泳和充电时听见声音。

**Discussion**: 讨论首先被一项历史经验泼了冷水：一位曾在 Cochlear 工作约 4.5 年的工程师说，他参与过 Cochlear 上世纪 90 年代末的全植入项目 TIKI（TIKI 在 2000 年前后因利润问题被大规模裁员），最终植入志愿者的设备因为收进太多身体/血流噪声而被判定失败——他特别想知道 Med-El 是如何滤除体内噪声的。另一条主线是维修权与厂商锁定：多位评论者担心 15 年后必须开刀换电池，而如果公司届时消失或改款，用户几乎没有更换品牌的余地，并称骨锚式助听器（BAHA）甚至 IV 输液港都有类似的专有接口锁定；也有人认为心律调节器式的植入电池更换周期早已是常规，MED-EL 自 1990 年起持续扩张、并非上市公司，短期风险可控。关于「人工耳蜗听起来是什么样」，评论者用声码器（vocoder）作类比：正常耳蜗有 1.5–2 万个毛细胞，植入体只有 12–16 个电极触点，复音基本听不出来，但有用户表示自己学小提琴的失聪儿子依然热爱音乐，说明大脑适应能力惊人。其余讨论转向耳聋的社会代价——嘈杂餐厅里的孤立感、助听器在言语识别上的局限、以及 GJB2 基因治疗（Skylark、Sensorion、礼来收购 Akouos，再生元则把资源集中在 OTOF）可能让这类设备在未来变得不再必要。

**Worth reading**

## 20. Using Blu-ray M-Disk as backup of last resort {#story-20}
*#31 on HN · [smyck.net](https://smyck.net/2026/10/03/holocron-the-backup-of-last-resort/) · 92 points · 93 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49951693)*

A blog post by hukl describing a DIY decentralized archival scheme: burn your most essential data (digitzied documents from paperless-ngx, music, trip photos, plus whatever is needed to reboot a digital life) onto 3 Blu-ray M-Discs, pad them with 1 cm PE foam inside a 33.74€ B&W outdoor.case Type 500, seal the case with numbered breakable plastic seals, and hand copies to two or three trusted friends. The boxes get re-burned and re-sealed every 1-3 years, with the seal inspected and photographed each cycle; a silica gel bag goes in for moisture. The author notes he is not counting on the discs surviving 25 years — only 1-3 years between rotations — and that the passphrase can optionally be included as a digital legacy.

**Discussion**: The thread largely ignores the sealed-box craft and turns into a backup-media argument. One camp prefers LTO tape: robotmay bought an LTO-6 drive because it beat expanding a NAS on cost, leptons reports used LTO-5 tapes at roughly $1.40-$4/TB and no drive problems over years, and several people note that LTO is designed to sit offline on a shelf where hard drives and SSDs are not. The counter-camp questions the LTO ecosystem — clhodapp worries about reading tapes forward in 10-15 years, sekh60 notes LTO-10 broke backward compatibility, and ciupicri cites IBM's own spec (16-32°C allowable, 46°C kills tapes) to argue tapes fail in exactly the hurricane/AC-outage scenario you would need them for. A recurring correction concerns M-Disc itself: commenters including ndiddy point to a 2015 review arguing Verbatim's BD-R M-Discs were never substantially different from ordinary inorganic HTL BD-Rs, and jordand/m463 warn that Blu-ray drives and BDXL media are already getting scarce. A separate, more philosophical thread doubts the point of it all — several people say their kids and grandchildren will not care about hundreds of thousands of photos — while practitioners like csnover and dzink argue cold hard drives are worse than the thread assumes. The rough consensus is that no single medium wins and that verification, redundancy and periodic migration matter more than the choice of disc, tape or disk.

**Worth reading**
- [dzink](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49974417): Someone who actually ran the experiment warns that discs above 50GB need a $300 BDXL drive, that Japan-sold 「original」 M-Discs look black rather than gold, and that cloth/plastic sleeves can degrade the media — 50GB media plays fine on old players.
- [ndiddy](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49971953): A detailed correction with a 2015 review link arguing Verbatim's M-Disc BD-R was never materially different from quality inorganic HTL BD-Rs, and that M-Disc's own longevity data only ever covered DVD products.
- [csnover](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49971734): First-hand report of a new CMR drive burned in for 72 hours, padded and stored offline for ten years, then yielding so many surface errors that most data was unrecoverable — rescued only because a second copy existed.
