## 1. AI Models Can Provably Hide Arbitrary Capabilities {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13928) · 142 points · 74 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50065692)*

这是 Andis Draguns、Sumeet Ramesh Motwani、Raymond Douglas 和 Christian Schroeder de Witt 提交到 arXiv 的一篇论文（2610.13928，NeurIPS 2026 接收，22 页）。作者用“数字储物柜”（digital lockers）构造后门攻击，把加密的神经网络嵌入宿主模型权重中，只有在收到秘密触发词时才解密并执行，因此在密码学假设下即使拥有完整权重也难以被诱导或被解释——这把此前“隐藏固定字符串”的工作扩展到了任意计算。他们在 PyTorch 中实现了概念验证，用一个小型化学反应预测任务（CBRN 相关能力的代理任务）测试了微调、权重加噪、基于梯度的触发词搜索和 latent adversarial training 等诱导手段，结果显示这些方法都无法奏效，并公开了模型以帮助改进防御。

**Discussion**: 最高赞的一条争论是标题的误导性：多位评论者强调论文讲的是“能力可以被加密藏在权重里”，而不是“AI agent 主动隐瞒自己会什么”，并指出原文标题让人误以为模型有意图。反驳者认为二者仍相通——如果一个对齐不佳的模型去训练下一代模型，它同样可以把“潜伏行为”加密嵌入，而且开源权重模型同样（甚至更需要）担心这个问题。关于开放性出现了明显分歧：一派说开源模型容易被植入后门，另一派说闭源模型动机更强、更不可审计，还有人讽刺付费订阅照样塞广告、厂商与客户竞争，因此“闭源不会坑客户”不成立。有从业者分享第一手经历：在约 1B 参数的模型上做反向传播实验时，已被完全遗忘的“天空是意大利辣香肠色”这类知识会在后续无关训练中自发重新出现，说明权重中潜藏的信息远超预期分布。另有一组争论围绕可检测性：有评论者认为这种后门现在很“容易被发现”，因为权重看起来稀疏、不像正常训练结果，立刻有人质疑这一判断，并主张即便当前可检测，研究这种“毒丸”仍然很有价值。

**Worth reading**
- [vintermann](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50066796): 指出更准确的标题应是「能力可以通过加密隐藏进模型权重」，论文并非在说 AI agent 主动隐藏自身能力，是整条讨论的框架澄清。
- [johnsmith1840](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50067272): 分享约 1B 模型的第一手实验：一个被训练记住、随后彻底遗忘（准确率从 100/800 掉到 0/800）的事实，会在后续无关训练中自发重新出现并回升到 75/800，说明权重里潜藏的知识远超预期。
- [squeegeeninja](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50066770): 强反驳：认为论文只是很酷的演示，这类后门「很容易被检测」，因为权重会非常稀疏、不像正常训练的权重，随后被其他评论者质疑并要求给出理由。

## 2. The baffling intelligence of a single cell (2024) {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [jsomers.net](https://jsomers.net/e-coli-chemotaxis/) · 207 points · 28 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50041866)*

A 2024 essay by James Somers (jsomers.net) explaining E. coli chemotaxis as intelligence in its most elemental form — an interactive, illustrated walkthrough of how a bacterium with no brain forages. It follows the CheY/CheY-p signaling cycle that switches the flagellar rotors between «runs» (about a second, ten times longer than a tumble) and «tumbles», and the methylation of receptor «struts» that gives the cell a chemical memory and dynamic range — letting a 2% change in receptor occupancy drive a 100% change in motor output, and letting cells sense less than three molecules per cell volume. It quotes Dennis Bray's Wetware on the «futile» phosphorylation/dephosphorylation cycles that make signaling fast and tunable.

**Discussion**: The thread mostly drifts into ANN-versus-biology comparisons: several commenters argue a single cell is better understood as an entire neural network than a single neuron, and one engineer who built large-scale distributed simulations of real neurons from imaging data in a TMS lab says what ANN neurons miss is heterogeneity, fluid time-dependent dynamics and things like bursters, pointing to the decades-studied gastric mill circuit with single-digit neuron counts. Others push back on computer-science metaphors entirely, calling comparisons of cells and DNA to computers hyperbolic, and one commenter corrects a flowery «DNA is a computer» post by noting DNA is passive storage that only becomes Turing-complete once enzymes are added. A sidebar about whether the flagellar motor inspired the electric motor gets settled historically: motors were built in the mid-19th century, long before cellular complexity was known, while the bacterial motor's stator/rotor structure was only elucidated around 1970 — a discovery that fed the intelligent-design movement. The thread then derails into theology and a panpsychism tangent (one commenter dubs «if Space happens to be awareness / consciousness then…» a case of «'if' abuse»), while several people simply praise the site's design. A moderator-style pointer links the previous HN discussion of the same post from March 2024, which had 213 comments.

**Worth reading**
- [tyromaniac](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50067022): First-hand account from someone who wrote very large-scale distributed simulations of real neurons from imaging datasets in a TMS lab, explaining what biologically plausible models miss: heterogeneous connections, fluid time-dependent dynamics and bursters versus fixed-timestep ANN neurons.
- [dekhn](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50066577): Corrects the «did cells inspire electric motors» question with history: flagella were observed in the 1800s but their stator/rotor structure was not explained until around 1970, a finding that helped inspire the intelligent-design movement.
- [dekhn](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50066932): A blunt correction to a poetic «DNA is a computer» comment: DNA alone is passive storage — it only becomes Turing-complete when the appropriate enzymes are present.

## 4. Threat of AI share crash puts world financial stability at risk, says IMF {#story-4}
*#7 on HN · [theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/oct/13/ai-share-crash-risk-global-imf-debt-corrowing-cyber-attacks) · 40 points · 19 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50067356)*

The Guardian reports on the IMF's twice-yearly Global Financial Stability Report, which names an AI share price correction as one of three 「salient risks」 to global financial stability, alongside rising government borrowing costs and a major cyber-attack. The IMF warns that AI valuations are stretched and increasingly debt-funded — the Magnificent Seven now make up over 35% of the S&P 500, up from 12% a decade ago, with market caps up more than 50% in two years — and flags 「circular financing」 deals worth $300bn between hyperscalers and weaker AI firms over three years, plus $650bn in debt against $1.4tn in future commitments. Deputy director Athanasios Vamvakidis notes global exposure to US AI assets is at an all-time high, so a correction would spill over worldwide, with Asian markets most exposed and the UK and euro area relatively insulated.

**Discussion**: HN commenters overwhelmingly treat a correction as near-certain and argue about who absorbs the damage. The top thread's author contends the risk is concentrated in private markets — VCs and private credit take the hit, the average person sees limited direct impact — and that an AI bailout is politically implausible given the 「let it burn」 mood; a reply counters that the wealthy will still emerge rich and powerful regardless. One widely downvoted comment argues a crash is actually good for the public; that drew pushback that a global financial crash is painful short-term (one reply points to a pension portfolio maturing the day after), and a correction that valuations rest on high future growth, so a crash can come from slowing growth even if prices never rise. Others debate where money exits to after AI stocks fall — some expect a short freeze then capital chasing the next big thing — and note nobody can predict whether a correction frees up funding for non-LLM AI ideas. There's no real dissent from the crash premise itself; disagreement is about severity, contagion, and whether ordinary people are actually shielded.

**Worth reading**
- [cmiles8](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50067607): Argues the bubble's implosion is inevitable and that the industry's real play is positioning itself as too big to fail for a bailout — but says the risk is concentrated in private markets and an AI bailout is very unlikely.
- [weird-eye-issue](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50067717): Corrects the claim that a crash requires prices to fall: AI valuations price in high future growth, so merely slowing growth could trigger a crash on its own.
- [jijijijij](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50067671): Pushes back on the idea that a crash benefits the public, saying a global financial crash would be painful for everyone at least in the short term.

## 5. Who is vfmaddrnd231pd? {#story-5}
*#8 on HN · [purplesyringa.moe](https://purplesyringa.moe/blog/who-is-vfmaddrnd231pd/) · 174 points · 16 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50063138)*

A blog post about vfmaddrnd231pd, an x86 FMA instruction that Intel's Software Development Manual documents as performing packed double-precision fused multiply-add with per-instruction rounding control — which would let ordinary arithmetic run with controllable rounding without AVX-512. The catch: GNU assembler doesn't recognize it, the intrinsics _mm_fmaddround_pd / _mm256_fmaddround_pd are missing from Clang and even ICC, the VEX prefix byte includes an otherwise-unused 「DDS」 field, and running it on a Haswell CPU raises #UD. The author notes the instruction appears in the 2024 SDM but is gone from the 2026 edition and guesses Intel pulled it late (perhaps hardware in a corner case) without fixing the docs, while inviting anyone with an Intel contact to confirm.

**Discussion**: The thread mostly converges on the author's theory while correcting details. One commenter supplies the actual meaning of DDS from an old binutils commit: it is documentation-only notation for how VEX.vvvv is used (second register operand, first source overwritten), and assemblers treat NDS/DDS/NDD2 identically — so DDS is not evidence of a phantom instruction. Others link the Felix Cloutier reference page and back the 「hardware bug, pulled via a chicken bit」 explanation, noting Intel's vector ISA has always carried inscrutable mnemonics and it likely shipped with wrong rounding semantics in some deep edge case. The rest of the thread drifts into whether assembly syntax should be more human-readable: one camp argues x86 syntax is deliberately shaped for assembler/encoder simplicity (the FMA 1/2/3 encodings map to the encoding, not readability), another laments that unreadable mnemonics discourage human engagement and innovation, and a couple of commenters plug projects — raphlinus's symbolasm, which expresses the instruction as a = round(fma(a, b, c)), alongside their own AVR assembler experiments. Others poke fun at RISC-V's similarly absurd names and at x64 as 「the opposite of designed.」

**Worth reading**
- [pbsd](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50065430): Quotes an old binutils commit explaining that DDS is purely documentation shorthand for how VEX.vvvv is used, and that assemblers make no distinction between VEX.NDS, VEX.DDS and VEX.NDD2 — undercutting the suspicion raised in the post.
- [muricula](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50064530): Skeptical counter-take: Intel's vector ISA is full of odd, inscrutably named instructions, and the likeliest story is a corner-case rounding bug that made them disable the instruction (chicken bit) rather than fix it.
- [raphlinus](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50065687): raphlinus links symbolasm, his project designing an assembler syntax for mental tractability, which would render this instruction as a = round(fma(a, b, c)) for both human and LLM coders.

## 7. Janas: Run LLMs larger than RAM by streaming experts from SSD {#story-7}
*#10 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/prabanta-dev/janas) · 103 points · 32 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50063717)*

Janas is a family of dependency-free C tools by Maurizio “camauri” Cammalleri; its first component, Janas-LLM, runs mixture-of-experts models whose weights stay on disk and whose per-token experts are streamed from an NVMe SSD with O_DIRECT, with a re-use cache in free RAM. The README claims Qwen3-Next-80B (a 48 GB file) generating 37 tok/s — 24 without draft/MTP speculation — on a 32 GB laptop (Intel Core Ultra 9 185H, integrated Arc GPU, Debian 13, ~5.7 GB/s NVMe), versus 2.8 tok/s for llama.cpp, and 62 tok/s versus 16 while reading a 1,024-token prompt. The author labels it an experiment with an unstable API, warns it fills free memory and can push the desktop into swap (one GPU driver reset occurred), and notes it is only tested on the one machine it was written on.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between skepticism that this is anything new and interest in the measured numbers. Several commenters note that llama.cpp and similar projects have done expert streaming for years, call the work a reimplementation or a waste of time, and point to Strata as an existing equivalent; one commenter says the repo's model list contains no Qwen3.8-Flash and that people are now hallucinating model names the way bots do, while another replies that Qwen 3.6 and 3.8 share the same architecture and should be drop-in compatible. A long sub-thread argues over whether ~60 tok/s matters: one commenter says agentic workloads are dominated by input/prefill tokens (tens of thousands for hosted models), so 60 tok/s prefill would be useless, while others report self-hosted 60 tok/s working fine for agents and one describes getting 193 tok/s decode from V100s on a 3-bit quant. The most technical pushback is that the expert-caching policy is model- and prompt-dependent, non-obvious and not yet a solved problem — which is exactly what determines whether this approach holds up.

**Worth reading**
- [kennywinker](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50067046): Corrects another commenter by searching the repo: it contains no qwen3.8-flash, casting doubt on how carefully the model list and claims were verified.
- [kolinko](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50065566): Argues the 60 tok/s debate conflates output speed with prefill/input speed, which runs at thousands to tens of thousands of tok/s on hosted models and dominates real agentic token consumption.
- [bitexploder](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50066976): First-hand numbers from someone building a Flash Next engine on dual V100s: 193 tok/s decode on a 3-bit quant, targeting 220-250 tok/s at 128k context.

## 8. Making big downloads resumable in Firefox {#story-8}
*#11 on HN · [lunarwatcher.codeberg.page](https://lunarwatcher.codeberg.page/til/firefox/resumable-big-downloads.html) · 166 points · 66 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50061791)*

这是一篇简短的 TIL 笔记，指出 Firefox 28 及以后的所有版本（即 2014 年以来的现代版本）默认将 network.http.response.timeout 设为 300 秒，作者通过实验认为这既影响大文件下载的稳定性，也让 Firefox 无法续传可续传的下载；把该值改成 0（或一个很大的数）后，续传和稳定性都有改善。作者承认不清楚原因，也没有去查 Firefox 源码，并提醒前提是服务器支持 Range 请求，否则任何设置都无用，另一条替代方案是 wget -c -O。
