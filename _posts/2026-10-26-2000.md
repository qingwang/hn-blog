## 1. Microsoft Announces Type Server Protocol, Extension of Language Server Protocol {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/microsoft/pylance-release/blob/main/docs/tsp/type-server-protocol.md) · 93 points · 42 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50248371)*

This is the documentation for TSP, a JSON-RPC protocol that a client uses to ask a “type server” for Python type-analysis data, published in the pylance-release repo. It reuses LSP conventions (Position/Range, string URIs), namespaces methods under typeServer/, and defines version negotiation via semver, snapshot IDs that invalidate stale results, and three query kinds — computed, declared and expected types — plus import resolution and search paths. An optional multi-connection mode lets clients open read-only IPC channels alongside the main stdio/Node-IPC connection; it is in the 0.x line, so minor versions may break. The announcement posts are Pylance + Pyrefly integration in VS Code, though the submitted link itself is just a Markdown spec.

**Discussion**: The thread largely ignored the protocol and turned into a Python love/hate argument: one commenter called Python “a disaster of a language and ecosystem” that keeps bolting on things other languages had for decades, and replies split between agreement (packaging, virtualenv and the activate/PYTHONPATH hack drew the most specific complaints) and defense (ease for newcomers, NumPy/SciPy, the standard library, “worse is better”). On the actual story, the sharpest critique is about naming: commenters argue “Type Server Protocol” mines a generic namespace for something Python-only, especially from the company behind LSP, and others note the design is Python-specific — no for-all binder in the result types — so it is unlikely to generalize to Elixir or other dynamic languages; a counterpoint is that it lives under the pylance project and was announced on the Python dev blog, so the title was misleading rather than Microsoft's intent. Two concrete corrections surfaced: the basedpyright maintainer has publicly criticized TSP, while Astral's Ty will apparently support it, and one commenter asks why a new protocol kept LSP's UTF-16 character offsets instead of fixing that decision. Others were simply confused about whether LSP already covers type-at-point queries, with the explanation being that TSP lets the type checker (Pyrefly, Pyright) be decoupled from the editor front end.

**Worth reading**
- [nikolatt](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50249708): Links the basedpyright maintainer's detailed criticism of TSP and notes that Astral's Ty nonetheless plans to support it, giving both sides of the ecosystem reaction.
- [anonymous908213](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50249007): Argues the name squats on a generic protocol namespace for a Python-only spec, which is especially odd coming from the creators of LSP.
- [0cf8612b2e1e](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50249301): Points out the new protocol kept LSP's UTF-16 character offsets, missing a chance to correct that choice.

## 2. Sebastian Raschka's from-scratch video course on building reasoning models {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [magazine.sebastianraschka.com](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/building-reasoning-models) · 398 points · 55 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50245138)*

Sebastian Raschka released eight lecture videos (11+ hours) accompanying his book Build a Reasoning Model (From Scratch). Starting from pretrained Qwen3 0.6B weights in Python/PyTorch (environment set up with uv), the course builds a math-answer verifier and evaluates on MATH-500, then implements inference-time scaling (temperature, top-p, self-consistency, log-prob scoring, self-refinement) and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards via a simplified GRPO loop; only the later training experiments really need a GPU, since early examples run on CPU or Apple Silicon. He frames reasoning models as the engine inside an agent harness, with most current LLMs being reasoning models with adjustable effort.

**Discussion**: The thread is overwhelmingly praise for Raschka's teaching: multiple readers say his books Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch) and the reasoning-model sequel made attention, autograd and backprop finally click, and several describe reading them alongside study groups or video lectures. The real argument is about purpose: one commenter (hypfer) says he cannot see who the course is for beyond curiosity, argues video tutorials will not suffice for anyone genuinely training a model, and calls the enterprise a personal-brand monetization play selling an identity — which drew heavy pushback, including from a commenter who post-trains models professionally and says Raschka's book taught him more than any other source, and from others pointing out the videos are free and that learning-by-building is the whole point. A long subthread explores whether there is a hobbyist path past toy exercises: suggestions include LoRA fine-tuning of small or mid-size open models, training a small classifier for a real problem, and pivoting into data/model-infrastructure roles rather than trying to compete on compute; several concede that without serious GPU resources, home experiments are mostly noise. A side thread comparing Raschka to Karpathy gets corrected — they are contemporaries, with CS231n and Raschka's first ML book both appearing in 2015. Rough consensus: excellent pedagogy and free material, with skepticism only about whether it leads anywhere practical for the individual learner.

**Worth reading**
- [libraryofbabel](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50249391): Argues the point of from-scratch tutorials is understanding the layer below what you actually use (like Crafting Interpreters), and notes you implement real RLVR training — the technique behind 2025–26 gains in coding and math — rather than just reading about it.
- [danielmarkbruce](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50248438): A commenter who post-trains models at work says he learned more from Raschka's LLM book than any other source, directly countering the claim that the material is impractical.
- [kadoban](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50249835): Suggests the realistic hobbyist payoff is post-training and LoRA fine-tuning existing small-to-medium models on your own data, which needs far less compute than pretraining.

## 4. Oldest GUI application that still runs on Windows 11 {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [blog.enlyth.com](https://blog.enlyth.com/posts/oldest-windows-gui-app-win-11/) · 82 points · 29 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50248055)*

A blog post by enlyth hunting for the oldest unmodified GUI program that runs on current Windows 11. Because 64-bit Windows dropped NTVDM and cannot run 16-bit software, the trail leads to FREECELL.EXE dated 22-Feb-93 from early Win32s, which runs and plays perfectly (33 years and 8 months old); digging through NT 3.1 pre-releases on the Internet Archive hit dead ends (a 1991 draft PE format, and pre-mid-1992 builds using cdecl so API arguments get popped twice), until CDPLAYER.EXE from Build 347 (16-Nov-1992) worked because it links against the system crtdll.dll, which Windows 11 still ships in SysWOW64 — though it needs a small cdemu.dll to fake a CD-ROM drive and produce audio. As an acknowledged cheat, the DOS-based otvdm runs REVERSI.EXE from Windows 1.01 (15-Nov-1985) on Windows 11.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between nostalgia and pushback. The top comment laments the loss of the menu bar and native Win32/C development to 「mobile first」 design and Electron wrappers, with replies romanticizing skeuomorphism and 1990s Microsoft/Apple usability research — but others correct the premise, noting Windows 10 was a huge improvement over 8 and that plenty of apps still have menu bars. A second cluster debates whether writing new Win32 apps still makes sense in 2026: one developer reports a Win32 image viewer was very responsive but far less readable than React, another is happily using AI plus C and WinAPI, and a third argues for cross-platform toolkits (EGUI, Qt) because looking native matters less than not building barriers between OSes — countered by someone who cares whether apps behave natively (keyboard shortcuts, macOS accessibility integration). A tangent asks whether an 1980s X11 binary could run via Xwayland; the consensus is no for an unmodified 1980s binary, with BSD a.out/4.3BSD ABI support and SCO OpenServer floated as the only plausible ways to beat the November 1992 date, and a side argument over how badly dynamic libc linking breaks Linux binaries across distros. Scattered through it are links to a CD Player screenshot gallery, a FreeCell Pro-inspired web remake with a roguelite mode, and solitairealonetogether.com.

**Worth reading**
- [ndiddy](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50249857): ndiddy pins the target to beat at 16-Nov-1992 and suggests BSD a.out / 4.3BSD ABI support or still-patched SCO OpenServer as the realistic places an older binary might run.
- [0x0203](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50249899): 0x0203 explains concretely why old Linux binaries break across distros: dynamic libc version mismatch, with static linking helping but complicating X/Wayland GUI programs.
- [userbinator](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50249495): A developer who wrote small Win32 utilities in the early 1990s says they still run unmodified today, now mostly under WINE.

## 6. Nietzschian Debugger {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/DilawarShafiq/nietzschian-debugger) · 62 points · 27 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50247493)*

一个命令行工具（npm 上以 v0.1.0 发布，另有一份 Python 3.10+ 源码），会读取你粘贴的 bug 信息和文件路径，并通过 Anthropic API 调用 Claude，让模型不给出修复方案，只向你提问。它提供三档强度（socrates、nietzsche、zarathustra）、一个本地「成长分数」以及保存在 .nietzschian/sessions/ 的会话记录；README 的标语是「杀不死你代码的东西会让它更强」，并把它定位成应对开发者对 ChatGPT 产生依赖的补救方案——尽管用户需要自带 Anthropic API key。

**Discussion**: 大部分讨论集中在这个项目的「反 AI」标签上：它需要 Anthropic API key，配图是 AI 生成的尼采像，README 和代码由 Claude 合写，因此有几个人说它不过是一个套了层壳的提示词——好几位评论者直接问，为什么这不能做成一个 Claude skill。哲学爱好者则争论这个命名是否站得住脚：有人指出尼采在《偶像的黄昏》里恰恰是在攻击苏格拉底式辩证法的，所以苏格拉底才该是这个工具真正的名字；还有人指出「杀不死我的……」这句话原本出自爱默生，尼采是说给自己的。最实质的质疑来自一位认为这套脚手架仍然替代了用户思考的评论者：从「我不知道」跳到「我该去哪里查」，本身就是调试中关键的技能，而这里却是模型替你做完了这一步。也有一些人喜欢这个点子，其中一位预测，10B 左右的小型本地模型就足以驱动它。

**Worth reading**
- [bazzmt](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50248534): 指出其中的讽刺之处：尼采本人在《偶像的黄昏》中就攻击苏格拉底式辩证法，所以阿多诺的否定辩证法——那种永无止境的调试——或许更贴切。
- [ethin](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50249274): 自称有使用前沿模型经验的评论者说，这类工具总会以两种方式失败：给出「提示」后马上把答案泄露出来，或者变得极其吹毛求疵、抠你的用词——即便反复尝试调整也依然如此。
- [samstokes](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50249129): 提出更有力的反驳：像「去看看日志」这样的引导，恰恰是把最难的思考步骤——「我该去哪里找证据？」——替用户做掉了，因此依赖关系依然没有改变；他建议用更彻底的苏格拉底式提问（「你怎么知道它返回的是 403？日志里还能看出什么？」）。

## 8. A Man from the Future: Why Johnny Can't Stop Abstracting {#story-8}
*#8 on HN · [muratbuffalo.blogspot.com](http://muratbuffalo.blogspot.com/2026/10/the-man-from-future-why-johnny-cant.html) · 41 points · 14 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50247928)*

A blog post by Murat Buffalo reviewing Ananyo Bhattacharya’s biography “The Man from the Future: The Visionary Life of John von Neumann,” arguing that von Neumann’s real superpower was not general thinking but a specific “art of abstraction”: strip a messy field down to the smallest set of objects and axioms, write them down so everyone can argue about the right thing, then leave. The post walks through his Budapest upbringing under a banker father who ran dinner as a seminar, his 1923 ordinal definition of numbers (each number is the set of all numbers before it), his type-theoretic fix for set-theoretic paradoxes, the Hilbert-space unification of matrix and wave mechanics, the minimax theorem and the 1944 “Theory of Games and Economic Behavior” with Morgenstern, and the 1945 EDVAC draft that gave us the stored-program architecture. It also notes his misjudgment of Nash’s non-cooperative equilibrium and his hawkish nuclear work, including the Nagasaki implosion lens.

**Discussion**: Almost no one engaged with the post’s actual thesis about abstraction — the thread is two book recommendations and then a long fight about von Neumann’s deathbed baptism. Readers praise Bhattacharya’s biography and, separately, Benjamin Labatut’s novel “Maniac,” which one commenter says captures von Neumann’s emotional and social character better than any nonfiction account. The quoted line that his daughter attributed the conversion to Pascal’s Wager set off a theological argument: one side says an all-knowing God would see such calculated betting as mercenary or even punish it, and that the wager only works if the Christian God is the sole possibility, since a different god could punish belief in Yahweh. The other side counters that Pascal’s wager was anchored in specific attested claims about Jesus, and a commenter pushes back hard that this is a re-reading — the wager’s whole point was to justify belief absent evidence, via the infinity-of-reward versus triviality-of-cost asymmetry. A separate objection is that belief is not voluntary at all: you can attend church or donate, but not decide to believe something you find silly, which several commenters call the wager’s real flaw.

**Worth reading**

The sharpest disagreement is about model quality: hansvm and saurik ask whether forcing valid tokens distorts the model's distribution compared with retrying an entire failed tool call, which also yields 100% schema accuracy and avoids context pollution; hansvm gives a long-JSON failure example and a toy distribution to argue that retry preserves learned preferences such as ripgrep over grep, while jesdo says the post deliberately matches Moonshot's recommended serving behavior and cites mixed evidence on quality effects. Several practitioners say vLLM is powerful but fragile and confusing to configure, and that hosted providers vary widely; llama.cpp users note grammar-constrained decoding has existed there for ages, and draxen says OpenAI and Anthropic's real moat is 100% tool-call accuracy plus prompt caching, citing workloads of ~300 tool calls per prompt. bob1029 argues 92% is nowhere near enough because 95% per-call success compounds to only ~8% success over 50 sequential calls, while docjay suggests simply renaming tools to the names models already emit, such as `search_web` instead of `web_search`.
