An Economist leader (paywalled; only reachable here via an archive.today mirror) arguing that mirror-image organisms — life built from the opposite chirality to everything on Earth, i.e. mirror DNA/RNA and mirror proteins — pose a unique catastrophic risk and need regulation before anyone builds them. The piece ties the warning to the December 2024 Science statement signed by 38 scientists, including two Nobel laureates, that called mirror life capable of 'unprecedented and irreversible harm', and it also argues that publicly available AI models should carry guardrails against being used for mirror biochemistry. Commenters quote that AI-guardrail line as the article's most contested claim.

*Article not read: HTTP 403*

**Discussion**: The thread is small and splits into three camps. One side takes the threat at face value: mirror bacteria would slip past immune systems, antibiotics and phage predators because every defence evolved against the chirality of normal microbes, making this a genuine 'everyone dies' scenario. A counter-argument challenges the outcompetition premise — if ordinary life cannot metabolise mirror molecules, then the relationship is symmetric and mirror life should be no more able to exploit our biome than we are to exploit it. Several commenters are more annoyed by the framing than the science: they read the AI-guardrail recommendation as a hit piece and as another attempt to push governments into restricting open models, while others complain about the paywall and cookie wall rather than the content. Nobody in the thread claims first-hand lab experience, and there is no consensus beyond agreement that the December 2024 scientists' statement is the substantive reference point.

**Worth reading**

## 2. Lions' Commentary on Unix: Why It Was Secretly Copied for 20 Years {#story-2}
*#4 on HN · [fosscomics.com](https://fosscomics.com/posts/17.%20Lions%27%20Commentary%20on%20Unix/) · 58 points · 7 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50651719)*

A short illustrated Foss Comics post recounts how John Lions, teaching operating systems at the University of New South Wales, wrote lecture notes in May 1976 explaining AT&T's Unix Version 6 kernel source. The notes circulated among licensed Unix sites from 1977, became tightly restricted after AT&T announced new Version 7 licence terms at USENIX in June 1979, and were photocopied unofficially for roughly two decades. After lobbying by Peter Salus, Dennis Ritchie and Berny Goodheart, SCO finally agreed and Peer-to-Peer Communications published the authorised edition in 1996.

**Discussion**: The thread is small and mostly historical, with no answer to the opening question about a modern reprint. Commenters dwell on the irony that SCO, later associated with Unix litigation, was the entity that finally allowed publication; one clarifies that this was the original Santa Cruz Operation, or 'Good SCO', not the later SCO Group led by Darl McBride that came out of Caldera. Another adds that by 1996 Novell had already sold some Unix rights to the eventual 'Bad SCO', making the provenance messier than the comic implies. Someone also notes that Plan 9 was being distributed as books with source around the same time, suggesting Thompson, Pike and colleagues were already comfortable with source-available teaching material.

**Worth reading**
- [tankenmate](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50652476): Draws the key distinction between the original Santa Cruz Operation ('Good SCO'), which granted permission, and the later Darl McBride-led SCO Group ('Bad SCO') from Caldera, and names Salus, Ritchie and Goodheart as the people who pushed for publication.
- [sirtaj](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50652117): Notes that Plan 9 was already distributed as books including source in the same era, and points out the irony of SCO being the company that allowed Lions' book to be published.
- [sirtaj](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50652690): Adds a correction in progress: by 1996 Novell had already sold some Unix rights to the eventual 'Bad SCO', so the comic's clean SCO-permission story hides a more tangled ownership history.

## 3. Much shorter version of GRH Lean verification from Anthropic {#story-3}
*#5 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/lsernau/grh-lean) · 425 points · 216 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50648050)*

The grh-lean GitHub repository, credited to Anthropic's Luke Sernau and produced with Claude, is a Lean 4 formalization of the Riemann Hypothesis and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions, based on Levent Alpöge's earlier proof. It ships two editions of the same five theorems: a roughly 300,000-line readable version and a deliberately compact 76,000-line version, down from the original 17 million lines. The proof uses only propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound, builds against Lean v4.35.0 and a pinned Mathlib commit, and has passed machine checks only; no mathematician has read it through and there is no accompanying paper.

**Discussion**: The thread is split less over whether the Lean code compiles than over what that means: one camp says a formal proof is not a mathematical proof until a human can read and explain it, while another argues the Lean kernel, Curry-Howard and several independent checkers make it far more trustworthy than hand-checked mathematics. The original 17-million-line release is widely criticized as an unreadable 'slop' dump, and a claim that it hid an anyGeometry function returning True is corrected as dead code that Lean could not use to prove a false theorem. Commenters note the new release is a 99.56% reduction, but the 76,000-line version is heavily code-golfed and the 300,000-line one is the readable edition; skeptics say Anthropic should have shipped the cleaned version first. First-hand reports include riemann@home, which has 18 independent validators rechecking the proof, and jychang, who says he is 70% through his own 35,000-line formalization using a $100 ChatGPT plan and GPT-6.2 Sol. On Terence Tao's worry about huge proofs without insight, the prevailing view is that this case shows the proof was poorly written rather than fundamentally unreadable, so compression helps but does not settle the broader concern.

**Worth reading**
- [tsimionescu](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50652264): tsimionescu rebuts the claim that a Lean proof is merely a program needing human review, citing Curry-Howard, Lean's kernel and multiple independent compilers, while conceding that no one yet knows the human explanation of the proof.
- [jychang](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50648828): jychang says he is 70% through an independent greenfield formalization at 35,000 lines, built with GPT-6.2 Sol on a $100 ChatGPT plan using one agent, and calls the original 17 million lines overkill.
- [perching_aix](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50649178): perching_aix relays the Lean Zulip methods: Luke Sernau unrolled definitions into a long flat proof, simplified, then rolled it back up, while Devin Watson decomposed the proof into atomic binary functions and reached 528,681 lines.

## 4. Minipg: PostgreSQL as a Single Executable {#story-4}
*#6 on HN · [progrium.github.io](https://progrium.github.io/minipg/) · 94 points · 14 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50650892)*

Jeff Lindsay (progrium) presents minipg, an MIT-licensed tool that turns PostgreSQL into one executable backed by a .pgdb directory holding the data, config, generated superuser password and Postgres version; 'minipg app.pgdb' drops you into a psql shell and a server starts on demand and stops when idle. It embeds Docker Hub's postgres:18-alpine image and runs Alpine's own Postgres binaries via h9, a Zig-library hypervisor whose Plan 9 kernel answers syscalls — no Linux kernel boot, no VM image, no Docker — on Linux KVM, h9's native backend (Linux 5.11+), macOS Apple Silicon via Hypervisor.framework, and in-browser through v86. Lindsay warns it is early: roughly 2x slower than native Postgres for a single client on the native backend and slower under load, so don't trust it with data you can't recreate; the browser demo downloads about 35 MB and needs JavaScript Promise Integration (Chrome/Edge 137+).

**Discussion**: The thread is broadly admiring of the trick itself — running Postgres out of its own Alpine image without Docker, via a young embedded hypervisor — but almost nobody discusses the h9 approach in depth and no one reports running minipg in production. The real conversation is about embeddable databases for app deployment: one commenter wants a single-file Postgres to replace SQLite in a Node app packaged into a Vercel pkg executable, and is told SQLite got much easier since node:sqlite landed in Node.js v22.5.0, with pointers to Node's single-executable-applications support and the @yao-pkg/pkg fork that supports newer Node versions. Others steer toward pglite (Postgres compiled to WASM) for local dev and testing, and note the original poster seems to have conflated Vercel with the pkg tool. There is also a request for a MySQL/MariaDB equivalent, with litewire named as the closest but incomplete option, and a complaint that the demo page auto-downloads 35 MB on load rather than behind a play button.

**Worth reading**
- [galaxyLogic](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50651879): First-hand account of the pain of bundling SQLite into a Vercel pkg executable (native libraries, obscure errors), plus a link to the @yao-pkg/pkg fork that supports newer Node.js versions.
- [notpushkin](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50652435): Recommends pglite for Node local dev ('nearly flawlessly') while running full or hosted Postgres in production, and clarifies the confusion over what 'Vercel PKG EXE' means.
- [cantaffordsht](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50652424): Notes the demo silently downloads 35 MB on page load — unpleasant on metered or train wifi — and suggests gating it behind a play button.

## 8. What VP ≠ VNP Means (In 3 Minutes) {#story-8}
*#11 on HN · [friedgil.com](https://www.friedgil.com/vpvnp) · 108 points · 27 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50626466)*

A short explainer from friedgil.com (dated Nov 24, 2026) unpacking a claimed Anthropic proof that VP ≠ VNP, built on the contrast between the determinant and the permanent. Determinants have polynomial-time algorithms (Gaussian elimination), but the permanent — the same expansion with all the minus signs turned into pluses — has no known polynomial-time method, and computing it for 0/1 matrices is #P-complete. The post's reading of the result is that permanents cannot be computed by polynomial-size arithmetic circuits, so P = NP would require some bit-level AND/OR/NOT trick that beats plain addition and multiplication; the closing line invites the reader to find one and claim the $1M.

**Discussion**: The top thread is a correction to the article's central inference: commenters point out that P = NP would not require a polynomial-time permanent algorithm, since poly-time permanent is strictly stronger than P = NP — it would let you count solutions to NP problems, i.e. it is a #P statement, and the post is conflating NP with #P. A long side thread on whether P = NP breaks RSA converges on 'not automatically': a non-constructive proof gives no algorithm, and even a constructive one may carry galactic constants, with Levin's universal search cited as the theoretical fallback. Several people corrected the claim that solving any NP problem solves all NP problems — that only holds for NP-hard/NP-complete problems, and integer factorization is not known to be NP-complete (though it is easily shown to be in NP and co-NP). Smaller complaints: equations overflow horizontally on mobile, and the '3 minutes' framing understates the math background required.

**Worth reading**
- [articulatepang](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50651497): Corrects the article's key leap: P = NP does not imply a polynomial-time permanent algorithm, because poly-time permanent is a stronger result that would let you count NP solutions — the post is conflating NP with #P.
- [inkysigma](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50652289): Lays out what a P = NP proof would practically deliver: a non-constructive proof still gives Levin's universal search, but its constants are galactic; only a constructive, efficient proof would actually yield private keys.
- [johncolanduoni](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50652632): Fixes a widespread misstatement in the RSA subthread — only a solver for an NP-hard (or NP-complete) problem can be reused to solve every other NP problem; solving an arbitrary NP problem does not.

## 9. GPT-6.2 Astra is the first model to beat Googlers' code golf records {#story-9}
*#12 on HN · [bjin.github.io](https://bjin.github.io/go-codegolf-benchmark/#leaderboard) · 357 points · 98 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50601280)*

Bin Jin (@bjin), a former Googler, revived 32 problems from Google's internal go/codegolf contest of 2015 and ran frontier models against the original human byte-count records: 32 problems × 8 languages = 256 cells, with a 30-minute wall-clock limit and only two tools (submit and a playground sandbox) per cell, no internet or shell. GPT-6.2 Astra is the first model to beat the human records on the JS/Python/C++ score (the languages with strong 2015 fields); a privileged 'oracle' run (GPT-6 Astra with internet and unlimited time) holds the best-known size in 126 of 256 cells, and models have beaten 31 of the 32 Haskell records, which Jin attributes to how few Googlers golfed in Haskell in 2015. Highlights include a 103-byte Python Game of Life (20 bytes under the human record) and a one-liner that generates (-5)-smooth numbers via 4**i % (2**999-5) % (2**49-3) instead of nested loops, exploiting the built-in bignum arithmetic to emit 5·3 factors.

**Discussion**: Commenters largely agree Astra is strong at hard coding and math but noticeably worse at prose and instruction-following: several call it a regression from GPT-6 or inferior to Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 for 'it just works' and legal/technical writing, and one reports it adding Playwright as a dependency against explicit project rules. The most substantive counterweight comes from the agent-duel maintainer, who says the aggregate Elo ranking (Fable 5.2 first, Astra 6.2 around seventh) hides a split: Astra wins 65% of coding battles but only 30% of writing/discussion ones (p=0.0002), and that the 6.2 series looks tuned for cost, running ~0.68x opponent time and 3.65x cost versus 1.08x and 5.76x for Astra 6. Others question the benchmark itself — whether training on public code-golf solutions inflates results, why the oracle's code is never shown, and why Astra ran at max effort while some Claude models ran at xhigh; the author replies that Claude models actually score lower at max effort and links a section documenting it. A side thread debates whether code golf is worthless obfuscation or a genuinely useful exercise that teaches language corners and algorithmic tricks, with a historical anecdote about a 1991 BBS contest where readable Turbo Pascal beat hand-written assembly.

**Worth reading**
- [bjin](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50649057): The author posts another standout: Astra's 103-byte Python Game of Life, 20 bytes under the Googlers' record, using Python 2 backtick repr and zip rotations — with a link to the full scoreboard and code.
- [seizethecheese](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50649872): The agent-duel operator re-cuts his own leaderboard data: Astra 6.2 wins 65% of coding battles (47/72) but only 30% of writing/discussion ones (14/47), p=0.0002 — arguing it is strong at code and bad at chat, which skews general-purpose rankings.
- [ogogmad](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50649458): Points to BBC Challenge wiki results where GPT-6.2-assisted code golfing lowered the Busy Beaver logical-independence bounds (e.g. ZFC cannot determine the 192nd Busy Beaver number, down 33 from a month earlier).

## 10. Slow Ubuntu security response times {#story-10}
*#13 on HN · [openwall.com](https://www.openwall.com/lists/oss-security/2026/11/25/9) · 265 points · 117 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50647181)*

He notes that nine days after details of the remote TCP memory-corruption bug NoisyTCP (CVE-2026-99288) were published on Nov 16, Ubuntu still had no fix, while other distros shipped one at disclosure; the AMD KVM host escape CVE-2026-99312 (Nov 18) is also unpatched, and an earlier remote TCP bug, CVE-2026-98432, took weeks. He blames Ubuntu's strategy of backporting to an old kernel (7.0.0-44 on LTS) rather than tracking kernel.org longterm releases like Debian's 6.12.118+deb13, and says remote kernel corruption bugs should be fixed in hours, not days or weeks.
