## 2. Nobel Prize in Physics 2026: Francis Halzen {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [nobelprize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2026/) · 412 points · 127 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49976265)*

The Nobel Prize in Physics 2026 was awarded to Francis Halzen of UW-Madison for 「decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin.」 IceCube embeds over 5,000 optical sensors in boreholes up to ~2.5 km deep in a cubic kilometer of Antarctic ice at the South Pole, catching the faint Cherenkov light from the rare neutrino interactions. The award is notable for going to a single physicist, the first solo physics laureate since 1992.

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly appreciative explainer territory: commenters recap what neutrinos are, why they are called 「ghost particles」, and how Cherenkov radiation works, with some physicists refining the point that it is the phase velocity of light in the medium (not c) that is exceeded. Several people with direct South Pole experience chime in — one describes installing Debian for the data-processing systems, another spent 13 months there and says he would do it again, another notes IceCube hires two people to winter over each year with no physics background required. A recurring technical debate asks why it must be built at the South Pole; the answer given is that it is the only place with cubic kilometers of stable, dust-free, transparent old ice, plus existing skiway and station infrastructure — Greenland is logistically easier but has worse dust/optical properties due to European dust layers. A side thread argues over India's cancelled INO neutrino observatory, with a trained high-energy physicist defending environmental objections against the pro-science framing. There is also grumbling that the prize went to one person rather than the ~400-person collaboration (commenters note the most relevant co-contributors are dead), and one angry outlier rant dismissing the whole enterprise as academic waste.

**Worth reading**
- [exmadscientist](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49980603): A former UW-Madison student who sat on the author's thesis committee says the prize is not for conceiving IceCube but for actually making it happen, and vouches for him as a rare 「decent human being」.
- [assemblyman](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49980761): A trained high-energy physicist argues against destroying Indian forest and elephant habitat for the proposed INO, saying there are always alternate sites and experiments and that opposing a facility is not automatically anti-science.
- [cozzyd](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49982133): An experimenter who has worked in both Greenland and Antarctica explains Greenland's ice is likely worse for IceCube because of more dust layers, and describes Summit Station's comparatively spartan conditions (tents, then polar-bear-driven hard-sided berthing).

## 3. Release of Polars 2.0 {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [pola.rs](https://pola.rs/posts/release-polars-2/) · 327 points · 66 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49977177)*

The Polars team's announcement post (by Ritchie Vink) for Polars 2.0, which turns the streaming engine into the default for LazyFrame.collect() — meaning no guaranteed row order for join, group_by and unpivot unless maintain_order=True — and enables out-of-core spill-to-disk by default, spilling at ~80% of RAM with a 64GB disk budget (sort, window functions and many expressions; joins and group-bys to follow). It also adds first-class SQL plus a native Arrow Map dtype with dictionary-style expressions, and a stricter dtype/explicitness push the authors frame as faster feedback for AI-driven development via collect_schema(). The benchmark section claims default Polars is fastest on all but one TPC-H/TPC-DS query against DuckDB 1.5.6, DuckDB 2.0 alpha and DataFusion 54.0.0 on c7a.4xlarge and c7a.metal, while conceding a constant overhead at 192 threads that makes a 32-thread build competitive or winning.

**Discussion**: The bulk of the thread is a pandas-versus-Polars-versus-DuckDB debate: several long-time users call Polars a full replacement for 99.9% of pandas work, citing the query planner and API, while others say they still reach for pandas out of habit and for DuckDB when SQL is faster to write (one commenter cites a 1-billion-row benchmark of pandas 4m28s versus Polars 5.04s and DuckDB 5.19s, with DuckDB using 19x less memory). There is real disagreement about the benchmarks: one engineer warns that vendor posts like this are never 「A is X% faster than B」 because query choice, hardware, caching and thread counts dominate, and Polars developer orlp responds at length that he deliberately used third-party queries (DuckDB's tpch_queries/tpcds_queries), a third-party generator (tpcgen-rs), stock AWS hardware and out-of-the-box settings, with the single exception of also running Polars pinned to 32 threads. A recurring gripe is memory: commenters report OOMs from Polars' heavy threading, one fixing it by setting POLARS_MAX_THREADS=1 (which also sped up a narrow workload), and orlp points to per-query peak memory in the benchmark repo's raw results. The pandas-grandfather nostalgia thread drifted into R data.frames and Malloy/jq replacements, and a subthread tracked GeoPolars, which ritchie46 notes is now being developed on a dev branch rather than main.

**Worth reading**
- [orlp](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49979697): Polars developer orlp explains the measured 32-thread win as a concrete implementation detail: joins partition into T partitions per each of T threads, creating T^2 partitions, which is wasteful on a 192-core machine at SF=10 and is what he is fixing next.
- [orlp](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49982073): orlp's methodological rebuttal to benchmark skepticism: everyone always wins their own benchmarks, but he argues he used DuckDB's own queries, DataFusion's tpcgen-rs, stock AWS machines and out-of-the-box settings, with only the 32-thread Polars run added as an exception.
- [kaathewise](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49981783): A practitioner reports OOMs when incrementally reading Arrow IPC files with large batches because each Polars thread loads substantial memory, and says setting POLARS_MAX_THREADS=1 fixed it and even improved performance on a narrow task.

## 4. Tapo (Rust/Python library) now speaks TP-Link's TPAP protocol {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [mihai.dinculescu.dev](https://mihai.dinculescu.dev/posts/tapo-speaks-tpap/) · 99 points · 34 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49978563)*

A blog post by the maintainer of tapo, an unofficial Rust client library for TP-Link Tapo smart plugs, lights, hubs and cameras (with a Python wrapper over the same crate), announcing that v0.11.1 can log in over TPAP — the undocumented protocol devices use when the Tapo app's 「Third-Party Compatibility」 switch is off. The releases landed fast (v0.10.0 on 28 Sep, v0.11.0 on 2 Oct, v0.11.1 on 4 Oct) and also add the H200/H500 camera hubs with MPEG-TS recording download, plus plug schedules and timers. The key claim is that the compatibility switch is really a security downgrade: it restores KLAP, whose recorded login can be attacked offline, whereas TPAP uses SPAKE2+, so a captured handshake is useless for password guessing and later-learned passwords don't decrypt earlier sessions — though some cameras, e.g. a C210 on firmware 1.5.2, still require the switch on.

**Discussion**: The thread splits roughly three ways. A large group attacks the prose style as machine-flavored, long-winded magazine buildup with a weak payoff — several suspect AI, and one commenter points to a disclaimer that AI was used to 「polish」 the writing; the author (faithraven) replies that he did read it over multiple days and that paying ~$10k for ghostwriting release notes isn't worth it, while others defend the article as clear and tell critics to skip it. A second, more technical thread argues that since GLM 5.2 reverse-engineering closed protocols has become routine: give an agent IDA or Ghidra MCP plus a binary and sloppy instructions and it works; Retr0id and bri3d push back that the popular Ghidra MCP is badly architected and that scripting headless Ghidra or bintools directly gives better results, with bri3d calling the IDA Pro MCP substantially better. The author confirms it took surprisingly little time to add TPAP support, without an MCP server. Finally, newcomers confused about what Tapo or TPAP even is are told Tapo is TP-Link's smart-home brand and TPAP the TP-Link Authentication Protocol; the author corrects a misconception that KLAP is the old XOR protocol — that's the Kasa-line protocol on port 9999, while Tapo's original AES-over-HTTP was replaced by KLAP in 2023 and by TPAP in 2025. On local control he notes devices must be set up once via the TP-Link cloud app, but afterwards most functions work fully offline.

**Worth reading**
- [faithraven](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49981085): The author's detailed correction: the XOR/port-9999 protocol is the older Kasa line's, not KLAP — Tapo went AES-over-HTTP → KLAP (2023) → TPAP (2025) — and he explains that after one-time cloud-app setup, most devices can be controlled locally with no internet access.
- [teravor](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49979493): Claims that since GLM 5.2 it is remarkably easy to reverse-engineer any closed protocol with IDA/Ghidra MCP and a binary, where it used to not be worth the effort 95% of the time.
- [bri3d](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49980773): A reverse-engineer argues the popular Ghidra MCP is badly architected and that letting LLMs use bintools and headless Ghidra directly is more efficient, while the IDA Pro MCP is much better built.

## 5. Benchmark in Milliseconds {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [matklad.github.io](https://matklad.github.io/2026/10/05/benchmark-milliseconds.html) · 83 points · 18 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49967427)*

A short post by matklad (rust-analyzer, TigerBeetle) arguing that a microbenchmark should be tuned until it runs for about 300ms. His reasons: milliseconds are integers from 1 to 999, so they are precise enough to spot small wins and easy to scan without unit conversions or floats; anything under ~10ms risks being dominated by fixed costs like interpreter startup; and hundreds of milliseconds is long enough to be perceptible to a human but short enough to run ten times in a row while eyeballing variance. The stated assumption is that benchmarking exists to build intuition for a decision, not to produce a precisely accurate measurement.

**Discussion**: Most commenters accept the advice as a rough default for ordinary application code but push back hard on its universality. The strongest objection is methodological: spankalee argues absolute numbers say little, and that you should compare implementations round-robin within the same run and use 95% confidence intervals rather than means, as Google's Tachometer does — overlapping intervals mean you cannot tell which is faster. vlovich123 says the 10ms cutoff reads like Python-specific experience and notes Java needs JIT warmup and criterion needs stabilization time, though another commenter corrects the Python assumption by pointing to matklad's rust-analyzer and TigerBeetle work. Others add domain-specific caveats: browser benchmarks round timers to 1ms (one WebGPU author targets 50-100ms), HFT-style work cares about 10µs and needed core pinning, pre-allocated pools and no GC to get reproducible results, and a commenter measuring high-volume Java services says sub-millisecond server latencies and 99th/99.9th percentiles matter more than averages. A recurring meta-point, citing xkcd 2400, is that the rule works while you are chasing 3x wins on unoptimized code, but breaks down once you are hunting 2% improvements; a couple of people also note results can be unreproducible anyway because of CPU frequency scaling, interrupts and SMIs.

**Worth reading**
- [spankalee](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49981209): Argues benchmarks should report confidence intervals from round-robin comparisons within a single run, not absolute numbers or means, and points to Google's Tachometer as a runner that does this.
- [thadt](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49980536): Corrects the claim that the author's experience is Python-only by linking to matklad's rust-analyzer and TigerBeetle repositories.
- [bhouston](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49980394): First-hand report from building a WebGPU microbenchmark: browser timers round to the nearest 1ms, so browser benchmarks need roughly 50-100ms of runtime for accurate results.

## 6. Gleam doesn't compile to Erlang source anymore {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [gleam.run](https://gleam.run/news/gleam-doesnt-compile-to-erlang-source-anymore/) · 228 points · 99 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49975619)*

A release announcement from Gleam's creator Louis Pilfold for Gleam v1.19.0 (published 5 October 2026). The headline change is that Giacomo Cavalieri entirely rewrote the Erlang code generator: instead of emitting Erlang source text, it now emits Erlang abstract forms encoded in Erlang's external term format, skipping the front half of the Erlang compiler. Claimed benefits are significantly faster builds on the Erlang target, stacktrace and crash-report line numbers accurate to the original Gleam source rather than the generated Erlang, and cleaner compiler internals. The post also covers JavaScript-target work — flatter pattern-matching output from John Downey, direct prepend-based construction for short list literals, and a TypeScript overload so type-guard functions like Box$isFull preserve type parameters — plus build-tool commands aimed at Mix and rebar3 support, and LSP support for labels/fields.

**Discussion**: A large share of the thread is pure appreciation for the community and especially for Giacomo Cavalieri, whose Twitch streams and hand-drawn presentation slides several commenters praise; Cavalieri and Pilfold both show up to reply. A long sub-thread argues over whether a Rust programmer will find Gleam easy to pick up — commenters say yes because of shared static typing, unions, records and pattern matching (there is even an official Gleam-for-Rust-users cheat sheet), while Pilfold insists the two languages share almost nothing and that ML-family languages (Standard ML, OCaml, Elm, F#) are the real analogues, noting that 「How do I do X in Gleam like I would in Rust」 is a top source of newcomer support questions. On the technical substance, one commenter explains that Erlang abstract forms are just the Erlang compiler's AST and that most BEAM languages target it (Elixir does, LFE does), though LFE reportedly switched away from Core Erlang — a claim Pilfold corrects with source links. Others debate native compilation: Pilfold says Go is a poor target because its GC and shared-heap goroutines suit immutable/persistent data badly, that JavaScript's GC happens to fit immutable code well, and that C, Wasm or LLVM IR would be better for native output. There is also a predictable digression into English comma usage and a couple of hostile comments about the language's logo.

**Worth reading**
- [0x69420](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49977014): Explains what Erlang abstract forms actually are, why the standard library makes them pleasant to manipulate, and that Elixir and most other BEAM languages already target them.
- [lpil](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49978285): Maintainer Louis Pilfold explains why Gleam used to emit Erlang source — abstract forms weren't established as the go-to format, Core Erlang had no stable API usable from the Rust compiler, and the source-output escape hatch was valuable for a young language.
- [lpil](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49981143): Pilfold pushes back on the idea that Rust programmers slide easily into Gleam, saying the languages are very different and that this assumption generates a constant stream of confused newcomer questions.

## 7. AI is now capable of developing its own inference hardware {#story-7}
*#7 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/FeSens/openTPU) · 140 points · 125 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49980715)*

openTPU is an open-source AI accelerator published as a single small monorepo (SystemVerilog RTL, ISA, a bit-exact simulator, a kernel language and compiler, host tools like otpu-chat and otpu-smi, and a browser profiler called Lens), built with heavy LLM-agent involvement in a 'recursive improvement loop.' It runs ten real models bit-for-bit identical to the simulator on a decommissioned datacenter PCIe card (Inspur YPCB-00338, Xilinx Kintex-7 xc7k480t, two DDR3-1066 channels at 17.1 GB/s peak), reporting e.g. LFM2-2.6B at 11.07 tok/s, SmolLM3-3B 8.92, Phi-4-mini 6.69, plus MoE offload streaming giving 10.6 tok/s for LFM2.5-8B-A1B and 3.95 tok/s for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. The headline claim is that an AI agent, prompted by a human, produced the accelerator design; the author frames FPGA work as proving an architecture before committing to a custom TSMC ASIC, and notes decode is DRAM-bandwidth bound at 82-85% of the DDR3 peak.

**Discussion**: The thread's biggest fight is with the headline itself: the top comment argues the real description is a human prompting an LLM to build a hardware-design simulation, then prompting it to optimize within it, and a long subthread debates whether that distinction matters (agency/accountability versus raw capability, with an atomic-bomb analogy and 'can a submarine swim?' rejoinders). Skepticism also lands on simulator fidelity — if the simulation doesn't match silicon, optimizing inside it proves little — though the author insists everything matched the card token-for-token. A second thread asks why labs don't burn frontier models into silicon; answers converge on tapeout lead times and SOTA models deprecating faster than fabs can produce, poor fab capacity, lost programmability, and the difficulty of high-density ROM for weights, with Etched, Taalas (now acquired by AMD with nothing shipped), and the OpenAI/Broadcom deal cited — a counter-voice notes plenty of one-year-old models still serve billions of tokens and ASICs make sense for 'good enough' models. FPGA cost/speed digressions followed, with the author clarifying FPGA isn't a speed play but a way to validate architecture cheaply, and a reader dug into the RTL to find nonstandard floating-point handling, which the author acknowledged as harmless for LLM inference. The author is active throughout, claiming the design went from a few tokens per second to 80+ on small models via the recursive loop and that it already hits ~90% of achievable throughput without giving up programmability.

**Worth reading**
- [mbgerring](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49981884): The top-voted rebuttal: what actually happened is a human prompted an LLM to build a hardware simulation and then optimize within it, not an AI autonomously developing inference hardware.
- [random__duck](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49982005): A reader opened the RTL, found the floating-point math is not IEEE-correct enough for general use, and closed the page; the author replies that the deviations affect only very small/large values, are harmless for LLM inference, and asks for any concrete bug.
- [fsbonetto](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49981177): The author explains the board is a cheap decommissioned datacenter card popular with hobbyists, and that for inference the whole game is extracting as much memory bandwidth as possible per unit of logic, area and power.

*#29 on HN · [mastodon.social](https://mastodon.social/@bagder/117392573268225646) · 44 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49977832)*

这是 curl 维护者 Daniel Stenberg（bagder）在 Mastodon 上发布的一条帖子，称 curl 遇到两个只在 ARM64 上触发的编译器优化问题，一个涉及 GCC 15/16，另一个涉及 Rust（帖子中还提到 quiche）。由于原帖无法抓取正文，具体技术细节只能依据标题、帖子内容和评论区复原；评论者指出帖子实际指向的是 curl 仓库的 issue #23237。

*Article not read: page had almost no readable text (probably needs JavaScript or a login)*

**Discussion**: 评论区整体持怀疑态度，认为事情被过早地定性为「编译器 bug」。最有力的反驳来自 vlovich123：目前根本没有上游编译器的 bug 报告，更谈不上确认或补丁，也没人真正分析过编译器输出，证据仅仅是「-O3 会触发 valgrind 警告而 -O2 不会」，他并举出 OpenSSL 内联汇编写错、只是被 GCC 更聪明地利用的先例（openssl/openssl PR #23233），认为 Rust 那边同样可能是 quiche 里的 UB 被暴露出来。account42 也强调编译器 bug 虽非闻所未闻但足够罕见，默认假设应当是自己的代码有问题。另一方 rurban 则认为这可能是一个合法的新优化，只是 valgrind 对未初始化值过于严格（在 OpenSSL 的 strlcpy 上如此），并相信相关方最终会处理掉。edoceo 给出了讨论所依据的实际 issue 链接，算是为话题补上了缺失的一手来源。

**Worth reading**
- [vlovich123](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49978862): 指出目前并无上游编译器 bug 报告，并以 OpenSSL 内联汇编写错、被 GCC 更激进优化暴露的 PR #23233 为例，认为 Rust 侧也可能是 quiche 的 UB。
- [account42](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49979756): 批评证据链薄弱——没有编译器输出分析，只凭 -O3 触发 valgrind 警告就断定是编译器 bug，主张默认先怀疑自己的代码。
- [edoceo](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49978509): 补上了帖子实际指向的 curl issue 链接（curl/curl#23237），是评论中唯一的一手来源。
