## 1. GPT‑6.2 Astra and Sol {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [openai.com](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-2-astra-sol/) · 664 points · 379 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50535230)*

OpenAI's launch post for two new models: GPT‑6.2 Astra, pitched as the frontier model for hard professional work, and GPT‑6.2 Sol, the cheaper everyday model. Astra's API prices drop 20% versus GPT‑6 Astra to $8 input / $0.40 cached input / $40 output per million tokens, and OpenAI claims it beats Claude Fable 5.2 by 5.5 points on Agents' Last Exam at 5% of the cost, doubles Fable's pass rate on Harvey's legal benchmark at about a sixth of the cost, and tops AA‑3D with 1,318 Elo (Fable 5.2 at 1,271). **Discussion**: The thread splits between people impressed by the price/performance and people who say benchmarks keep not matching their day-to-day experience. A large camp reports that GPT‑6.x over-engineers — pointless abstractions, too many files, huge diffs — and several say they now default to Opus 5.5 or Fable 5.2 for coding; one commenter's agent-battle data puts Sol 6.1 at 1.42x the diff size of Opus 5.5. Defenders counter with effort-normalized tables showing Astra ahead of Opus 5.5 at medium, high and xhigh reasoning and far cheaper per task, while conceding Fable still wins at max effort. **Worth reading**

## 4. Solid 2.0 {#story-4}
*#6 on HN · [solidjs.com](https://www.solidjs.com/blog/solid-2-0-the-long-hold/) · 57 points · 13 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50536387)*

Solid's creator Ryan Carniato marks the stable release of Solid 2.0, five years after 1.0 and after 23 release candidates in 14 weeks (971 pull requests merged, 753 issues closed). The headline changes: async primitives folded directly into the fine-grained reactive graph so promises and async iterators roll back speculative state automatically, server functions moved from SolidStart into Solid core, and several APIs removed entirely (createResource, batch, startTransition, on, produce, createComputed, createMutable). It also argues the shrinking API surface and runtime diagnostics — detecting waterfalls, effect loops, wide fan-outs, with stable error codes and source locations plus a Sentry plugin — make Solid unusually well suited to AI agents writing code, and retires the SolidStart metaframework in favor of framework-agnostic Vite plugins and options like TanStack Router and Query.

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly congratulatory and short on technical debate, with one commenter lamenting Solid's thin component-library ecosystem and another correcting them with a list — Kobalte, Ark UI, Park UI, Shadcn Solid, Corvu and Vercel's new UI SDK, which now ships Solid support. The livelier exchange is over the article's 'built to be written by agents' pitch: one commenter argues agents remove the reason frameworks exist at all, while others counter that LLMs use reactive frameworks effectively and produce more consistent code when a framework enforces patterns. A Svelte user reflects on why the community chose Svelte over Solid years ago — simpler-looking templates, React fatigue — and now wonders whether Solid's plain-JavaScript model would feel more natural.

**Worth reading**
- [pwmglenn](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50537180): Pushes back on the claim that Solid lacks component libraries, listing Kobalte, Ark UI, Park UI, Solid UI, Shadcn Solid, Corvu, Material Design Solid and Vercel's newly launched UI SDK with Solid support.
- [rafram](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50537093): Strong counter-argument to 'agents make frameworks obsolete': LLMs are very effective at using reactive frameworks and write more maintainable, consistent code with them than hand-rolled DOM JavaScript.
- [_nothing](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50537109): A Svelte user explains the historical drift toward Svelte (simpler templates, React fatigue) and says Solid's plain-JavaScript approach may now feel more natural, while noting it's a good moment to try 2.0 before coding models catch up.

## 5. What is the best harness for each model? {#story-5}
*#7 on HN · [runta.com](https://runta.com/blog/introducing-frontierharness-eval-v1-1/) · 268 points · 94 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50532191)*

Runta (with Baseten serving the models) published FrontierHarness Eval v1.1, a leaderboard of 22 harness configurations across 5 open-weight models (GLM-5.3, GLM-5.3-Flash, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017, Muse, Kimi-K3), 62 terminal tasks and 4 attempts each: 27,280 runs and 83B tokens. Its claim is that the best-value harness changes when you change models — Codex CLI is the cheapest per pass with GLM-5.3 ($0.803) but 16th on cost per task with Kimi-K3, and cost ratios between best and worst harness within a model run 1.8x to 5.0x. A two-way ANOVA attributes 92.8% of cost variation to the model, only 2.3% to the harness alone and 4.9% to the pair; 64.5% of spend went to trials that did not pass, and 216 trials were flagged for reward hacking (163 of them online solution lookups, scored 0).

**Discussion**: The thread is split between interest in the data and skepticism of the framing. The strongest pushback comes from a commenter who ran his own 927k-run experiment and found little harness-by-model interaction, contradicting the headline claim, plus complaints that the 62 tasks (17 from Terminal-Bench 4, 45 from TB 2.1) were never justified and look cherry-picked; the author replies that full datasets were too expensive and that task lists are published on the Harbor hub. Several people argue the 64% failure cost is unsurprising — failing attempts are where search happens, the same way human prototypes fail — and that 'steps per pass predicts cost' is close to tautological, since cost is token-proportional. Practitioners add that a domain-specific bespoke harness beats any general one on their own work, that binary test pass/fail misses code quality, and that the results only cover open models served by one provider, so closed models like Claude or GPT could reshuffle the rankings. The founder of Runta showed up to summarize the findings and was flagged by moderators for apparently AI-generated writing.

**Worth reading**
- [seizethecheese](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50533954): A commenter replicated the study at 927k agent runs instead of 27k and found little harness-by-model interaction, directly undercutting the post's central claim (OpenAI models do show some preference), and notes the submission's original title was a clickbait framing about wasted spend.
- [xyzzy_plugh](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50535332): Argues that for a single domain with tailored tools and enforced token budgets, a custom harness 'absolutely smokes' every mainstream harness, so leaderboard results from a handful of benchmark scenarios rarely map to real needs.
- [bisonbear](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50534665): Points out the cost metric decides the winner: measuring cost per task instead of cost per pass flips the overall winner from Codex CLI ($0.937 per pass vs $0.573 per task) to OpenCode, and the author's reply only restates the preference rather than resolving it.

## 7. Show HN: Alloy CSS, dialogs, menus and tabs that work with JavaScript off {#story-7}
*#11 on HN · [alloyhtml.com](https://alloyhtml.com/example.html) · 140 points · 23 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50532385)*

Alloy CSS is a class-based stylesheet whose demo page is a fictional invoicing app called 'Ledger' — a dashboard of invoice stats, an overdue list, an activity feed and an FAQ — built as a single HTML file with no script at all. The pitch is that native web primitives (<dialog> with commandfor, popover and :has()) now handle menus, modals, drawers, tabs, tooltips and toasts without JS. Author 'prafandy' says it has existed since 2018, is MIT-licensed, ~24 KB gzipped on npm and jsDelivr, and needs Chrome 135, Firefox 144 or Safari 26.2 for the dialog behaviour; unlike Pico it is not classless, using a fixed scale of classes, and he sells page templates on top.

**Discussion**: The thread is broadly sympathetic to the no-JS ethos — several commenters say static pages feel more stable and permanent, and one notes his own network is not free even if code is, asking why a Jira board needs 42 MB. The sharpest pushback is practical: the class names are common enough to collide with existing styles, and the author concedes it is a new design system rather than a drop-in utility layer, offering to point people at just the classes they need. Accessibility is the main fault line — commenters call no-JS widgets inaccessible, the author asks for specifics and admits the tabs are announced as radio buttons by screen readers, while another commenter gives a concrete case: the 'Selected invoices' banner and download button appear with no announcement to a screen-reader user. One defence of pure HTML is that toasts not auto-dismissing is correct per accessibility guidance, and a sceptic argues that progressive enhancement was always a lot of work nobody asked for and complexity will bring back Rube Goldberg machines. An Open UI contributor notes that primitives like popover were incubated there precisely so developers don't rewrite these patterns per project.

**Worth reading**
- [lopis](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50535538): Gives a concrete accessibility failure: a screen-reader user is never told that the 'Selected invoices' banner and its download button appeared, which needs ARIA live-region handling.

## 8. New Evidence Shows School Phone Bans Are Gradually Improving Test Scores {#story-8}
*#14 on HN · [nytimes.com](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/17/upshot/school-phone-bans-study-scores.html) · 994 points · 497 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50530163)*

A New York Times Upshot piece on the first large national analysis of school phone bans, published by Harvard's Center for Education Policy Research. It compares MAP fall-to-spring test scores for 3.3 million students in grades 3-10 across 4,600 U.S. schools from 2021 to 2026, and finds schools with strict bans (phones locked in pouches all day) gained roughly the equivalent of one to two extra weeks of schooling a year in math and reading — reversing about half the average annual pre-pandemic loss. Author Thomas Kane argues earlier studies that dismissed phone use as a minor factor were 'mistaken.'

**Discussion**: The thread is broadly sympathetic to bans but splits sharply on the evidence. Many parents and teachers supply anecdotes: kids like the ban once everyone is covered by it, lunchtime conversation returns, and phone-free soccer trips build camaraderie; teachers describe confiscation authority eroding under parent pressure and legal threats, and several blame school-shooting fears and parent demands for contact for phones being tolerated in the first place. The most substantive pushback is statistical: the study's headline effect is really about 0.01 standard deviations, and one commenter notes that dropping the authors' post-hoc trimming of extreme observations shrinks it to 0.0058 SD and kills significance, while another cites a prior study in the same setting that found no significant effect, plus diverging pre-trends, cross-state propensity matching and ESSER funding as confounders. Others point out that anecdotes about behavior and morale may be real even if test-score effects are tiny, and a side thread argues Chromebooks and tablets are the next device to go. Rough consensus: something was lost when phones entered classrooms, but the size of the academic gain is much smaller than the headlines suggest.

**Worth reading**
- [rahimnathwani](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50531865): Breaks down the actual numbers: the effect is 0.0087 ± 0.0043 SD (95% CI roughly 0.0003 to 0.0171), and without the authors' exclusion of 0.5th/99.5th percentile observations it falls to 0.0058 SD and loses statistical significance, with no sign the trimming was preregistered.
- [tfirst](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50531895): Argues the identification is shaky — diverging pre-trends, propensity matching across state lines, and a value-added model instead of raw grades — and notes a similar study in the same setting (Dee et al.) found no significant effect, plus that pouch-buying schools had more ESSER money to spend on tutoring.
- [eitally](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50531931): First-hand report from a parent chaperoning youth soccer travel: taking phones at 9pm instead of lights-out measurably improved camaraderie and maturity among 18-24 girls, offered as evidence the social benefit is real even if test scores barely move.

## 10. First Photos of the Poles of a Rod-Shaped Bacterium {#story-10}
*#16 on HN · [nautil.us](https://nautil.us/check-out-the-first-photos-of-the-poles-of-a-rod-shaped-bacterium-1286664) · 94 points · 20 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50518328)*
