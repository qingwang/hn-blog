## 1. Things to know when you get cancer {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [jjmaxwell4.com](https://jjmaxwell4.com/writing/things-to-know-when-you-get-cancer/) · 1,168 points · 296 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50093568)*

A first-person post by jjmaxwell4, diagnosed in May 2026 with squamous cell carcinoma in his mouth, listing practical lessons from his treatment so far: call back for appointment cancellations, don't trust LLMs that told him he probably didn't have cancer, push hard for comprehensive genetic testing after a multi-month delay, read the NCCN standard-of-care flowcharts yourself, get a second opinion and a second pathology read at a top cancer center, and check the Sijbrandij Foundation's (Sid Sijbrandij of GitLab) resources. He argues that if you live within a 2-3 hour drive of a major center (he cites MD Anderson) you should go there instead of a community hospital, mainly because answers come in minutes rather than days.

**Discussion**: The thread is dominated by near-unanimous agreement on second opinions and aggressive self-advocacy, backed by many hard personal stories: a mother whose stage 1 diagnosis became stage 4 after a Dana-Farber second read, a misdiagnosed lung cancer treated as low iron, a brain tumor found only after a third doctor ordered an MRI, and one commenter who says it took 21 doctors to find his friend's mother's brain tumor. Several practitioners-of-the-system add tactical nuance: get the second opinion before a treatment plan exists, because specialists otherwise won't override the first oncologist, and be careful comparing scans read at different hospitals. Dissent comes from two directions — a commenter with colon cancer says the list is practical but misses the real work (estate, funeral, accepting death), and another argues second opinions give false confidence since diagnostics are low-signal and everyone follows the same NCCN guidelines. Non-US commenters (Ireland, Nordics, India) say the whole list is US-coded: they got scans in days, free or nearly free care, and same-day specialist access without chasing anyone.

**Worth reading**
- [jcims](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50096154): A caregiver for a wife with ovarian cancer argues to get second opinions BEFORE a treatment plan is set — otherwise specialists like Cleveland Clinic and MSK simply refused to override the original plan, making the visits no-ops — and warns that any immunotherapy treatment disqualifies you from most subsequent immunotherapy trials.
- [jongjong](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50096478): A husband whose wife had stage 3 cancer describes building an LLM-assisted decision tree over a weekend, then pushing back when the doctor recommended chemo-radiation for a tumor type that responds to immunotherapy — the tumor shrank ~50% in a month, and he notes she was a textbook match for a famous 100%-recovery trial.
- [tonymet](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50096104): A counter-argument that second opinions are false confidence: diagnostics are primitive and low-signal, all centers share the same NCCN treatment guidelines, and patients must make peace with never really knowing whether a half-million-dollar multi-year course was necessary.

## 2. OpenCode 2.0 {#story-2}

## 3. Tiny Little Office {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [tinylittleoffice.com](https://tinylittleoffice.com/) · 324 points · 46 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50096142)*

Tiny Little Office (tinylittleoffice.com, 324 points) is a browser-based office suite — word processor, spreadsheets, presentations, an animator and a trip planner — whose gimmick is that the entire document is serialized into the link itself, so a shared URL is the file. The site's landing page returned almost no readable text to the fetcher (JavaScript-dependent), so the specifics here come from the title, the HN post and what commenters describe. A commenter points to the co-founder's write-up on aboard.com, which says the idea dates back six years and that earlier versions of these sites fell off the web and their links broke.

*Article not read: page had almost no readable text (probably needs JavaScript or a login)*

**Discussion**: Commenters are mostly admiring of the execution (「absolutely lovely」, 「so well done」) and immediately map it onto predecessors: TiddlyWiki saving to itself, bento.page's presentation editor that embeds editor and content in one HTML file, nathanvy's singlebook for documents, and one user's semantic-HTML remix at hashpage.dev. The most consistent objection is that there is no way to open an existing document — no .csv, .txt or .ics import — which several call a real blocker for spreadsheets that are rarely started from scratch. A second, structural complaint: keeping the document in the URL caps its size at roughly 2048 KiB in Chrome and 64 KiB in Firefox, so 「easy to share」 falls apart for anything big. One commenter reports the site's OpenStreetMap tiles returning 403, and others reply that OSM's tiles are not intended for end-user apps and should be replaced with Mapbox or self-hosted tiles. Alongside small bug reports (cursor placement in the word processor when switching typing methods, black-on-dark text in dark-mode presentation previews), people ask for live collaboration, PDF annotation, a how-to guide, and a native desktop alternative to MS Office — though one sceptic dismisses the whole thing as a worse way to redo something solved decades ago.

**Worth reading**
- [simonw](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50096438): Simon Willison notes that pointing an LLM at the site's llms.txt lets it generate URLs that render documents, and links a deck he had generated about the URL format.
- [yellowapple](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50097353): Reports that the site is being blocked by OpenStreetMap with a 403 and links OSM's blocked-tiles policy — a live operational problem.

## 4. LFM2.5-24B-A2B: Built for fast on-device agents {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [liquid.ai](https://www.liquid.ai/blog/lfm2-5-24b-a2b) · 167 points · 39 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50097369)*

## 6. Americans' social activity has steeply declined in the past 30 years {#story-6}
*#7 on HN · [dataforprogress.org](https://www.dataforprogress.org/blog/2026/9/29/americans-social-activity-has-steeply-declined-in-the-past-30-years) · 345 points · 333 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50089921)*

Data for Progress surveyed 1,098 U.S. adults in 2026 about how many times they did specific social activities in the past 12 months, replicating the DDB Needham Life Style surveys (1975–1999) that Robert Putnam cited in 「Bowling Alone」, and compared the results with 1995. It reports double-digit declines in nearly every activity tested: entertaining at home fell from 88% to 67%, church attendance from 74% to 54%, picnics from 58% to 39%, club meetings from 45% to 26%, plus drops in card playing, breakfast out and volunteering — while internet use rose 90 points, home video games 22, exercise classes 14 and concerts 13. The authors warn the two surveys used different sampling frames, modes (mail quota sample vs. web panel) and weighting, so the numbers should be read as suggestive of trends rather than precise estimates.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between technology explanations — phones, TV and streaming as a parasitic draw on attention and the killer of boredom, with many pointing back to Putnam’s work and the familiar 「Bowling Alone」 thesis — and structural ones: cost of going out, expensive babysitting, car-dependent suburbs with no third places, long work hours, and (per one commenter) housing prices turning pubs into condos. A recurring counter-argument is that people have simply chosen other uses of their time, not that barriers are insurmountable: several say picnics and hikes are nearly free, that a $200 dinner-plus-$100-parking night out is a self-imposed choice, and one immigrant in a 600-unit high-rise insists abundant social contact is available to anyone who looks for it. Practical experience is mixed — hobby and creative meetups (music, D&D, tabletop) reliably produce lasting friendships, but many report that post-Covid meetups no longer convert into out-of-event contact, that club leaders aged out and nobody replaced them, and that exchanging Instagram handles is a weaker commitment than a phone number. A methodological objection appears repeatedly: the data counts events and never asks about group chats, gaming together online or other digital socializing, so 「in-person」 would be the more honest headline. There is rough agreement that the decline is real and not purely American, and open disagreement about whether the primary cause is media, money, work, or fading shared culture/religion.

**Worth reading**
- [paimapi](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50091577): A community organizer says the barriers they actually see are exhausted day jobs, bad traffic, needing a second shift, and the cost of a place to meet — not lack of interest — and argues Putnam blamed television instead of the underlying structural causes.
- [lotsofpulp](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50098256): Corrects the thread’s iPhone narrative: the 2007 model had no App Store and no 3G, was mostly used for texting, and the always-on, endlessly entertaining device really arrived with the iPhone 3G in 2008.

## 8. The Double Headed Stack Allocator {#story-8}
*#9 on HN · [blog.demofox.org](https://blog.demofox.org/2026/10/03/the-double-headed-stack-allocator/) · 50 points · 11 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50098274)*

A blog post by Alan Wolfe (demofox) explaining the bump/stack allocator he implemented for animation blending on Surreal Software's cancelled open-world game 「This is Vegas」 — allocation is an integer add to a top-of-stack offset, and freeing everything is a single assignment back to 0, which removed the allocator from the profiler entirely. It then describes the double-headed variant he learned from James Anhalt at Blizzard's StarCraft 2 team: level data grows up from offset 0 while character data grows down from the end of the same block, so each system can free and reallocate its whole region without fragmentation, with OOM detected when the two offsets cross. A third section extends the idea to storing variable-sized objects in fixed memory using one headed stack for the objects and the other for an array of offsets, and links AMD's VulkanMemoryAllocator docs on the linear algorithm.

**Discussion**: Commenters mostly agree the pattern is sound and widely used, then push on the alternatives and the concurrency claim. Panzerschrek argues a stack-like allocator is the wrong tool for streaming level data and suggests a cyclic allocator, which he says Quake used for its surface cache; saagarjha describes instead double-mapping a GPU staging region so pointers wrap around, which he found more powerful because it supports allocating multiple pipeline stages ahead, whereas the two-headed layout only affords one extra stage. HexDecOctBin disputes the article's core justification, arguing that bounding each system separately (x ≤ X and y ≤ Y, e.g. via cell-based levels and asset-pipeline limits) is much easier to reason about than ensuring every level/character size combination fits under x + y ≤ Z. The most-argued point is thread safety: the article asserts a compare-and-swap loop is unnecessary but never explains why, and several commenters propose schemes — packing both offsets into a single 64-bit atomic CAS word (still addressing 4 GB), a seqcst fence before the overlap check, and using a CAS loop rather than fetch_add so that overflow cannot let later allocations falsely succeed. Woodi adds that stack-style freeing is effectively very fast garbage collection, and one practitioner notes atomic bump counters suffer cache-coherency thrashing, so per-thread allocators (merged afterward or walked via a custom iterator) can be faster.

**Worth reading**
- [saagarjha](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50098885): First-hand report of using a twice-mirror-mapped virtual region so an in-order GPU streaming allocator wraps around, which supports allocating several pipeline stages ahead — something the two-sided allocator cannot do.
- [singron](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50099318): Detailed concurrency analysis: incrementing past each other is fine if overlap is detected and the allocation fails, but fetch_add is unsafe under overflow, so a packed 64-bit CAS over both offsets is likely both correct and faster.
- [moring](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50099955): Quotes the article's claim that no compare-and-swap is needed and points out it never explains how the two-counter version actually works in a multithreaded setting.

## 19. Has AI tells {#story-19}
*#26 on HN · [theconsensus.dev](https://theconsensus.dev/blog/2026/10/14/has-ai-tells.html?focus=1) · 111 points · 97 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50090799)*

A post on The Consensus (theconsensus.dev) by Phil Eaton, published October 14 2026, describing an in-house AI tells detector he built over the past month. It is not a machine-learning model but a set of static rules derived by comparing pre-2023 with post-2023 writing, accounting for yearly drift since; it does not claim an article was AI-written, only that it may remind you of AI writing, and it has occasionally flagged Eaton's own pieces. Articles in the 99th percentile of AI tells are barred from the daily At a Glance section, the feed's sparkle icon and the weekly newsletter, though readers can still filter them out with aitells<90 or the sparkle filter. Eaton cites Cynthia Dunlop's survey showing readers overwhelmingly prefer even bad human writing to good AI writing, and says running the commercial detector Pangram (which The Consensus pays for) on every incoming article is financially impossible.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between Isamu's opening proposal to stop policing provenance and just reward better writing, and a large camp insisting provenance is important metadata in itself — xyzsparetimexyz wants to know someone cared enough to write it, while Zambyte, scubbo, piro0919 and morganf carve out exceptions: technical documentation only needs to be correct, emotional writing needs a human, and non-native English speakers legitimately use AI as a translator. socializer argues that near-zero-cost infinite output is the real problem and will crowd out human expression regardless of quality; calebkaiser counters that the internet already has an infinite supply of writing and curation still filters it, and comex partly concedes while warning about everyone ending up in a personalized bubble. A long subthread debates effort as a proxy: some will not read something written with less effort than they spend reading it, others say low-effort but accurate technical posts are fine and that effort is really a see-saw where the writer's savings become the reader's cost. The author, eatonphil, turns up to answer questions — the feed has roughly 23,000 articles, tagged by technology and semantic category, with marketing content and listicles already filtered out — and confirms this is an in-house labeling tool rather than a product, since Pangram per article would be too expensive; commenters also note the post never links to the detector itself. Several people report that the post's own minor typos and awkwardness were oddly reassuring.

**Worth reading**
- [eatonphil](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50096568): The author explains the scale and mechanics behind the detector: ~23,000 articles in the feed, each tagged with technologies (Postgres, Lean) and semantic categories (compilers, network protocols), with AI-tell checking as the next filter stage after marketing/listicle removal.
- [gfody](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50092098): Argues there is a massive say-do gap — people loudly hate AI writing while a HN AI-detector analysis suggests much AI-assisted writing still gets voted to the front page — and cites makeup-preference studies as a parallel.
- [skissane](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50097467): Reports an obvious bug in Simon Willison's LLM cliche highlighter: it flags markdown bullet lists as an AI tell, because three sentences opening with a dash look like repeated sentence openers.

## 20. Passkeys are not a trap {#story-20}
*#27 on HN · [scotthelme.co.uk](https://scotthelme.co.uk/passkeys-are-not-a-trap/) · 572 points · 725 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50088935)*

Scott Helme (founder of Report URI, creator of WhyNoPasskeys.com) responds to a widely-shared Substack post titled 「Passkeys are a trap. Do not use them.」, which warned that passkey attestation is a 「hidden dial」 that sites could turn up to restrict logins to approved software and OSes. Helme argues attestation as implemented only identifies the authenticator's make and model via its AAGUID plus a vendor certificate chain, says nothing about your OS, browser, extensions or whether your phone is rooted; that the synced passkeys most people use (iCloud Keychain, Google Password Manager, 1Password, Bitwarden) return 「none」 for attestation and consumer sites have been advised not to request it for years; that YubiKeys — which the original post recommended — are themselves the attestation-capable authenticators; and that abandoning phishing-resistant passkeys for TOTP leaves users exposed to Evilginx-style real-time relay phishing, citing Troy Hunt as an example.

**Discussion**: The thread largely rejects the rebuttal on the grounds that it answers the wrong question: as many put it, 「is currently good」 does not follow from 「is not a trap」, and showing attestation is unused today does nothing against a gradual ratchet. Several commenters give concrete counterexamples already in force — Swiss Post's SwissPass whitelists passkey providers and has blocked Bitwarden; a corporate Microsoft account can only use Microsoft Authenticator on an unrooted approved device; Austria's ID-Austria restricts which hardware tokens work; relying parties have threatened to block KeePassXC; and a Pixel/GrapheneOS attested passkey reportedly exposes verifiedBootState. A separate camp insists the standard and protocol are sound and the problem is implementation: several say passkeys on a Mac/iPhone are strictly nicer than passwords, and one IT lead who rolled out YubiKey-based passkeys to blue-collar gas-grid staff reports overwhelming enthusiasm and cost savings versus phishing. There is heavy criticism of the author's incentives (Report URI sells 「Passkeys Protection」, sponsors WhyNoPasskeys.com, and business partner Troy Hunt sits on 1Password's advisory board), and a widespread usability complaint about lock-in and the inability to export passkeys — 1Password only exports via its mobile app, and Bitwarden exports plain JSON where others only do app-to-app transfer. The rough consensus is that passkeys are better than passwords alone but a confusing, fragmented UX mess, with real disagreement over whether the attestation risk is a live threat or a slippery-slope argument.

**Worth reading**
- [apexalpha](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50089791): An IT lead who deployed YubiKey passkeys across a gas-grid company (including blue-collar staff) reports extremely positive feedback — users loved dropping passwords — and says it will cut phishing risk and save money.
- [tennysont](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50090079): Challenges the article's central factual claim, noting that Pixel hardware attestation reports verifiedBootState != Verified on GrapheneOS and can expose device identifiers to relying parties.
- [YakBizzarro](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50091018): Cites SwissPass, a live service from Swiss Post that whitelists WebAuthn providers and has blocked Bitwarden, as proof the lockdown risk exists today rather than in some future.
