## 2. Docker is weird {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [codeandcake.dev](https://codeandcake.dev/posts/2026-10-22-docker-is-weird) · 366 points · 180 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50202681)*

A developer building a hobby container runtime writes up what surprised them about Docker: containers are ordinary processes visible in /proc, not VMs, and 「container」 is really a bundle of namespaces, cgroups, networking tricks, and chroot/pivot_root over an image directory. They argue Go is a poor fit for runc — its multithreaded runtime forces a pre-runtime C shim for single-threaded syscalls, it has no real fork, so runc re-execs /proc/self/exe — and note the daemon historically needs root, with rootless setups still having to reimplement networking in userspace.

**Discussion**: The thread is largely taken over by Docker creator Solomon Hykes, who answers questions about Docker's pre-2013 dotCloud history, the switch from openvz to lxc to an in-house Go libcontainer, the deliberate choice of Go to ship one compiled binary that all language communities could adopt, the failed Moby restructuring, and his current project Dagger; he concedes the article is right that Go fights the kernel interfaces but says it was the correct call at the time. Commenters push back on details: one ex-Docker engineer notes the most important part of the runtime is written in C, and several insist Docker has not required root for about seven years, with rootless operation limited mainly by networking — various point to slirp-style userspace stacks, Podman, and nspawn as alternatives. A recurring counter-argument is that the article misses the genuinely odd design decision, the client/server daemon, which others defend as the only practical way to let unprivileged users start containers and to give Mac and Windows users the same CLI. Language flame wars flare over whether Rust would have been better than Go (most agree Rust was too immature in 2013, and defenders cite Go's safety and contributor friendliness over C), while a smaller camp says nothing on the list is actually weird once you know the history. The mood is mostly nostalgic and grateful, with people recalling early demos, Vagrant/LXC pain, and Docker's role in killing 「works on my machine」.

**Worth reading**
- [shykes](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50203555): Hykes corrects the claim that Docker was a commercialization of LXC: it started on OpenVZ (which predates LXC), switched to the LXC userland tool, then replaced it with the in-house Go libcontainer — and he pushes back on the idea Docker commercialized anything well.
- [shykes](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50204886): Hykes recounts the 2013 PyCon lightning talk where Docker effectively broke out: he expected 10-20 people, was put on the main stage, ran over time, and came back to a crowd at the dotCloud booth asking about a 「side project」 the marketing team had never heard of.
- [stingraycharles](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50203300): A strong counter-argument to the article's list: the truly weird decision was the client/server daemon architecture, which commenters below debate as either a permissions nightmare or the thing that made Docker work identically on Mac, Windows and Linux.

## 3. LLMs are making formal software verification feasible at a much larger scale {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [anthropic.com](https://www.anthropic.com/research/formal-verification) · 286 points · 157 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50202217)*

An Anthropic research post, done with the startup Theorem, reporting that Claude autonomously wrote Rocq proofs that 239 fuzz-test entry points across 123 open-source OSS-Fuzz libraries never crash on any input under 64 MB — proofs over compiled RISC-V assembly, totaling about 180 million lines of Rocq, with a typical proof around 600,000 lines for binaries of median 18,000 instructions. Assumptions are narrow: single-threaded static binaries, fixed memory budget, RISC-V on Linux, with the trusted base limited to the RISC-V/Linux process semantics, the theorem statements, and the Rocq checker. While proving, Claude found and patched bugs fuzzers had missed, including a decompressor counter underflow needing a ~20 MB input with a rare layout, an image-decoder out-of-bounds read requiring 64 MB of input and over 2 GB of memory, and a rediscovery of CVE-2026-56132 in libexpat; Theorem is releasing the pipeline and a Claude skill, and Anthropic notes Opus 5.5 proved half of 36 small verification tasks using ~40% fewer tokens than Opus 5.

**Discussion**: The dominant thread is trust: what exactly does the proof cover, and how do you know the spec matches the code. Several commenters pushed on the theorem statements and the RISC-V/Linux models; one of the paper's authors replied with direct links to the rocq-linux-process-semantics repo, riscv-coq and the oss-verify proof repos. A second cluster argues this is not real formal verification because the spec is just 「this fuzz test never crashes」 — defenders counter that many fuzz tests encode genuine properties (roundtrip, idempotence, differential testing) and that quantifying over all inputs under 64 MB is strictly stronger than any fuzzer, while skeptics note it says nothing about intent, concurrency or multi-threading. The sharpest critical thread quotes the paper's own appendix admitting agent proofs are verbose and may hit a complexity wall without better abstractions, connecting it to claims that LLMs won't generalize past the convex hull of human mathematics; the paper's co-author argues software verification differs from math because the bottleneck is scale, not out-of-distribution generalization. Practitioners weighed in positively: one is translating C and Rust into Lean to verify a Postgres rewrite (about 12% of the code, ~70k lines of C, single-threaded only, per his own admission), another merges SIMD/CUDA optimizations at work backed by Rocq equivalence proofs at the LLVM IR level, and others report Alloy and ESBMC-based workflows. A vocal minority calls it a marketing exercise, invokes the halting problem (quickly corrected — the claim is about specific programs, not arbitrary ones), and argues the industry will spend the gains on shipping more unverified code anyway.

**Worth reading**
- [jaykru](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50202573): Quotes the paper's appendix conceding that agent proofs are verbose and may hit a complexity wall (as in MachCSL), tying it to the 「convex hull」 hypothesis that LLMs stall just beyond human mathematics.
- [malisper](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50203167): A practitioner reports LLM-written Lean proofs of behavioral equivalence for ~12% of a Rust Postgres rewrite (~70k lines of C), already catching cross-platform floating-point differences.
- [sestep](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50203196): First-hand production use: an engineer merges SIMD/CUDA performance PRs confidently because each ships a Rocq proof of behavioral equivalence to the original code, with the proof reading the actual code and fuzzing the semantic model against real hardware.

## 4. This I Believe (1952) {#story-4}

A short 1952 essay by Robert A. Heinlein, hosted on the meditation site Awakin.org, in which he declares belief not in religion but in ordinary people: Father Mike the priest, Doc the veterinarian who gets up at night for a stray cat, townspeople who feed anyone who knocks and says they are hungry, honest craftsmen and — more contestably — 「almost all politicians」. He argues decency is not news, that for every criminal there are 10,000 honest people, and ends with a sweeping claim that humanity will 「survive, endure」, outlast its home planet and spread to the stars. The page is framed with reader reflections and Awakin Circle discussion notes, one of which disputes the dating of the piece.

**Discussion**: The thread is small (14 comments) and splits between admiration and pushback. The sharpest disagreement is over Heinlein himself: one commenter calls him 「extremely progressive on one hand and extremely regressive on the other, sometimes in the span of a single paragraph」, which draws replies arguing that 「regressive」 is a loaded antonym, that he was simply conservative, and that conservatism is a coherent strategy of keeping what works — countered by the argument that conservatism runs on an in-group/out-group divide and shouldn't be sold as a universal ethic. Several readers take issue with the essay's substance rather than its author: one says it is unwise in 2026 to believe almost all politicians are honest, and another notes that many places are nothing like Heinlein's hometown, so the picture isn't universal. A skeptic on the space optimism points out that in 2026 we still can't get more than a fraction of launch mass to actual space, and fuel energy density and throughput remain far short of what the essay's star-faring vision requires. The most practical warning is that the Awakin page's text appears edited, with paragraphs removed, and may be partly machine-generated — so the version on the site may not be Heinlein's own. Two comments supply the emotional core: a Philip K. Dick anecdote about Heinlein quietly loaning him money for the IRS, and the observation that civilizations die when they stop believing this.

**Worth reading**

## 5. The foolishness of "natural language programming" (1978) {#story-5}
*#7 on HN · [cs.utexas.edu](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~EWD/transcriptions/EWD06xx/EWD667.html) · 113 points · 70 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50203161)*

EWD667 is a short 1978 note by Edsger W. Dijkstra, transcribed on the UT Austin CS archive, arguing that letting people instruct machines in their native tongue would not make life easier. His claim is that an interface change is not a fixed division of labour — work added on both sides of the fence can grow, which is why narrow interfaces win — and that formal symbolisms are valuable precisely because their manipulations need satisfy only a few simple rules, ruling out the nonsense natural language makes easy. He ends with the guess that machines programmable in Dutch, English or Swahili would be as damned difficult to make as to use.

**Discussion**: The thread mostly reframes the essay for LLMs, and the sharpest disagreement is over the compiler analogy: nickm12 argues an AI model is not a compiler but a replacement for the developer, because a compiler preserves the semantics of a program while many programs satisfy one natural-language spec; others reply that prompts span abstraction levels, and a prompt like 「declare a boolean array mapping to each UI key」 really is an imperative language with the model as compiler. A sub-thread devolves into pedantry over whether human spec-to-code variation is 「stochastic」, with one commenter insisting that word means something else entirely. Practitioners report that LLM code looks good mainly when you don't understand the codebase, and that writing a formal spec (one names TLA+) is what exposes the decisions the model silently made; another says it is great for demos and templates but that in mature software every detail matters. On the essay itself, several call it a recurring must-read, one argues its premise is dated because nearly every program's requirements are already communicated in natural language, and a counterpoint cites the 600-odd meanings of 「run」. A large, jokey side thread dissects the em dashes in the transcription and the suspicion that the text was AI-written.

**Worth reading**
- [nickm12](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50203552): Argues the popular 「English is the new high-level language」 analogy is flawed: AI models replace software developers, not compilers, because a spec is not a program and many programs satisfy it.
- [uriegas](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50204237): First-hand account that he gets pickier with coding agents the better he understands a codebase, and that formally writing out what he wants (citing Lamport's TLA+) is what forces the real decisions into the open.
- [kevin_thibedeau](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50203637): Corrects the em-dash thread: the typed original used double hyphens that were converted to dashes in transcription, and links the scanned PDF, with follow-ups noting Dijkstra typed until 1979.

## 7. Show HN: OnKey – I'm a bad singer, so I built an app to show where I'm off-key {#story-7}
*#17 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/dsmurrell/onkey) · 18 points · 11 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50195488)*

OnKey is a free, MIT-licensed macOS app by Daniel Murrell that listens while you play a song in Spotify, Apple Music or YouTube, learns the lead melody from a single play, and then scores your live singing against a scrolling pitch track. The analysis chain is UVR's MDX-Net Karaoke 2 for vocal separation, CREPE for pitch tracking and the GAME note model for turning pitch into one bar per syllable; the project grew out of a Claude Code experiment that produced a Python terminal pitch display but could not track the original singer in real time. It requires an Apple Silicon Mac (M1 or later) and macOS 14.2+, never writes the music to disk, and only sends a short Shazam fingerprint, LRCLIB lyric lookups, iTunes search queries and a GitHub update check.

**Discussion**: The thread is small (18 points, 11 comments) and almost entirely positive, with the top exchange being a joke that the demo video uses Red Hot Chili Peppers' Anthony Kiedis as the vocal model — 「like if you had let Shaquille O'Neal teach you how to shoot threes」 — which the commenter quickly walked back and the author cheerfully accepted as accurate. Commenters liked that songs do not have to be pre-programmed or exported to disk, and one asked directly how pitch is detected; the author answered with the concrete stack (CREPE-tiny ported to Rust running on ONNX Runtime for the user's voice, full CREPE for the reference singer, UVR for separation). The author also explains the phone gap: mobile OSes won't let an app hear another app's audio, so the plausible route is a companion app fed pitch tracks learned on the laptop. A commenter links Yale's musiclab relative-pitch quiz as an adjacent self-test, and there were no real counter-arguments — the only noted weaknesses are ones the author had already listed (noisy backing tracks, occasional octave errors, lenient beginner scoring).

**Worth reading**
- [manlymuppet](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50205298): Points out that a singing tutorial demo modelled on Anthony Kiedis is a self-defeating choice of vocal reference, comparing it to Shaquille O'Neal teaching three-point shooting.
- [dsmurrell](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50199323): The author details the actual pitch-detection stack: CREPE-tiny ported to Rust on ONNX Runtime for the singer, full CREPE for the reference vocal, and UVR for vocal separation.
- [dsmurrell](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50205277): Explains why there is no phone version — mobile apps cannot hear other apps' audio — and floats a companion app that would import pitch tracks learned on the Mac.

## 8. A lost Schrödinger manuscript reveals a twist in quantum physics {#story-8}
*#19 on HN · [sciencealert.com](https://www.sciencealert.com/a-lost-schrdinger-manuscript-reveals-a-surprising-twist-in-quantum-physics) · 23 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50192396)*

科学记者报道了物理学史学者、日内瓦大学的 Flavio Del Santo 在奥地利一家图书馆中发现的一份三页手稿：薛定谔写于 1944 年 11 月之后、同样题为「Collapse of Determinism」的未刊回应。它针对 Whittaker 1943 年用光子通过偏振片来「证明」隐变量不可能的论证，指出隐变量不必只有两个取值，可以取 0 到 1 之间的任意值，并给出了测量后隐变量的确定性更新规则，从而在不需要单次随机性的情况下复现量子概率。文章还强调薛定谔本人其实长期持非决定论立场（受 Franz Exner 影响，称经典决定论为「哲学偏见」），手稿未发表可能是因为 Pelzer 在 1944 年已发表类似模型；Del Santo 的论文在 arXiv 上。

**Worth reading**

## 10. Tell HN: I unknowingly broke the PHP RCE embargo. I am sorry. {#story-10}
*#21 on HN · text post · 493 points · 199 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50193416)*

A Hacker News text post by user petecooper apologizing for a post he made announcing that two PHP remote-code-execution flaws, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, were about to land — apparently ahead of a coordinated disclosure schedule, which he says was then changed, adding work for everyone involved. He says he was not a party to any embargo and had been explicitly told by a contact (who had been told the same by her own contact) that no embargo existed; his goal was to warn other people who compile PHP from source on the four-weekly release cycle. The post concedes the 「considerable knock-on effects」 and says he takes full responsibility and has learned his lesson.

**Discussion**: The thread's dominant view is that petecooper did nothing wrong: an embargo binds the parties who agreed to it, and whoever passed him the information without the embargo metadata broke it, not him — cperciva and several others make exactly this point, and petecooper accepts the narrower framing that he is responsible only for posting without knowing the facts. A minority argues he still had a duty of care — that he knew an announcement was scheduled and should have vetted his source or treated an unknown vulnerability as something to report rather than publicize — and a separate subthread dissects the phrase 「I take full responsibility」 as vacuous corporate-speak that devalues the words. On severity the thread splits sharply: tptacek calls the bug a 5-alarm fire exploitable on stock PHP applications and warns that the widely circulated non-working PoCs are irrelevant next to the working private ones, while another commenter insists the direct route needs a supply-chain compromise and the web route needs ASLR disabled, prompting a pointed rebuttal plus a working PoC repository from a commenter who says heap feng shui alone was the hard part. Key factual corrections: an AWS threat-intelligence post reportedly shows php2shell exploitation before petecooper's post, CVE ID allocation is not the same as CVE publication and announcing reserved IDs is not a leak, and petecooper confirms he has no affiliation with Chainguard or Calif. Others warn that a written admission of responsibility is a gift to US litigants and urge a blameless post-mortem instead of an apology.

**Worth reading**
