## 1. AMD Specific Patches for Llama.cpp {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [devnull.land](https://devnull.land/amd-specific-llama-cpp) · 140 points · 44 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50657265)*

A blog post recommending llama-cpp-rdna-boosts, a small patch set re-applied against pinned llama.cpp commits that adds AMD/RDNA-specific kernels. On the author's setup — an AMD R9700 on an eGPU, running ukisai/Swift-1.5-Qwen3.8-27B-GGUF at Q6_K_L with MTP — token generation went from 35 to 55 tok/s and prefill from 700 to 1300 tok/s, using a prebuilt podman image (ghcr.io/stew675/llama-cpp-rdna-boosts:server-rocm-10.1). The post also argues against a wave of new model- or hardware-specific 'vibe-coded' engines such as Strata, Basalt and RustingStrata, claiming Strata boosts speed by changing model behavior — e.g. preferring experts already resident in VRAM over the ones the model would actually select.

**Discussion**: Most of the thread is practitioners trading numbers on cheap AMD hardware: old gfx906 datacenter cards (MI50, Radeon VII) with huge HBM2 bandwidth, 7900 XTX boxes, MI100, and Strix Halo, with several reporting 40-120 tok/s from their own forks after having a frontier model rewrite kernels. The sharp disagreement is about quality: the author and some commenters distrust engines like Strata that trade model fidelity for throughput, while one MI50 owner says quality benchmarks match an unmodified Q8 27B with only slightly worse tool calls, and another notes smaller cards burn cooler. A skeptic asks whether anyone actually verifies output quality rather than just speed, and one gfx1103 user reports the patch approach gave only 15% prefill / 3% token gains and broke unified-memory allocation, forcing a return to Vulkan. A side argument broke out over whether llama.cpp now counts as NVIDIA-owned, since the core team joined Hugging Face and NVIDIA is acquiring Hugging Face — with the original claimant citing a llama.cpp discussion thread and NVIDIA's blog post, and others pointing out the deal has not closed and no control is exercised.

**Worth reading**
- [rao-v](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50657791): A gfx906 owner explains the cottage ecosystem around cheap 32GB HBM2 cards with ~1 TB/s bandwidth, and gives concrete numbers from his own llama-of-vega.cpp fork: a 27B Q8 going from 250 pp / 25 tg to 600 pp / 40 tg before speculative decoding.
- [badsectoracula](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50658612): Argues the whole point of the article is that speed-focused forks like Strata alter how the model computes results and can degrade quality, so he trusts only llama.cpp — and says he would rather wait for these AMD improvements to reach mainline than run the linked fork.
- [0xbadcafebee](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50658667): A contrary first-hand result: on a gfx1103 iGPU the patches yielded only ~15% prefill and 3% token gains and could not use all unified VRAM, so larger models stopped loading and he reverted to the Vulkan backend.

## 2. CTO of 'RansomwareHelp' Indicted for Wire Fraud {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [krebsonsecurity.com](https://krebsonsecurity.com/2026/11/cto-of-ransomwarehelp-indicted-for-wire-fraud/) · 82 points · 39 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50657645)*

A KrebsOnSecurity report on the indictment of Juan Ricardo Palacio Escobar, 52, co-founder and CTO of Florida-based ransomwarehelp.com, which advertised 'Do not pay the ransom!' and claimed proprietary cryptographic tools with 99%+ recovery rates and 80 experts. According to the Rhode Island indictment, the company simply bought decryption keys from the attackers and marked them up: in an FBI sting using a fake victim and a fake ransomware crew, Palacio negotiated a key down from about 0.2 BTC (~$13,000) to roughly $6,000, then billed the fake victim $16,010 including a $2,750 diagnostic fee and a $13,260 'decryption' charge. Security firm Soteria's Paul Ihme describes a 2024 Lockbit case where RansomwareHelp quoted an accounting firm $438,880 for '510 preconfigured forensic servers' and used Anydesk access to delete ransom notes so the victim couldn't negotiate directly; the firm paid a legitimate negotiator half that. The article also notes related DOJ cases against Cybersteward's Edward Dubrovsky, MonsterCloud's Zohar Pinhasi, and negotiator Angelo Martino (70 months).

**Discussion**: The dominant debate is whether this is fraud at all: several commenters argue the victim got working data at an agreed price, that markups are normal, and that Palacio may even have negotiated a discount, so no one was harmed. Others counter that the deception was material and structural — the company advertised that it never pays ransoms, billed for fabricated engineering work and nonexistent forensic servers, and positioned itself as the no-ransom option rather than an honest negotiator, which they call textbook wire fraud. One widely upvoted reply argues he wasn't even helping the client: he quoted a price based on the attackers' initial demand plus his fee, then negotiated downward only to enlarge his own cut. Side threads cover whether paying ransoms is itself illegal (commenters say it generally depends on sanctions and jurisdiction, not a blanket ban), the claim that ransomware-recovery firms paying ransoms behind a veneer of plausible deniability is an open secret, and jokes about the Escobar surname, 'Florida Man', and the FBI's haggling.

**Worth reading**
- [QuantumNomad_](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50658873): Argues the economics were purely extractive: he started from the attackers' opening demand, added his markup on top, then negotiated the ransom down to pocket the difference rather than to save the client money.
- [gtowey](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50658890): Pinpoints the legal theory: billing for engineers and compute capacity that never existed is fraud, while an openly disclosed 'we negotiate and add a markup' model would likely be lawful — but would have had no customers.
- [RobotToaster](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50658541): Says it was an open secret that these ransomware-unlocking firms just pay the ransom while selling victims plausible deniability.

## 3. YouTube Ad Punisher – Auto-reloads YouTube pages when an ad plays {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/antonpictures/youtube-punisher) · 72 points · 109 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50657530)*

A GitHub repo (antonpictures/youtube-punisher, MIT, built 'for George Anton') containing a Manifest V3 Chrome/Brave/Edge extension that watches for YouTube's .ad-showing class and skip-button overlays, saves video.currentTime to sessionStorage, then reloads via location.replace() with &t=Xs so YouTube auto-seeks back to the same spot. After three failed reloads it falls back to redirecting to the ad-free yout-ube.com mirror (with &loop=0); it also logs every ad it catches to a local history exportable as JSON/CSV, and ships an experimental AppleScript watcher for Safari. The README's claim that YouTube serves 'an ad roughly every 60 seconds' was challenged in the thread, and the author conceded it was a bad generalization — ad density is channel-dependent.

**Discussion**: The dominant response is that this solves a problem most commenters no longer have: many report years of ad-free YouTube using uBlock Origin on Firefox plus SponsorBlock, and several say the author's own README exaggerates ad frequency. Others push back that the problem is real but invisible until it hits you — YouTube runs long-term A/B tests, so some accounts get 'Experiencing Interruptions' errors, fake buffering and videos that only play after a reload, while identical setups elsewhere are fine; a few note ad density also varies by location and season (one says 5 ads per 7-8 minutes in December versus 2 per 10 minutes in summer). The bigger argument is economic: some call $9-14/month Premium obviously worth it for the world's largest video service, while others refuse on privacy grounds (phone verification, tracking), object to ads inside videos that Premium doesn't remove, or simply won't pay a company they consider hostile. A small YouTuber says ad revenue meaningfully supplements a low income and asks blockers to consider subscribing; others counter with Patreon/Nebula/Revanced. There's also a recurring warning that popular extensions get bought out and turned into data collectors, and a debate about whether YouTube actively blocks all ad blockers on Chrome post-MV2 (one cites a paper finding MV3 blockers no worse). The thread's rough consensus: if uBlock Origin on Firefox already works for you, this is redundant; it's for locked-down Chrome/Chromebook/Safari environments.

**Worth reading**
- [iantongeorge](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50658748): The author (iantongeorge) concedes the '60-second ad' line was a bad generalization, insists it's a 'punisher' rather than an ad blocker, and says he built it specifically for Chrome/MV3, Chromebooks and Safari where uBlock Origin is detected or unavailable — telling uBO users to keep uBO.
- [bemtevi](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50658925): A self-described small YouTuber (under 10K subscribers) says ad revenue puts food on the table and pays bills, doesn't judge blockers but asks six-figure engineers to consider a subscription, and notes he disables mid-rolls and gambling/weight-loss ads on his own channel.
- [dns_snek](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50658202): Argues YouTube's long-running A/B tests explain the wildly divergent reports: he never saw an ad get past his blocker but got months of fake-buffering and videos refusing to play, and only understood others' complaints once his account was targeted too.

## 7. NP_R ≠ PAR_R {#story-7}
*#8 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/samueleverett01/bss-permanent-separations) · 112 points · 121 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50657059)*

A GitHub repository by Samuel Everett and Deven Mithal presenting new algebraic complexity separations in the Blum–Shub–Smale model, built on the recent VP vs VNP breakthrough: the graph of the real permanent lies in parallel polynomial time PAR_ℝ but outside Σ₂^ℝ, constant-round real-message Arthur–Merlin proofs, Boolean-message interactive proofs (BIP_ℝ), and over ℂ outside NP_ℂ and coNP_ℂ. The authors say the proofs were found and most of the paper written by Claude Fable 5.2 from a conversation with Everett, seeded by earlier work with ChatGPT Astra 6.2, and that the results are formalized in Lean 4 v4.36.0 with Mathlib in about 36,000 lines with no sorry — but only conditionally, since the key border-complexity lower bound for the permanent (Theorem 2.3, from the Anthropic 'VP ≠ VNP over every field' manuscript) is taken as an explicit hypothesis. The work is explicitly un-peer-reviewed and mostly AI-written.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people impressed that consumer-grade models (Fable 5.2, GPT Astra 6.2) produced frontier results and people uneasy about what that means. Several commenters attack the credit and rigor: one argues that dumping Lean files you haven't checked, with an AI-written README, is clout chasing, and another says they generated many 'open problem' proofs with a model but published nothing because they can't vouch for validity. Others push back that Everett is a genuine complexity theorist at UChicago, and that Lean checking plus explicit hypotheses is more than most arXiv preprints offer — though a recurring objection is that only the proofs are formalized while the *definitions* in the theorem statements (the analogue of how riemannZeta is subtly defined via analytic continuation) have no human review, so correctness of the statement itself rests on trust. Lay readers got an explanation of the model: classically PAR equals PSPACE, so this is closer to an NP vs PSPACE separation than to P vs NP, and P_ℝ ⊆ NP_ℝ ⊆ PAR_ℝ. Side threads cover disclosing token/energy cost of such proofs, whether these results have any practical import (most say no, though formalization techniques may help hardware and software design), and a long argument about whether Anthropic's internal models are much stronger than the public ones.

**Worth reading**
- [bananaflag](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50657985): Explains that classically PAR equals PSPACE, so this result is the real-number analogue of NP vs PSPACE rather than P vs NP, which corrects a common misreading in the thread.
- [Jaxan](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50658186): A mathematician's key caveat: Lean verifies the proofs, but the definitions in the theorem statements are not in Mathlib and are not being human-checked, and something like riemannZeta is only right because experts wrote it, which is exactly what you cannot assume for LLM-authored definitions.
- [ibejoeb](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50658626): Counters the 'random crank' framing by noting Sam Everett is a legitimate complexity theory researcher with a UChicago CS page.

## 8. Curiosity Is No Solo Act {#story-8}
*#9 on HN · [thereader.mitpress.mit.edu](https://thereader.mitpress.mit.edu/curiosity-is-no-solo-act/) · 92 points · 22 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50636931)*

The article text itself could not be fetched (HTTP 403), so this is based on the title and on passages commenters quote: curiosity that aims less 'to know' than to 'make connections, build constellations, find links, and follow threads,' and knowledge that is 'procedural' rather than propositional, whose wisdom 'lies in sharing more than stating.'

*Article not read: HTTP 403*

**Discussion**: The thread splits sharply. A large camp found the piece impenetrable and hollow: several readers say it could have been two paragraphs, that the density of social-science jargon masks an empty argument, and one calls it a wordy repackaging of AI talking points (individual thought inferior, surrender your ego, do social math like the Riemann Zulip collaboration).
