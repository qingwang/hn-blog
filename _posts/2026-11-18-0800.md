## 1. AI is killing All About Berlin {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [nicolasbouliane.com](https://nicolasbouliane.com/blog/death-by-ai) · 82 points · 41 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50542705)*

Nicolas Bouliane, who has run the Berlin relocation and bureaucracy guide All About Berlin since 2017 (full-time since 2020), writes that the site has lost 70–75% of its traffic after seven years of growth because Google AI Overviews now answer queries using his content before linking to him. He notes that Berlin's immigration office uses his data about itself and that government offices refer people to his guides, and says he is now spending his days preparing for another income source rather than writing new guides. He cites similar collapses elsewhere (StackOverflow traffic down 99% from its peak) and two recent Google announcements about AI answers replacing search results and carrying ads.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between sympathy and tough-love. Several commenters make the case that Google is simply monetizing a monopoly and that users genuinely prefer a synthesized answer to clicking links of unknown quality and freshness — while others counter that those summaries are frequently sourced from a random Redditor and are often wrong, and that users rarely verify. The sharpest pushback comes from a commenter who notes the site's bank guide leads with affiliate links to banks under scrutiny for anti-money-laundering failures, and argues that a business wholly dependent on a third party was never stable; another says LLMs will eat any information-only site that hasn't anchored an audience. Others describe opting out of creating publicly (one stopped posting useful work entirely), propose paywalling content or joining a lawsuit against Google, and note Google had already pushed organic results below the fold long before AI. One commenter corrects the framing: the post and the Google announcement it cites date from May, and the story was already discussed on HN then.

## 2. Java4Shell: RCE in OpenJDK when processing Base64 {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [calif.io](https://calif.io/java4shell/) · 839 points · 219 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50538240)*

Calif's disclosure of CVE-2026-114938, a heap out-of-bounds write in OpenJDK HotSpot's hand-written x86-64 Base64 intrinsic, rated 9.2 CRITICAL under CVSS 4.0. When the AVX-512 VBMI code path handles a padded tail, it computes a negative output length, turns the store mask into all-ones, and writes 64 bytes starting at offset 48 of a 50-byte buffer; a 65-byte input of 64 'A' characters plus '=' triggers it (128 'A' plus '=' on OpenJDK 17). Calif says AI found it in about an hour and produced a first exploit in two more for under $200 in tokens, and demonstrated pre- and post-auth RCE in unnamed widely deployed Java apps plus sample Struts and Spring Boot applications, with a client-side TLS variant credited to samczsun. The write-up admits there is no universal exploit, each target needs a bespoke heap-grooming chain, and the attack is sensitive to network RTT; it ends with a pitch for 'vibe-uncoding', stripping unused features to shrink attack surface.

**Discussion**: The thread is split between 'patch now' alarm and 'not Log4Shell' realism. Several commenters argue from the exploit's own limitations section that short-term mass exploitation is unlikely: each target needs a custom chain, the remote heap Feng Shui is sensitive to RTT and jitter (Calif rented a VM in the same region to make it work), and the write is largely uncontrolled. Others counter that renting VMs in many regions is cheap, that a handful of clouds host most of the internet, and that an LLM-driven long tail of unpatched high-value Java services — Keycloak and similar off-the-shelf auth servers are named — is the real risk; one commenter openly doubts LLMs can chain exploits at all and calls Calif an OpenAI-funded outfit. Practically minded replies dominate the rest: Semgrep's ievans posted free detection scripts listing the five conditions (HotSpot VM, x86-64 host, version range, avx512_vbmi plus avx512bw with UseAVX >= 3, Base64 intrinsics enabled), and -XX:-UseBASE64Intrinsics is repeatedly recommended over -XX:UseAVX=2 because the latter breaks on non-AVX2 hosts. Commenters correct Oracle's affected-version list (JRE 8u511 is included, not just 17+), clarify that Azul Platform Core uses upstream HotSpot and is affected while closed-source Prime is unverifiable, and note that ARM/Graviton and GraalVM Native Image without -march=native are unaffected. A separate argument covers whether a machine's AVX-512 support can silently change under you via cloud live migration, and there's recurring annoyance at the article's LLM-flavored tone and its 'vibe-uncoding' conclusion.

**Worth reading**
- [ievans](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50538391): Semgrep's ievans links free detection scripts that spell out the exact five conditions for exposure, including how OpenJ9, GCJ and Azul Zing fall outside the HotSpot stub and which JVM flags disable the intrinsic.
- [bikezen](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50539305): Quotes the article's Oracle-supplied vulnerable version list to note that Java 8 (8u511b33-perf) is affected too, contradicting the thread's assumption that this is a Java 17+ problem.
- [_msw_](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50538884): Argues -XX:UseAVX=2 is a poor mitigation because it breaks on machines without AVX2, and recommends -XX:-UseBASE64Intrinsics instead.

Practical reports are mixed: people describe video-call workarounds for lacking a smartphone, failed verification because a street number on an ID differed by one digit from a utility bill, and Login.gov rejecting VoIP and prepaid numbers, while others say Login.gov's in-person post-office verification is easy. **Worth reading**

## 4. Quad9's withdrawal from France: A canary in the coal mine {#story-4}
*#5 on HN · [blog.cloudflare.com](https://blog.cloudflare.com/quad9-withdrawal-from-france/) · 514 points · 160 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50538994)*

A Cloudflare blog post about Swiss non-profit Quad9 pulling its free public DNS resolver (9.9.9.9) out of France after a French court imposed daily financial penalties for refusing to block domains tied to alleged copyright infringement — litigation driven largely by beIN Sports. Cloudflare argues DNS blocking is ineffective (a user can switch resolvers in a minute, or use a raw IP), blunt (it blocks whole domains, not specific files), opaque (sites just fail to load), and dangerous when imposed on global resolvers, since one country's order leaks across borders. ## 5. Google's AI answers reducing search traffic by 40% {#story-5}
*#6 on HN · [revscope.co](https://revscope.co/blog/search-seo-geo-2026/) · 317 points · 249 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50540178)*

A RevScope blog post (written by the HN submitter, slashdev) that combines DataForSEO panel data with a randomized controlled trial to argue that Google's AI Overviews now appear on 61.43% of tracked US keywords (up from 5.93% in September 2024) and cut clicks to outside websites by about 40%. The strongest evidence is a working paper by Saharsh Agarwal and Ananya Sen that hid Overviews from a random subset of US desktop Chrome users in January–February 2026: 0.37 versus 0.62 outbound clicks per search on queries that would show an Overview, searches ending without any outbound click rising from 54% to 73%, ad clicks unchanged, and an ~18.5% loss across all searches. It also reports first-result desktop CTR falling from 17.83% to 7.43% year over year (a drop the report itself declines to attribute), finds little evidence for 'GEO' tactics beyond standard SEO plus third-party presence and quotable pages, and notes AI assistants sent sites just 0.3% of traffic in March while Google disputes that overall traffic is declining.

**Discussion**: The thread splits cleanly. One camp is openly glad: search was already buried in SEO clickbait and content farms, and a direct answer to 'who is on SNL tonight' is the better product — several ex-SEO people say good riddance and argue Google is rationally disrupting itself before OpenAI does. The other camp calls it theft and traffic consolidation: Google scraped the content, built the index, and is now capturing the value while the sites that produce the material die; multiple publishers report losses far worse than 40%, with one site owner citing an 80%+ decline and planning to put his site into maintenance mode. Several commenters push back on the nostalgia by correcting the premise — Google was never good at that SNL query either, and the Huffington Post era already showed the SEO war — while others note that Pew and the cited studies disagree on how much click-through actually remains and that Google's 'quality clicks' claim is unverifiable by publishers. Repeated recommendations for Kagi and DuckDuckGo run alongside skepticism that paying for search can reach critical mass, and a side thread worries that ads, GEO/AEO gaming and model poisoning will simply reproduce the old SEO dynamic inside AI answers. The author participates, conceding the keyword sample skews commercial because it is grown from Google Ads Keyword Planner data.

**Worth reading**
- [nicbou](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50542429): First-hand counterweight to the 40% figure: the operator of allaboutberlin.com says his traffic is down over 80%, that the info is easy to steal and expensive to make, and that he expects to put the site into maintenance mode and find another job.
- [slashdev](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50540575): The article's author explains how the DataForSEO keyword list was built and concedes it is biased toward queries with commercial value, which matters for how far the percentages generalize.
- [jeffbee](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50540661): A blunt counter-argument to the whole framing: Google web search's goal has always been putting answers in front of eyeballs, never driving traffic to sites — ads and Search are separate product areas, and most people refuse to believe it.

## 8. Why is Gemini 4 Carbon taking so long? {#story-8}
*#11 on HN · [kernel.dev](https://kernel.dev/blog/why-is-gemini-4-carbon-taking-so-long/) · 59 points · 31 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50541790)*

A kernel.dev benchmark post that ran Gemini 4 Carbon on private bug-fixing evals and found it took close to an hour on tasks that Opus and Astra finished in minutes. Tracing the runs, the authors attribute only 16–30% of the gap versus Astra (45–49% versus Opus) to slower inference; the rest is trajectory length — roughly 7x the steps, 11x the tool actions, 7x the output tokens and 16x+ the processed input of Astra. Half of Carbon's steps went to work the ticket never asked for (auditing unrelated code, hardening against already-excluded cases), and injecting a 'do you already have enough to make the fix?' check-in every five turns cut one trial from 37 to 28 minutes; mechanical issues (about one tool call per step versus Astra's two-plus, and 39% re-read lines versus 7%) are estimated at roughly a quarter of the excess runtime.

**Discussion**: Nearly everyone agrees Carbon is painfully slow in practice, but the thread splits on why. The top comment argues Google simply has no incentive to optimize serving — inference revenue is a rounding error next to ads, DeepMind is subsidized, and Chinese labs with 10–100x less compute must ship efficient models — while replies call that over-theorizing and say the likelier explanation is worse data, algorithms and training environments (one also blames TPUs). Several practitioners say the slowness buys something: a lighter touch and better architectural judgment than the 'bulldozer' feel of Claude models, with Argon preferred for routine coding and Carbon kept for architecture and deep root-cause work. Others report that Argon equally 'just keeps going', and that Google's own docs describe Carbon as a version that just thinks much longer. Skepticism about a Theo video blaming time-to-first-token was met with a counter-link to CursorBench, where Carbon reportedly ranks top on real-world coding tasks; a side thread complains every lab is now optimizing for coding benchmarks at the expense of everything else.

**Worth reading**
- [tristanj](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50542293): Argues the slowness is structural: Google's revenue comes from ads, not inference, so unlike Anthropic and OpenAI it has no incentive to make serving token-efficient.
- [horacemorace](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50542250): First-hand report that Carbon is very slow but solves problems with a light touch and sensible architecture, a refreshing contrast to Claude's 'bulldozer' approach.
- [teknologist](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50542589): Pushes back on the Theo video with a link to Cursor's evals, where Gemini 4 Carbon comes out top on real-world coding tasks.

## 9. Archive Team is archiving the Apple Support Community (closes November 20, 2026) {#story-9}
*#12 on HN · [wiki.archiveteam.org](https://wiki.archiveteam.org/index.php/Apple_Discussions) · 173 points · 57 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50539747)*

This is a stub project page on the Archive Team wiki for a volunteer crawl of Apple's Support Community, which Apple announced would shut down on 2026-11-20 after giving users roughly ten days' notice on 2026-11-10; the site is already read-only. Crawling covers the many regional variants — discussions.apple.com plus communities.apple.com in fr/de/es/pt and the Chinese, Japanese and Korean discussion sites — with grab jobs feeding a live tracker. The page carries a donation banner and a call for volunteers to the ArchiveTeam Warrior, which the project's IRC channel coordinates.

**Discussion**: The thread is overwhelmingly hostile to the forum itself rather than the archiving: nearly every top-level comment says Apple Discussions pages were useless in search results, dominated by a handful of prolific users posting long copy-pasted answers ending in 'reset PRAM and SMC', 'reformat and reinstall macOS', or 'just update' — and several compare it unfavourably to Microsoft's and Google's support communities, which at least sometimes yield a real fix. A smaller counter-argument holds that preservation isn't about whether the answers were good: archivists keep the whole haystack because they cannot know what future historians will want, and one commenter cites Jason Snell and John Gruber both celebrating the closure while pushing back that unweighted bad answers are still worth preserving when clearly contextualised. A few corrections and practical notes run alongside: Archive Team is not the Internet Archive, Internet Archive pages are barely discoverable via search engines anyway, and volunteers share progress figures that drifted from 2.68M items remaining to about 7M as the queue kept discovering more work. Several people fired up Warrior containers after being told how, and one Apple Silicon user documented a Go/colima bug requiring a GODEBUG flag before the applediscussions project would run. There is rough consensus — even among those sympathetic to archiving — that Apple should have preserved its own community's twenty-odd years of posts instead of leaving it to unpaid volunteers.

**Worth reading**
- [amar1729](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50541148): First-hand gotcha: the Warrior container fails on an M1 Mac mini under colima due to a Go runtime bug (golang/go#77572); adding -e GODEBUG=madvdontneed=0 to docker run makes the applediscussions project work on other Apple machines too.
- [CharlesW](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50540433): Argues archival is a net negative if the pages stay indexed by search engines and LLMs, and quotes Jason Snell on karma-seeking answerers and John Gruber's 'Good riddance', with a follow-up conceding the value of preserved context.
- [maxbond](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50541403): States the archivist's case plainly: even useless answers are valuable metadata for future historians, and preservation tries to keep the entire haystack because you can't predict what will be needed.

## 10. Highly classified US national security communications network goes down {#story-10}
*#13 on HN · [cnn.com](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/17/politics/highly-classified-national-security-communications-network-goes-down) · 560 points · 650 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50532849)*

CNN reports that NSANet — the network the NSA uses to move classified information internally — went down on Tuesday, with the Fort Meade campus escalating from 'code yellow' to a brief 'code green' and then 'code orange', closing it to all but essential personnel. The NSA called it 'temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance' with mission-critical capabilities intact, while Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton said on Fox Business that patching for PHP vulnerabilities discovered last month (which researchers exploited with AI help) left systems that 'did not come back up as expected'. Sources say there is no evidence of a cyberattack, Five Eyes intelligence sharing was unaffected, and a former senior NSA official described NSANet as 'a network of networks' that is alarming but 'likely not crippling'; the story lands amid the FAA's nationwide grounding (traced to one router in Arkansas), a Microsoft Outlook/Teams outage and Chase online-banking complaints.

**Discussion**: The thread splits hard between pattern-matchers and debunkers. One camp lists the week's failures — Outlook, the FAA grounding, banks, Optimizely malware, Apple services, NSANet — and reads them as enemy action, AI-driven attacks, or the payoff of DOGE-era cuts to federal IT; the other camp calls it a frequency illusion, noting Microsoft and Azure have had multi-day outages for years and that the 'bank outage' was largely a DownDetector spike some banks denied. Several commenters who claim government or defense-network experience argue the real problem is that these environments are certified once and never re-certified, so agencies keep ancient firmware — one describes a vendor hunting eBay for old Juniper VPN appliances because that release was the one with the certification, backdoor included. A recurring sub-thread questions why the status of a classified network is public at all; the answer given is that the operational state rarely meets the 'damage to national security' bar for classification, and that once six 'people familiar' have told CNN, officials may as well confirm. Skeptics also flag the sourcing — 'six people familiar with the matter', CNN under Skydance/Ellison ownership — and there is little consensus beyond agreement that the whole week looks bad.

**Worth reading**
- [jiggawatts](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50537903): First-hand account of building 'very high security' government networks: the process is driven by one-time certification rather than security, so a vendor was sourcing old Juniper VPN appliances off eBay because only that firmware release was certified — the same release containing Dual_EC_DRBG.
- [no-dr-onboard](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50534227): Adds architecture context: NSANet carries confidential through TS//SCI, runs on a discrete backbone separate from the public internet, and is deliberately hard to bridge to unclassified networks — so a 'we got pwned' reading is premature.
- [krauses](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50536804): A detailed counter to the outage 'pattern': catalogues Microsoft/Azure/Teams/Exchange outages going back to 2018 and argues the current week only looks unique because of attention and press coverage — 'correlation != ____'.
