## 1. Muse Spark Open {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [research.meta.ai](https://research.meta.ai/blog/muse-spark-open) · 236 points · 146 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50107379)*

Meta 发布了 Muse Spark Open 的权重，这是一个原生多模态模型，总参数 816B、激活参数 42B、上下文窗口 1M token，主打编码与智能体（agentic）任务。Meta 称其为「迄今最强的美国开源权重模型」，并演示了它在两台搭载 M5 Ultra 的 Mac Studio（每台 512GB 统一内存）上通过 MLX 完整运行，还称这是首个能在桌面上运行的同级模型；权重采用附有限制性商业条款的 Muse Model License，托管推理合作方包括 Baseten、Fireworks、Groq、Modal、Nebius、OpenRouter 与 Together，同时 Day-one 支持 SGLang、vLLM、TensorRT-LLM、llama.cpp、Unsloth 等引擎，Helion GPU kernel 在 NVIDIA/AMD 上带来 1.03–3.95× 的 kernel 级加速。

**Discussion**: 讨论很快分成两条主线。一是许可证之争：多位评论者逐条读完后指出，这个许可禁止年营收超过 2000 万美元的公司未经单独协议就把模型作为 API「Model as a Service」转售，因此认为它根本不是「open weight」，只是可下载权重；也有人反驳说商业产品内部使用是被允许的，条款不算太苛刻。二是能力与硬件之争：多位评论者引用 Artificial Analysis 指出该模型智能指数约 39，落后于 GLM 5.3 Flash 与 Qwen3.8 Flash Next，大致与 DeepSeek v4.1 Flash 持平但运行成本高出约 10 倍；实际使用过前代 Muse Spark 1.3 的人说它在工具调用上出人意料地好用、性价比高，但明显低于 Astra、Opus 5.5、Argon 一档。硬件方面，评论者算账称两台 512GB M5 Ultra Mac Studio 至少约 2.8 万–3 万美元，而 512GB 配置当时尚未开售，并质疑 Meta 如何拿到未发布的机器；有人在 Fireworks 上实测觉得比 GLM 5.3 更笨但速度很快，另有人给出约 20 tok/s 的本地推理速度。此外，一条宣称「Muse 极具粘性、Meta 将赢下消费级 AI」的评论遭到集中反驳：有人质疑发布才一两周何谈粘性，有人指出 Agent 记忆确实带来迁移成本但 Muse 本身没有网络效应，也有人认为 Meta 的产品分发能力被低估。

- [GodelNumbering](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50108183): 给出 Artificial Analysis 上的具体排名：智能指数 39，与 DeepSeek v4.1 Flash、Mimo 2.5 Flash 相当，落后 GLM 5.3 Flash 与 Qwen3.8 Flash Next，并附对比链接

## 2. Neural networks are clean {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [already-clean.goodfire.com](https://already-clean.goodfire.com/) · 212 points · 70 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50105647)*

A long essay (roughly 65 minutes, ~20,000 words) by Nick Cammarata — co-author of early mechanistic interpretability work at OpenAI (Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits, Multimodal Neurons) and now at Goodfire — at already-clean.goodfire.com. His claim: the canonical evidence for polysemanticity and superposition was misread. InceptionV1's incoherent late layers were an ordinary architectural failure that later fixes repaired: with no change to size or training data, 99-100% of neurons in its last layer become easily interpretable, and the model stays legible across a 300× size range. In preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B he estimates ~90% of neuron poles have one main job, ~7% two related jobs, and only 2-4% are unexplained (closer to 97% non-arbitrary mixtures counting only strong activations), with similar results in models up to trillions of parameters including Kimi K3; the remaining mess is mostly a motif he calls recursive generalization (e.g. a neuron detecting 「iy」 sprouting to 「diy」 then 「do it yourself」). A companion paper is promised.

**Discussion**: The thread mixes genuine enthusiasm with substantive pushback. Practitioners who work directly on interpretability push back on the essay's neatness: one commenter warns against declaring superposition wrong, notes that many in the field deliberately avoid training a separate SAE/tracer model as another layer of abstraction, and predicts neurons will reorganize as continuously-trained models grow more complex; another argues small models must compress via superposition while 32B models and 10T-parameter models barely need to, citing a claim that a model has memorized essentially every lat/long on Earth. The sharpest critique is that SAEs are unreliable and much mech interp is glorified probing — you only find the Golden Gate Bridge neuron because you already knew to look for it — a point another commenter extends from his own work on explainable neuroimaging models, where explanations can't be verified outside domains we already understand. There is also a running dispute over whether the results prove anything general: a prominent skeptic says that if a model were truly explainable you could engineer its weights by surgery instead of training, while others reply that the essay's claims are narrower and that Rice's theorem arguments about undecidability apply only to the over-broad reading; that same skeptic later concedes the essay does blur post-hoc description with mechanistic explainability. Oddly heavy discussion of the page itself: a laggy embedded 3D city render that pegs CPUs and crashes phones, and JS fingerprinting warnings. Smaller threads debate what 「clean」 actually means (one reply insists neurons encode concepts fractionally, likening a layer to a hologram rather than a list of facts), use the Chinese 羊 to illustrate how small models merge goat/ram into one coarse bucket, and note that architecture matters — a deep transformer is likely more readable than a single huge layer evolved by neuroevolution.

**Worth reading**
- [cscheid](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50108254): Corrects the undecidability argument: Rice's theorem governs the general case, but any single network is one finite formula, so the essay's narrower claim about analyzing specific current LLMs is untouched — the analogy is Halting versus doing useful math in Lean.
- [jsrozner](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50107953): Says sparse autoencoders are unreliable (links a PMLR paper), calls much mech interp glorified probing, and notes you can only locate a Golden Gate Bridge neuron because you already knew to look for it — a new recovered truth couldn't be elicited a priori.
- [jacquesm](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50108074): The long-form skeptic case: if a model could really be explained you could engineer its weights by surgery rather than training, so the essay is speculative absent hard proof of how a specific model reached a specific conclusion.

## 3. Krea 3 – An image model built for editing at scale {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [krea.ai](https://www.krea.ai/krea-3) · 137 points · 61 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50105648)*

Krea 3 is a new frontier image-editing model from Krea, launched in open beta and pitched as built for production: regional edits (draw a box, only that area is redrawn), hex-code color matching scoped to the product, up to three reference images for composing people/products/styles, and native 2K output (2736 × 1536). The marketing page claims 10× faster and 3× cheaper than rivals, with a side-by-side against Nano Banana Pro and GPT Image 2.5 whose illustrative playback times are 8s vs 26.8s and 39.9s per edit, and a precision claim of 0.00% pixels changed outside the edit area for Nano Banana Pro on one test. Distribution goes through fal, Baseten, Comfy, Luminal, Together AI, Envato, Civitai and others; a technical report, LoRA fine-tuning and possible open weights are promised later.

**Discussion**: Krea's Diego (dvrp) opened the thread; he and teammate mattnewton answered pushback directly, saying editing was prioritized over text-to-image because that was the gap in the previous model, and that text-to-image is still first-class and should beat Krea 2 once tuning is final. The tone turned critical in places: commenters posted side-by-side UI-design tests versus GPT-Image-2.5 finding Krea 3 less visually creative but better at small text (one noted specified hex codes leaked into the rendered image), several reported concrete quality failures — an invented suit jacket overdrawn onto a T-shirt graphic versus Gemini, scrambled signage text, odd shadows, visible AI texture — and one accused the site of cherrypicking comparisons and being an ad-for-an-ad. The sharpest exchange was over pricing: a commenter called out a signup flow that offers a discount only after you decline, dvrp defended it as self-defence against rivals' dark patterns, and a serial entrepreneur argued that such A/B-tested dark patterns hide second-order brand damage (citing Adobe), which dvrp conceded was probably right. On open weights, dvrp said it is being discussed internally, confirmed LoRAs should be possible right away (locally or in-app, as with Krea 2) and that the team wants to open-source a version, while a Krea 2 user praised its steerability and benchmark standing among local models. Other threads covered cost versus a ChatGPT subscription, why FLUX and other families are absent from the comparison table, and whether diffusion models make sense for UI mockups at all versus LLMs.

**Worth reading**
- [jjcm](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50106946): Posts concrete Krea 3 vs GPT-Image-2.5 image pairs for UI design work, judging Krea 3 behind on visual creativity but strong on small text, and shows a hex code for a color reference leaking into the banking-site render.
- [boutell](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50107259): First-hand test: given a photo of a woman in a T-shirt and asked to seat her at a fancy bar, Krea 3 invented a suit jacket but drew the shirt graphic straight over it, and the commenter says Gemini did much better.

## 4. Wayland desktop for Linux with skeuomorphic design {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/fyzugx/DEsk) · 344 points · 141 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50103338)*

DEsk 是一个由 fyzugx 在 GitHub 上发布的 Wayland 桌面环境（v0.1.2 beta），完全采用 iOS 6 风格的真实感拟物化设计：窗口是胡桃木桌上的纸页，Dock 是抽屉，应用是书架上的活页夹，通知投进邮箱，系统控制面板则是嵌在桌背的阀门时代机器，还有前门造型的锁屏界面，可从 open-meteo.com 获取天气。房间通过 GPU 光线追踪渲染（GLES 3，CPU 追踪器作为回退方案），每种屏幕尺寸缓存一次；支持七种语言，并会从 Xfce/MATE 生态中挑选现有程序（Xfce Terminal、Firefox ESR、Thunar、Mousepad）来填补终端、浏览器、文件管理器和编辑器的空缺。开发者提醒这是首次发布，预计尚有粗糙之处；内存占用为 200–450 MB。安装脚本涵盖 Debian/Ubuntu、Arch 和 Fedora。

**Discussion**: 评论区严重分裂，但主要涌现出两种立场。一个阵营——呼应 Enlightenment、Compiz 和早期主题化时代的怀旧情绪——赞赏这种多样性和趣味，认为扁平化设计失去了可发现性：隐藏的滚动条、难以看出哪个窗口有焦点、弄不清刚下载的文件去了哪里。反方则反驳说，这些问题是设计不当，而不是缺乏拟物化就能解释的——更好的间距、颜色和焦点提示就能解决，无需任何皮革质感；一位评论者引用了一项贝尔实验室的研究，该研究发现更易记的命令名称对记忆并无实质帮助，并认为拟物化同样没有功能上的优势。许多人认为 DEsk 做得太过火：它像 Microsoft Bob 或 Myst，锁屏是一扇需要走过去、用钥匙打开的门，计算器变成了一张纸——令人愉悦，但对日常使用而言是反生产力的，一位评论者警告一个月后情况会怎样。大量的技术反对意见集中在 AI 生成的 README 上——一位评论者链接到一个 LLM 检测器的分析，认为该文档是 AI 撰写的，另一些人则批评安装脚本长达 583 行以及依赖拼凑一堆来自不同桌面环境的库。实操观察：一位评论者指出该设计高度依赖指针设备，可能更适合 VR；另一位则表示不会用 Wayland，因为 Mathematica 运行不佳。有好几位表示会试用，但会等它退出 beta。

## 5. I Know Why the Mathematicians Weep {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [derekthompson.org](https://www.derekthompson.org/p/i-know-why-the-mathematicians-weep) · 267 points · 411 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50103630)*

A Derek Thompson Substack essay built around a phone call with Cornell mathematician Steven Strogatz, arguing that OpenAI's recent mass publication of hundreds of proofs across number theory, theoretical CS and logic is succeeding at science's 「craft」 axis while severing its 「conversation」 axis. It quotes Fields medalist Hugo Duminil-Copin (「They have annihilated my field of research」) and Strogatz on 「knowledge without understanding」 — Google Translate having allegedly taught linguistics nothing — and on Regina Barzilay, whose mammogram-trained cancer model she admits she does not understand. Thompson's conclusion is that AI may take discovery while humans keep the relational half of science.

**Discussion**: The thread splits sharply on whether the grief is legitimate or ego. One camp says it is straightforward economic dread — commodified labor, status and decades of sunk effort — and calls out the lack of empathy in tech for people whose field is being erased to boost an IPO valuation; another camp (several developers) says engineers already went through this and mathematicians are in the early stages of grief, with some arguing their own job got more interesting rather than less. A recurring substantive objection is framing: many commenters insist current systems only answer questions humans thought worth asking, that problem selection and intuition for new abstractions are untouched, and that models are doing brute-force search over existing math rather than creating it. Others push back that these AI-solved conjectures — Crouzeix's conjecture, the Dinitz-Garg-Goemans counterexample — are real results and that dismissing them as fuzzing is cope. A large subthread argues about whether 「nothing has changed in math」 is defensible at all, and several note Thompson is a known AI optimist whose closing turn to human care is glib under current economic arrangements.

**Worth reading**
- [antics](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50108207): Directly rebuts the article's central 「we don't understand why it works」 claim: we know exactly why LLMs translate well — what's missing is a clean descriptive/specifiable theory, and that distinction underlies the linguist vs computational-linguist split.
- [soVeryTired](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50108245): First-hand detail from someone who looked at the output: the hundreds of OpenAI papers were largely incomprehensible prose, only about 30% carried Lean certificates, and the rough consensus is they are correct — but digesting them will take the math community years.
- [Ciantic](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50106816): Correction on a quoted figure: Duminil-Copin's grief, per his own podcast with Brady Haran, is about how the work will change, not bruised ego or gatekeeping — he is neither pessimistic nor anti-AI.

## 6. Celebrating 70 Years of Fortran {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [fortran-lang.org](https://fortran-lang.org/news/2026/09-15-Fortran-birthday/) · 130 points · 56 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50104726)*

A short news post on fortran-lang.org (by Joe Wallwork of the Institute of Computing for Climate Science) marking roughly seventy years since the language was first published, dated 15 October, and plugging an anniversary event at the Science and Industry Museum in Manchester on 1 December 2026 — a retrospective workshop with a keynote from Damian Rouson of Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, lightning talks, and a forward-looking breakout discussion, with free registration closing 9 November 2026 and a Zoom option. Sponsors include AMD and Dell Technologies, and the evening reception features a demonstration of the 1940s experimental computer 「Baby」. The post's claim is simply that Fortran remains the go-to language for nuclear engineering, weather and climate forecasting and materials science.

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly nostalgia plus a recurring argument about whether Fortran is still a sensible choice: one commenter who maintained a proprietary Fortran 77 jet-engine analysis suite calls it primitive and says C is now better for speed and readability, while others counter that on vector machines Fortran beat C because the absence of pointer aliasing let compilers optimise freely, with real teams doing C for front-end and I/O and Fortran for the compute kernel. Several practitioners defend modern Fortran outright — one who wrote simulation solvers professionally as recently as 2018 says it is very fast and nicer than C for linear algebra, and someone who wrote a niche x-ray scattering/absorption library asks what advantages remain, getting the answer of no aliasing plus continuity with existing numerical libraries (BLAS, LAPACK, MINPACK, FFTPACK). A subthread about Fortran originating the loop variables i, j, k turns into a correction-fest: one claim that I–N were implicitly integers because 「i」 was the first available name is reversed by others (mathematics used i, j for indices long before 1957, with an 1838 citation offered), and the same commenter's framing of early design as 「primitive thinking」 is disputed. On standards, one commenter laments that academia was still forcing Fortran 77 on students as late as 2007 while others list the succession of Fortran 90, 95, 2003, 2008, 2018 and 2023, and a Guinness World Records link notes Fortran is one year older than Lisp and two than COBOL — with a further claim that Lisp began as a proposed Fortran extension.

**Worth reading**
- [jleyank](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50106433): First-hand experience that on vector machines Fortran won because dusty-deck preprocessors existed and the lack of pointer aliasing let compilers optimise without pragmas, so teams split code into C front-end/I-O and Fortran compute.
- [Athas](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50108339): A correction: Fortran had arrays from the start but not first-class array expressions — APL invented array programming, and modern Fortran's support is merely 「serviceable」.
- [BeetleB](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50106896): A practitioner's complaint that as late as 2007 professors forbade anything newer than Fortran 77, despite the newer standards existing.

## 7. Libreboot 26.10 "Ominous Olive" released with UEFI, Chromebook support {#story-7}
*#7 on HN · [libreboot.org](https://libreboot.org/news/libreboot2610.html) · 196 points · 117 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50102923)*

Leah Rowe's release announcement for Libreboot 26.10 (15 October 2026), a free boot-firmware distribution that replaces proprietary BIOS/UEFI. The headline technical change is that MrChromebox's EDK2 UEFI payload is now the default on virtually every 64-bit board (bare-metal GRUB/SeaBIOS deprecated, kept as in-flash EFI builds reachable via ESC), enabling Windows, OpenBSD/FreeBSD UEFI boot, Linux UKIs and systemd-boot; 233 Intel/AMD Chromebooks were imported from the MrChromebox coreboot distro (source-only, no binaries, only Baytrail/Kip tested), AMD Fam15h boards like the KGPE-D16 moved to the 15h.org coreboot fork for reliable raminit, and PCIe rebar is supported but off by default because it breaks libgfxinit output. Rowe labels the new AGENTS.md anti-AI policy the most important change in the release.

**Discussion**: Almost the entire thread is about the AGENTS.md anti-AI policy rather than the EDK2 work. One camp defends it: boot firmware exists to be open and verifiable, so refusing unverifiable machine-generated code is sensible; free software is inherently political (Guix recently banned AI contributions for licensing reasons), and the project's stubbornness is why it exists at all. The other camp calls it dogmatic and the writing emotive — plans to 「de-slop」 and possibly fork GCC, GRUB and U-Boot, plus the claim that U-Boot is 「vibe-coded」, drew heavy pushback, with one commenter noting the kernel now takes AI-assisted patches and that the cited arXiv paper on AI's effect on developer productivity predates late-2026 models. Rowe herself replies in the thread, saying contributions from slop-tainted projects carry legal liability and outlining a plan to reuse lbmk's nuke.list to delete or rewrite affected files and strip git history inside tarballs. On the technical side, commenters point out Chromebooks already ran coreboot via MrChromebox, so Libreboot's value is default blob minimization and ME neutering — and one corrects that modern Libreboot is no longer fully blob-free (Canoeboot is), calling it 「decaf」, a claim another commenter accepts.

**Worth reading**
- [panny](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50105755): Corrects the thread's framing: modern Libreboot is no longer blobless — blobs are minimized and ME is neutered with me_cleaner, and Canoeboot is the actual blob-free successor.
- [cwiggs](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50104073): First-hand report from a Heads contributor who landed the first ChromeOS port using MrChromebox as an upstream dependency (linuxboot/heads PR 2133) and is working on two more, saying adding ports one by one was hard.
- [leahlibre](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50107830): Rowe explains the concrete mechanics of the anti-AI stance: contributing to slop-tainted projects is a legal liability, so she plans to reuse lbmk's nuke.list to delete or rewrite affected files and strip history from tarballs, hard-forking only where slop dominates.

## 18. C++26 Contracts: Introduction to Preconditions and Postconditions {#story-18}
*#19 on HN · [cppstories.com](https://www.cppstories.com/2026/cpp26_contracts/) · 94 points · 90 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50103164)*

A cppstories.com tutorial walking through the C++26 contracts feature added by proposal P2900, which moves requires-clauses into the function signature as pre(...) and post(...) specifiers, plus contract_assert(...) for checks inside a body. It contrasts contracts with assert (declaration-visible, multiple evaluation semantics, covers normal-exit promises, works on all return paths) and shows GCC 16 output — as of October 2026 the only compiler supporting it, via flags such as -fcontract-evaluation-semantic=enforce — including the four semantics (ignore, observe, enforce, and terminate-without-handler). It also flags gotchas: parameters used in postconditions must be const on every declaration, predicates are constified so ++count is rejected, and P2900 explicitly states contracts are not input validation or flow control.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people who see contracts as a strictly better assert (visible in headers, usable by IDEs and tooling, an observe mode that can gather data in production without killing the process, and a step toward Hoare-logic pre/post/invariant documentation) and critics who say the payoff is small. The sharpest criticism is that C++26 contracts cannot promise a check will actually run: ABI/ODR issues and the observe semantic mean contract_assert can be deleted, so naively converting assert(p != nullptr) to contract_assert can introduce UB that plain assert did not have. A second camp objects that the contract-versus-input-validation line is muddy and that it will be abused in practice, with the counter-argument being that any failure that must be reported is a bug in the caller and belongs in ordinary control flow — prefixed by the observation that asserts were never input validation either. Practically, several commenters note you cannot get the optimizer benefit in ignore mode, and that placing predicates in headers forces full type definitions, creating a compile-time problem the answer to which (modules) is untested in practice. On the philosophical side, a subthread laments C++'s ever-growing complexity, defends contracts as formalizing an existing concept, and drifts into Go's double-nil and Design by Contract in Eiffel and Clojure.

**Worth reading**
- [20k](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50107898): Argues contracts are 『ghost code』 that may be deleted by ABI/ODR or the observe mode, so converting assert(p != nullptr) into contract_assert can introduce UB that plain assert did not have — concluding they are less safe than asserts and have no clear use case.
- [nananana9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50104064): Points out the code-organisation catch: putting predicates in headers means you can no longer forward-declare argument types (you need foo.size() visible), while putting them in the .cpp throws away their only advantage over assert.
- [tsukikage](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50105985): Links a Godbolt demo showing there is no way to get the optimization benefit in ignore mode — the only mode that does not emit the runtime checks — comparing the useful unreachable-style codegen against what GCC actually produces.

## 19. Wanted to download an ISO, ended up writing a kernel exploit {#story-19}
*#21 on HN · [blog.dfb.sh](https://blog.dfb.sh/blog/eap770-root-for-two-zeros/) · 302 points · 152 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50070625)*

A write-up about a TP-Link EAP770(EU) v1.0 Wi-Fi 7 access point — Qualcomm IPQ9574, Linux 5.4.164, firmware 1.0.0 Build 20230605 — that silently corrupts TCP downloads over Wi-Fi in the LAN-to-Wi-Fi direction, damaging 64-byte aligned blocks (a cache line) while emitting a freshly recomputed, valid TCP checksum. TP-Link end-of-lifed the device with zero firmware updates, so the author could not flash a fix; the official remedy is two zeros written into /sys/sfe/ppe_rfs_feature to disable Qualcomm's SFE/PPE offload, but the SSH shell runs as uid 1. The root cause is Qualcomm's out-of-tree qca-nss-dp driver recycling packet buffers without invalidating the CPU cache before handing them back to the NIC, so a stale dirty cache line overwrites part of a newly received packet. To write those two bytes he chained CVE-2022-0185 (integer underflow in legacy_parse_param) with pipe-buffer grooming to forge a pipe_buf_operations and overwrite modprobe_path, iterating ~17 hours with AI assistance; he notes at least 13 more in-tree drivers with the same bug class that work on x86 but corrupt data on non-coherent ARM.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between the exploit itself and two side arguments. Many commenters pile on TP-Link (EOL'd-in-the-box hardware, support asking for photos before admitting it, models abandoned within a year), recommending UniFi/Ubiquiti, Mikrotik, OpenWrt-flashable gear, Cudy, GL.iNet or a plain x86 box running OPNSense/pfSense, while others push back that hardware-offload bugs and DMA cache coherency problems are endemic to NICs and SoCs and largely Qualcomm's fault, not TP-Link's. A correction on EU rules: one commenter says the 5-year-update mandate covers only phones and tablets, another says the Cyber Resilience Act applies to every device class. The second big thread is about Claude's cyber guardrails — the author says his Cyber Verification Program tier refuses things as mundane as fixing a SQL injection if the word 「ssh」 appears in test cases, and security engineers add that CVP still blocks reverse-engineering 1994 MS-DOS malware, with several people reporting they switched to DeepSeek, GLM or Kimi to get the work done while others defend Anthropic on ToS grounds. On the technical side, a commenter who hit the same bug on another IPQ SoC says cache coherency is defined at the platform level, so identical ARM cores and identical drivers can corrupt data on one SoC and not another, and another suggests the author should have used the exploit to install a startup script or patch the driver instead of re-running it each boot. The author replied that he has reported the bug to both Qualcomm and TP-Link, and declined the 「just return it」 advice because it was bought in another country.

**Worth reading**
- [reflexe](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50105899): A commenter who worked on a near-identical bug with a different IPQ and PCIe card explains that cache coherency is a platform-level (not core-level) property, so the same driver on the same ARM core can corrupt data on one SoC and be fine on another — and both RX invalidation and TX flush are needed.
- [jameshilliard](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50105292): First-hand account of rooting and live-patching the Quantum Fiber Q1000K ONT's buggy HSGMII/SoC driver with AI assistance, with links to his patches and the airoha SoC vendor report.
- [amluto](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50104344): A kernel-adjacent commenter answers the author's question by noting the DMA API does expose explicit cache-maintenance and coherent-mapping calls so drivers should not be doing arch-specific flushes by hand, and wonders why the exploit wasn't used to gain persistent access or patch the driver.
