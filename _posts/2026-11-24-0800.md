## 2. Stalwart is now a drop-in replacement for Microsoft Exchange {#story-2}
*#4 on HN · [stalw.art](https://stalw.art/blog/microsoft-exchange-replacement/) · 403 points · 207 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50620349)*

Stalwart's release post for version 1.2.0 announces that the open-source (AGPL) Community edition now speaks MAPI over HTTP (EMSMDB and NSPI), Exchange Web Services, Exchange ActiveSync 14.1/16.1 and Autodiscover, so classic Outlook, Outlook for Mac, Apple Mail, Thunderbird/EWS, Evolution and Android mail apps can connect as they would to Exchange — mail, calendars, contacts, tasks and notes in one account. The pitch is aimed at organisations that cannot move mail to Microsoft's cloud for sovereignty reasons, citing Exchange 2016/2019 support ending 14 October 2025, Exchange SE getting no new features, EWS in Exchange Online being switched off from October 2026 and gone by April 2027, and classic Outlook's reported support only to 2029. The Exchange layer is roughly 200,000 lines behind a single `exchange` Cargo feature, off by default, and omits public folders, archive mailboxes, MDM, RPC over HTTP and NTLM/Kerberos; the post explicitly warns that the new Outlook cannot work with any self-hosted server and relays IMAP credentials through Microsoft's cloud.

**Discussion**: The dominant objection is that the client, not the server, is the real lock-in: several commenters argue classic Outlook is being phased out and the new Outlook will never accept a configurable on-prem Exchange account, so a 'drop-in' replacement buys only a couple of years — and one long thread insists the real switching cost is the whole Microsoft 365 bundle (Teams, Office, Intune), not just mail. Defenders answer that the point is precisely to let the server move first while users keep their existing clients, that European public bodies and regulated firms are actively looking to leave, and that JMAP is Stalwart's actual long-term bet. German freelancers raise GoBD's 10-year immutable-archive requirement, which Stalwart's plans do not cover, and several people flag AGPL as a non-starter for large enterprises (others note the paid enterprise licence and that internal use is generally fine). Hands-on users split sharply on quality: some report a stable, low-resource server that replaced Postfix+Dovecot+Rspamd, while others complain the declarative config keeps changing, the SQL schema is poor, the Community edition is full of upsell prompts, and 200k new lines plus an AI-flavoured blog leave them unwilling to trust it with production mail. There is also a side debate on JMAP's adoption — Fastmail created it, Thunderbird never implemented it, and TUI/webmail clients like Meli, Aerc, Bulwark and a commenter's own vibe-coded web client do now.

**Worth reading**
- [traceroute66](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50623955): Argues 'drop-in' is overselling it: replacing Exchange usually means replacing the whole Microsoft 365 bundle (Teams, Office, Intune) that stretched IT teams depend on, and retraining thousands of career Microsoft users is the real cost.
- [kuschku](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50622543): An operator says the protocol parsers are solid but the config system gets redesigned repeatedly, breaking working declarative setups, and the SQL database schema is the worst they have seen.
- [jmoiron](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50622710): First-hand migration report from Postfix + Dovecot + Rspamd with ~15k ham and ~25k spam messages: fewer moving parts, built-in virtual accounts, lower resource use, stable, with only spotty spam detection.

## 5. Modernizing iperf/iperf3 and introducing fireperf {#story-5}
*#8 on HN · [blog.daniel-baumann.ch](https://blog.daniel-baumann.ch/posts/20261122-1.html) · 74 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50604710)*

A Debian packager's blog post (Daniel Baumann) describing a cleanup of the iperf and iperf3 packages: src:iperf now builds iperf-client and iperf-server (with systemd hardening, a dynamic systemd user and ufw integration), src:iperf3 builds iperf3-client and iperf3-server with a systemd service template so several servers can run on different ports (e.g. iperf3-server@5201, @5202) plus a debconf path, and both old packages become transitional ones that clean up leftovers. Packages are in sid with trixie backports at Debian Fast Forward. He also uploaded fireperf, an IPFire-project rewrite that serves many clients on one port, runs many workers and parallel sessions, acts as a client for iperf and iperf3 with autodetection (and as an iperf3 server), and uses less CPU via io_uring and zerocopy — while asserting that iperf2 is generally the better tool and that 'iperf3 is better because 3 > 2' is a misconception.

**Discussion**: The thread is small and mostly positive about the packaging work. The sharpest objection is naming: riobard argues that a from-scratch rewrite should get a new name, since bumping the version of an unrelated codebase (iperf2 → iperf3) creates lasting confusion. zamadatlit welcomes the default systemd service but says a bare 'iperf' command shouldn't assume either version, suggesting autodetection or a forced CLI override instead — adrian_b points out fireperf already does exactly that autodetection, so installing it gets the desired behaviour. A practitioner running 10G+ links says the parallel-clients-on-one-port feature is the real win, because iperf3's one-port-per-client model is painful behind firewalls and NAT and the test tool's single-threaded CPU often saturates before the link does, with a single flow hashing to one LACP member making bonded links look slower than they are — they call io_uring/zerocopy plus multiple workers a genuine fix; they also ask whether fireperf does UDP tests with jitter/loss reporting, which nobody in the thread answered. A final comment asks how fireperf scales against iperf3 with many clients, also unanswered.

**Worth reading**
- [vanshiglobal](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50624530): First-hand 10G+ operator says the shared-port server and lower CPU use fix real problems, and flags single-flow LACP hashing as the reason bonded links under-measure; asks (unanswered) whether fireperf supports UDP jitter/loss tests.
- [riobard](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50624886): Argues projects that rewrite rather than maintain a codebase should pick a new name instead of incrementing the version number, as iperf3 did.
- [adrian_b](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50624697): Corrects the assumption that 'iperf' must pick a version: fireperf's client already autodetects an iperf2 or iperf3 server.

## 6. Lowering Perplexity Without Scaling {#story-6}
*#9 on HN · [micjm.com](https://micjm.com/blog/lowering-perplexity-without-scaling/) · 30 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50607512)*

A blog post arguing that OpenAI's chart of falling perplexity over model generations is far less meaningful than it looks: the benchmark corpus, disclosed only in a footnote, is fixed code, so the drop may partly reflect models being trained on ever more code rather than general capability gains. The author points to an existing paper that trained a 160M model on five mixes varying only the share of code; with 75% code in the corpus, perplexity on non-code text rose dramatically. The post also notes OpenAI's own footnote conceding that code prediction is a poor proxy for general capabilities for GPT-2 and GPT-3, without extending that caveat to newer models.

**Discussion**: Commenters largely accept the critique. One points out a second confound the OpenAI post never addresses: perplexity is inflated at the start of the context window, and since context lengths have grown a lot since GPT-3.5, longer windows give the model more easy tokens to average away that early cost. Another notes the irony that private codebases are among the few large text corpora you can be confident are uncontaminated by internet text, which may explain the choice of benchmark. One commenter connects the finding to anecdotal complaints that models have got worse at creative writing, and asks whether this contradicts the common claim that training mix is not zero-sum. The mild consensus is that any perplexity benchmark needs a fixed dataset and should be reported across several kinds of data, not just one.

**Worth reading**
- [changoplatanero](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50624493): Raises a second unaddressed confound — longer context windows mechanically lower average perplexity by amortizing the costly first few tokens — and notes that private codebases are prized precisely because they are hard to contaminate with internet text.
- [fedpost](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50624914): Ties the code-heavy training mix to reports that models have degraded at creative writing, and questions whether that contradicts the claim that training data mixes are not zero-sum.

## 7. New York Bans Subscription and Post-Purchase Fees on Cars {#story-7}
*#10 on HN · [governor.ny.gov](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/money-your-pockets-governor-hochul-signs-bill-restricting-exploitative-vehicle-subscription) · 267 points · 77 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50622774)*

A press release from Governor Kathy Hochul's office announcing that she signed S.9348A/A.10411A, which bars manufacturers, dealers and third-party providers from charging subscription or post-purchase fees for any vehicle feature that uses pre-installed hardware with no ongoing cost to the seller — heated seats, remote start, built-in navigation and similar. The law takes effect February 27, 2027, and the announcement ties it to Hochul's broader affordability agenda. Crucially, the text exempts navigation updates, infotainment, satellite radio, in-vehicle Wi-Fi, telematics, roadside assistance, software-dependent driver assistance or automation features, and services that rely on cellular or other data networks to keep working.

**Discussion**: The dominant reaction is that the exemption list is the whole ballgame: commenters predict automakers will simply make heated seats 'rely on cellular networks for continued operation' and slide back under the carve-out, while others argue courts read statutes by intent rather than word games and such a design would be struck down. Several note the exemptions also spare Tesla's FSD/Supervised and SiriusXM, since both involve genuine ongoing costs; one commenter says the target is really German automakers that put features like heated seats and CarPlay behind subscriptions. A prominent contrarian thread argues the law exists mainly because dealers don't get a cut of post-purchase fees, and that it's a dealer-protection measure dressed as consumer protection, countered by people pointing out dealers have never taken a cut of, say, smart-doorbell subscriptions. On pricing, some expect sticker prices to rise as subscription revenue disappears; others say transparent up-front pricing is the point, and that only a handful of cars currently carry these fees so margins aren't really at stake. A long tangent about blinding LED headlights and Europe's anti-dazzle adaptive beams runs through the middle of the thread.

**Worth reading**
- [runako](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50623487): Reads the actual senate bill and lists the carve-outs (satellite radio, in-vehicle Wi-Fi, telematics, software-dependent driver assistance), concluding the law targets German-style heated-seat subscriptions specifically, with a link to the text.
- [adamsb6](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50623584): Argues the real motive behind the law is that dealerships don't get a share of post-purchase subscription revenue, so this is dealer protection rather than pure consumer advocacy.
- [swolios](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50624466): Claims the ban is nearly useless because it writes the exemption list into statute, effectively guaranteeing future cars will carry subscriptions for everything it exempts.

## 8. GPUI is getting its own repository {#story-8}
*#11 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/zed-industries/gpui) · 235 points · 89 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50619103)*

Zed Industries has split GPUI — the GPU-accelerated Rust UI framework behind the Zed editor — out of the Zed monorepo into a standalone repo, github.com/zed-industries/gpui. The repo page itself is bare scaffolding (it advertises a framework that 'combines immediate and retained modes,' cross-platform desktop/mobile/web), with no screenshots and little documentation yet; commenters treat the move as a signal that GPUI is being positioned as a general-purpose framework rather than an internal Zed detail.

**Discussion**: The largest thread is a language-aesthetics fight set off by the repo's hello-world example: several long-time Rust users call Rust 'consistently and uniformly ugly' for UI code, while others argue the verbosity is the library's API surface, not the language, and post equivalent hello-worlds in iced and Dioxus Native to prove the point. On the framework question, skeptics ask why GPUI gets attention when egui, iced, Slint and Dioxus exist and are more mature; the most-cited answer is the gpui-kit component library — with a correction that it is a third-party effort originating at Longbridge, not from Zed — plus the trust that comes from shipping in a widely used production app. Accessibility claims are contested in both directions (some say GPUI has native OS accessibility support and egui does not, others the reverse), and a licensing thread notes that Zed's own UI elements under crates/ui carry a GPL license, which one commenter calls near-useless for commercial use and others defend as merely inconvenient for corporations. There is also grumbling that a GUI framework launched with no demo or screenshots, and curiosity/skepticism about the prior history of GPUI being un-vendored before, given the existing community forks (gpui-ce, gpui-unofficial, gpui-fast).

**Worth reading**
- [nicoburns](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50621681): nicoburns ranks the alternatives by crates.io download volume (egui 30.3M total, iced 3.2M, Slint 2.4M, Dioxus Native 181k, Freya, Floem, Vizia) and argues GPUI suffers severe NIH syndrome by rebuilding crates that already exist, though he concedes its component library and code-editing control are genuinely good.
- [Klonoar](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50623915): Klonoar corrects the thread's central premise: the popular gpui-kit components were not built by Zed's GPUI authors but started as a third-party project by Longbridge, which is nonetheless why people are adopting it.
- [throwaway69123](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50621080): throwaway69123 points out that Zed's own UI elements in crates/ui are GPL-licensed, arguing this makes GPUI effectively unusable in commercial products and asking whether the standalone repo will change that.

## 10. Making a dumb TV just smart enough {#story-10}
*#15 on HN · [qsantos.fr](https://qsantos.fr/2026/11/19/making-a-dumb-tv-just-smart-enough/) · 59 points · 18 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50622934)*

A blog post by qsantos describing how he turned a cheap, non-networked TV into a remote-controlled hub using an ESP32-C3 Mini, a mini-breadboard, and an IR transmitter/receiver pair. He used Claude Opus 5.5 to decode the remote's RC-5 IR codes so that single button presses trigger whole routines: enabling the second monitor, launching an app, moving and fullscreening its window. The project grew into colored-button modes for DuckStation, PCSX2, Dolphin, Cemu and ES-DE with two Xbox controllers, plus a Fireplace mode (10-hour videos) and Steam Big Picture; after window-placement problems he moved to multiseat with two separate X sessions and built an admin panel with a virtual remote, virtual gamepad and live screen stream with click-through. Total time was about 10 hours, mostly spent configuring emulators, and he warns Claude needed a tight leash — wrong directions, obvious bugs, and fixes that missed root causes.
