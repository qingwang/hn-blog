## 1. Genie 4 {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [deepmind.google](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/) · 462 points · 271 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50261319)*

Google DeepMind announced Genie 4, a research-preview world model that generates interactive, photorealistic scenes in real time from text prompts and keyboard controls, now with native ambient audio and character dialogue. It uses Gemini models alongside Genie—Gemini Omni for real-world appearance and behavior, Gemini 3.8 Flash as an in-loop planning agent, and Gemini 4 Argon in robotics experiments—and is rolling out to trusted testers and Google AI Ultra subscribers through Project Genie, with SynthID watermarking on outputs. DeepMind acknowledges drift in object permanence and visual detail, weak complex physics, poor legible text, and limited long-session consistency.

**Discussion**: The thread is split between genuine astonishment at the fidelity and a broad refusal to be impressed by a closed demo with no weights, no general access, and almost no technical detail. Many commenters scrutinize the videos and find concrete failures—a castle wall and catapult vanish when the camera turns, a second castle appears, a marble bust floats rather than topples, checker physics are wrong—and argue these are better described as video-game models than world models. Defenders reply that cherry-picked demos still show a real trajectory, that lower barriers could help small game teams, and that imperfect simulation can still be useful for robot training; one commenter cites Waymo’s use of a DeepMind world model, while others insist the sim-to-real gap remains vast. A recurring side thread worries the technology will mainly generate ads and slop, and criticizes Google for announcing without shipping; others answer that frontier technology has always launched narrowly and expensively first.

**Worth reading**
- [overfeed](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50263934): Pushes back on the claim that these simulations cannot train robots by noting that Waymo has already used a DeepMind-provided world model for its robot cars.
- [smusamashah](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50261864): Identifies a concrete failure in the dragon demo: a large section of the castle, including a catapult, disappears when the camera turns back.
- [Bjorkbat](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50264089): Argues these systems are more accurately called video-game models than world models, because their physics is game physics rather than real-world physics.

## 2. Ghostty 1.4.0 {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [ghostty.org](https://ghostty.org/docs/install/release-notes/1-4-0) · 371 points · 119 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50261998)*

Ghostty 1.4.0 的发布说明，这是一个横跨七个月、包含来自 172 位贡献者的 2,889 次提交的大版本。亮点包括：完整的 Kitty 图形协议支持（动画、相对放置，以及宣称是 Kitty 之外首个 100% 覆盖的终端），图像负载的 SIMD 解析速度提升约 16 倍，不可见滚动回溯的透明压缩将物理内存降低 70–90%（因此默认限制从 10 MB 提高到 50 MB），以及在 macOS 上发现内核将 pty 读取上限限制在约 1 KiB 后，将 pty 排空和解析拆分到不同线程，使吞吐量提升 25–55%。GTK 应用获得了一个基于 EGL/DMABUF 的渲染器，修复了在 125%/150% 缩放下文字模糊的问题，而该版本还附带八项安全公告（恶意 Kitty 图形、tmux 控制模式崩溃、粘贴保护和拖动保护中的缺口、通过 OSC 8 链接实现的 macOS 命令点击 RCE），其 CVE 编号仍在等待 GitHub 分配。

**Discussion**: 最大的线索来自 Mitchell Hashimoto 本人，他重新将发布定位为 libghostty 的进展：该库现在为 Apple 平台、Windows、Linux、FreeBSD、Android、WebAssembly 和 freestanding 环境构建并在 CI 中测试，提供 C、Zig、Go、Swift、Python、Rust 和 .NET 的绑定，每终端内存减少约 90%，并运行在 4 核/8 GB 服务器上的 10,000 个活动终端上，同时驱动 JetBrains IDE 的终端（12.5M 用户）——一位 KDE 开发者确认他们正在基于 libghostty-vt 构建新的前端。一个反复出现的「为什么要用 Ghostty 而不是 Konsole/Kitty/WezTerm/Alacritty」子线程中，有人声称 Konsole 在他的机器上更快，对此其他人的反驳是 Konsole 不是 GPU 加速的、默认禁用 OSC 8 链接、缺少 Nerd Font 回退和 Symbols for Legacy Computing 支持，一位开发者在发布 CLI 应用时遇到了全部这些问题。最激烈的分歧是文本配置与 GUI 设置之争：一位用户抱怨没有默认配置文件，也不知道键名（font= 还是 font-family？），因而依赖 web 配置生成器，而其他人则认为驱动良好的零配置文本配置文件是一项特性，而不是缺陷。实际阻碍：Kitty 的 hints/quick-select 模式仍然是那些继续使用 Kitty 或 WezTerm 的人最常提及的缺失功能；一些人遇到 SSH 主机不识别 TERM=xterm-ghostty（建议使用 ghostty +ssh 或 SetEnv TERM=xterm-256color，但一位评论者认为在远程机器上安装 terminfo 是不礼貌的）；以及有人说从源码构建不推荐，因为 Zig 的编译器频繁变动。

**Worth reading**
- [mitchellh](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50262760): Mitchell Hashimoto 详细列出了 libghostty 的进展：平台覆盖范围、语言绑定、每终端内存减少 90%、4 核机器上的 10,000 个终端，以及驱动 JetBrains 的 12.5M 用户终端。
- [reissbaker](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50262841): 一位发布 CLI 应用的开发者举出具体例子说明 Konsole 的不足之处：默认禁用 OSC 8 链接、没有 Nerd Font 回退、缺少 Symbols for Legacy Computing 支持，而 Apple Terminal 则是「终端模拟器中的 IE6」。
- [wpm](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50264158): 对「零配置」说法的尖锐反驳：Ghostty 没有附带默认配置文件，所以用户除了参考文档之外别无他物，只能猜测选项名——这本身就是出现第三方配置生成网站的原因。

## 3. New 14‑inch MacBook Pro with M6 {#story-3}

**Discussion**: The thread is dominated less by the M6 than by M1 owners declaring no reason to upgrade: many running M1 Pro/Max/Air machines after five to six years call them still fast, and several say they are holding out for an M7 Pro/Max, while a few counter that newer macOS releases and AI features make old chips feel slower over time. Apple's memory bandwidth drew the most technical scrutiny — commenters note the base M6's 170GB/s is far below the M5 Max's roughly 600GB/s and that 32GB is the hard ceiling, making it a poor local-LLM machine, with rumors that Apple skipped M6 Pro/Max to concentrate on M7. **Worth reading**

## 4. Classification of Finite Simple Groups Formalized in Lean {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [cfsg.axiommath.ai](https://cfsg.axiommath.ai/) · 97 points · 25 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50261647)*

Axiom Math's project page (cfsg.axiommath.ai) announcing that the Classification of Finite Simple Groups has been formalized in Lean — the comment thread puts the artifact at 43.1M lines of Lean against a ~10,000-page natural-language proof. While formalizing the first-generation proof, the team says it found counterexamples to a closure assertion used in a published route to the Unbalanced Group Theorem, and a linked paper gives an alternative argument for the simple-group case built on Solomon's 1976 theory of maximal 2-components (key step: preservation of simple tops, Theorem 2.3). They are compiling an errata list with group theorists and publishing it on the same site.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between interest in whether frontier models could auto-formalize all of mathematics and whether the result is mathematically useful. jonath_laurent, who says he works on Axiom's MTS, confirms the “swarm of agents over sources” picture — the proof is scattered across 500+ articles and books — and names the real difficulty as reconciling slightly different, mutually incompatible formalizations of the same informal concept; others push back that a pile of Lean is not automatically a reusable library (citing a Xenaproject post on formalizing all of mathematics) and that someone should just try it. On utility, defenders argue the point is certainty: past gaps and omissions in the classification were real, the natural-language proof is enormous, and Lean largely eliminates lingering doubt, with one commenter noting verifying the statement is far easier than verifying the proof (and that a shared kernel bug is the residual assumption). QuesnayJr corrects a claim that a sporadic group was once missed — the famous gap was the quasithin case, a 600-page unpublished manuscript later found incomplete — and says he is surprised only one gap turned up, which others answer by noting decades of human patching and by citing Axiom's own figure of 34 noteworthy deviations from the published record. A minor thread warns the page has a redirect bug that hijacks the back button.

**Worth reading**
- [jonath_laurent](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50262065): A commenter identifying as Axiom MTS explains the method: a swarm of agents over 500+ articles and books, with the hard part being that the same informal concept gets formalized in incompatible ways.
- [QuesnayJr](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50262722): Corrects the thread's folklore — no sporadic group was ever missed; the big gap was the quasithin case, a 600-page unpublished manuscript later found incomplete.
- [bamb008](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50263183): Quotes Axiom's own write-up claiming 34 noteworthy deviations from the published record, the largest concerning the Unbalanced Group Theorem, with a link.

## 6. Python's experimental JIT to be removed {#story-6}
*#7 on HN · [lwn.net](https://lwn.net/SubscriberLink/1102102/0c00e4d550f25f8c/) · 368 points · 193 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50257419)*

An LWN report on the withdrawal of PEP 836, the proposal meant to turn CPython’s experimental copy-and-patch JIT into a supported feature. The JIT landed in CPython 3.13 in 2024 without a standards-track PEP; after the steering council demanded one in June and then raised concerns about maintenance, platform parity, security, compatibility and performance metrics, Savannah Ostrowski withdrew it on October 19, citing limited bandwidth, and removal from the main branch is expected soon.

**Discussion**: Commenters split between seeing the outcome as a failure of process and seeing it as the process working. Critics argue the steering council’s requirements — especially making the JIT team responsible for every affected third-party tool — amounted to a poison pill, and that a performance project cannot be specified like a language feature; defenders say the PEP was vague and overly optimistic, that the measured gain was only around 10%, and that core CPython cannot absorb an experimental compiler without clear maintenance and security commitments. Several point to the funding collapse behind Faster CPython, with Microsoft no longer paying for the team, and to the long graveyard of Python JIT efforts. The technical thread debates whether Python’s dynamic features really make JIT work uniquely hard, citing JavaScript, Ruby and PyPy/GraalPy, while others recommend Cython, Numba, CinderX with Static Python, or rewriting hot paths in Rust. The rough mood is sadness and frustration, but with little expectation that the decision will be reversed.

**Worth reading**
- [btown](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50258282): btown argues that requiring the JIT team to support every piece of Python tooling indefinitely was effectively an impossible condition and signals CPython resting on its laurels.
- [lemagedurage](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50258573): lemagedurage says the modest ~10% speedup makes the council’s caution understandable, especially after the pain of the Python 3 migration.
- [kannanvijayan](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50258052): kannanvijayan argues any successful Python JIT must first build a hidden type model for attribute and method lookup, rather than starting with bytecode specialization and machine-code generation.

## 7. Wikipedia's Cleanup of AI-Generated Content {#story-7}
*#8 on HN · [spectrum.ieee.org](https://spectrum.ieee.org/wikipedia-wikiproject-ai-writing-cleanup) · 371 points · 169 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50257326)*

An IEEE Spectrum Q&A with Eliza Strickland interviewing Ellia Lebleu, a founding member of WikiProject AI Cleanup, the volunteer project started in December 2023 to purge AI-written material that violates Wikipedia policy. Lebleu describes the shift from obvious tells (「stands as a testament」, 「rich cultural heritage」, 「Why is this important?」 sections) to subtler structural markers like the rule of three and negative parallelisms, plus telltale behaviour such as five multi-thousand-word articles in five minutes. Using Pangram detection — which she says produced no false positives on pre-ChatGPT text — she estimates roughly 20% of new articles and drafts were AI-generated in late 2025, falling to 15–16% in early 2026, though she can’t yet say whether policy or better models explain the drop; she also flags fabricated or misused citations as the biggest worry, and mentions a volunteer AI source-checker, the Wikimedia Foundation’s Paste Check tool, and Claude’s occasional refusal to write Wikipedia articles.

**Discussion**: The thread broadly endorses the cleanup, framing AI text as noisy and sameish at scale and warning that Wikipedia, a training source, shouldn’t become model output too — the 「oroborus」 worry, with one commenter linking MIT Technology Review reporting on AI slop corrupting small-language Wikipedias, and several enjoying the analogy that pre-LLM archives are the new low-background steel. A counter-current argues the real problem is quality, not provenance, since humans also fabricate citations — answered by the point that editors’ review bandwidth doesn’t scale with machine-generated volume. On whether LLMs could ever do this well, one camp says models have no provenance and cannot know what supports a claim, while others report LLMs accurately pulling quotes and page numbers from long documents. Several editors chime in with first-hand experience, including one whose manual fix of AI-mangled text was instantly reverted by ClueBot (others offer to help and note a ClueBot revert can itself be reverted), and the thread drifts into familiar side arguments about human editor bias, article bloat, and Grokipedia as an alternative.

**Worth reading**
- [DonaldPShimoda](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50259720): Links a September 2025 MIT Technology Review piece on AI-generated text corrupting the primary training data for under-resourced languages via non-English Wikipedias.
- [liveoneggs](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50260225): First-hand account of fixing AI-generated text with broken formatting and wrong-keyword links, only to have the edit immediately reverted by Wikipedia’s ClueBot, after which they gave up.
- [kzrdude](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50258554): Argues LLMs fundamentally lack provenance — they generate sentences probabilistically and cannot say which work a fact came from — so retrofitted citations stay inaccurate.

## 8. Microsoft and others tell crypto platform not to tokenize their stocks {#story-8}
*#9 on HN · [gizmodo.com](https://gizmodo.com/microsoft-mcdonalds-verizon-and-others-tell-crypto-platform-not-to-tokenize-their-stocks-2000830368) · 69 points · 41 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50232604)*

A Gizmodo report on the wave of public companies formally objecting to OKXICE — a joint venture of crypto exchange OKX and NYSE parent Intercontinental Exchange, co-chaired by former New York governor Andrew Cuomo — which plans 24/7 trading of tokenized versions of 63 U.S.-listed stocks under a temporary SEC exemption. At least 22 companies have objected, including Microsoft, Alphabet, Goldman Sachs, McDonald's, Caterpillar, Qualcomm, Intuit, Wells Fargo, Verizon and CoreWeave; under the SEC's Innovation Exemption a platform need only notify a company, which then has 30 days to object, and silence lets the listing proceed. Companies such as Applied Optoelectronics say the effects on pricing, liquidity and volatility are too uncertain and that investors may think the issuer endorsed the product; the article also notes the exemption lasts five years and that an objection blocks only that one venue, not offshore or alternative structures like Robinhood's.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people who think tokenized equities are just offshore access — commenters cite capital controls, no KYC, a single USDC deposit and global leverage as the real demand — and those who read it as regulatory arbitrage or plain law-evasion, with one arguing it is 「optimised for evading laws」 and another predicting governments will fight it. Several note the product is barely necessary: perpetual futures tracking a stock price already serve crypto-only investors, and one commenter checked the xyz:SP500 contract on Hyperliquid and found funding running roughly 0% to −2% annualized, meaning longs are actually paid slightly, which undercuts the 「you pay interest to hold」 objection. A recurring correction is that stocks are already ledger entries — ownership sits with Cede & Co. and the DTCC's NSCC — so the debate is really about which record-keeper you trust and what legal rights the entry confers. On enforcement, commenters are cynical: a VPN defeats a U.S. ban, though one points out Hyperliquid runs only about five validator nodes, all affiliated with the company, so it is not decentralized in the usual sense. The sharpest exchange is over whether the objecting companies know what they are doing: one calls the objections illiterate given banks have wrapped equities in 144A notes, swaps and warrants for decades, and another replies that Goldman Sachs and this list of issuers are unlikely to be confused about it.

**Worth reading**
- [phyalow](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50263682): Argues the objections are ignorant of decades of bank-issued equity wrappers — 144A/Reg S notes, zero-strike calls, warrants, swaps and fully collateralized performance notes — so nothing here is new.
- [compiler-guy](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50264101): Pushes back with the fuller objection list (Alphabet, Arm, Cisco, Cerebras, Moderna, Rocket Lab and more) and notes it is implausible that Goldman Sachs misunderstands a stock maneuver.
- [jcfrei](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50264128): First-hand check of Hyperliquid's xyz:SP500 perpetual: funding is usually negative, around 0% to −2% annualized, so long holders earn slightly rather than paying carry.

Commenters supply the mechanics the headline leaves out: much of the paper is SOFR-based floating-rate lending from business development companies (BDCs) and private credit funds, written in the cheap-money 2021 vintage on 5–7 year hold assumptions, with venture-backed software and SaaS the single largest sector among issuers.

**Discussion**: The thread is broadly bearish but disagrees on the mechanism. One camp blames the PE playbook itself — buy a niche-dominant software company, raise prices, load on debt — citing JAWS being bought by Vispero and pushed to annual subscriptions, and argues LLMs plus higher rates have wrecked that model and are a bigger cause of the software hiring slowdown than big-tech layoffs. A credit-markets commenter offers a different driver: the Mag7 have issued so much high-grade debt that middle-market borrowers ($15M–$500M revenue) are being crowded out and must pay up regardless of Fed policy. Others correct the common 'fixed vs floating' framing on both sides, noting most BDC/private-credit debt is floating rate and hedged but still has to be rolled every 5–10 years. Two related overhangs come up repeatedly: a write-down wall of unsold portfolio companies carried at stale valuations (one citation: ~32,000 companies, $4T of equity, average hold beyond 8 years), and whether defaults would be inflationary or simply absorbed by asset sales and bankruptcy. Tangential but lively: an AI-driven 'SaaS-pocalypse' in which firms reverse-engineer overpriced vendors, with replies defending SaaS as buying someone else to own uptime, security and scaling.
