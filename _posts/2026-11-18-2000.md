## 1. Unauthenticated remote code execution in Nginx {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [wiz.io](https://www.wiz.io/blog/nginx-http2-rce-vulnerabilities) · 667 points · 265 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50547374)*

Wiz Research, with Ikotas Labs, Metnir, Rapid7 and Xint, disclosed two critical HTTP/2 memory-safety bugs in NGINX — CVE-2026-123633 (a use-after-free when a connection marked reusable is drained while a request is being processed, affecting 1.19.7+) and CVE-2026-123007 (a send_window integer underflow leading to an out-of-bounds copy, present since 1.9.5 in 2015) — both rated CVSS 4.0 9.2 and yielding unauthenticated RCE even on servers serving plain static files. Wiz says it found them with its Atlas harness and validated impact using Gemini 4 Carbon and ChatGPT 5.6 Sol, and argues AI is dissolving the complexity barrier that kept such flaws unexploited. Fixes ship in NGINX OSS 1.30.7 stable / 1.31.9 mainline and NGINX Plus 37.0.7.1 / 37.3.0.1; the interim mitigation is disabling HTTP/2.

**Discussion**: The thread splits into two arguments that keep crossing. One camp says network-facing daemons in C are no longer defensible and pushes Rust servers (xinproxy, Ferron), Caddy or Envoy; the other, including several working sysadmins, counters that new servers are untested, Rust still has unsafe blocks and supply-chain risk, and that Fil-C or formal verification of existing code beats rewriting. A large sub-thread debates whether AI is 'burning down the internet' or finally securing it, with the sharpest point being that the same models make exploit development cheap for attackers who never needed to be smart. Practically, commenters flag that distro packages (Ubuntu, Debian) and the kubernetes ingress-nginx controller lagged the upstream fix, that Docker images trailed by hours, and that the patch commit was public for about a week with 'Reported by Xint.io' in the message. Several people temper the panic by noting HTTP/2 is off by default and used by only ~12% of nginx servers, and citing Rapid7's finding that the underflow needs ~128GB pushed over one connection, so links under ~300 Mbit/s hit nginx's one-hour connection cap before the exploit completes; others reply that lab-only difficulty did not save PHP2Shell in the end.

**Worth reading**
- [smallerize](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50550927): Cites Rapid7's writeup: exploiting the integer underflow requires about 128GB transferred on a single connection, so a link slower than ~300 Mbit/s hits the one-hour connection limit and fails — a significant practical constraint.
- [black3r](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50548510): Notes the fix was already in the public nginx repo a week before disclosure, with 'Reported by Xint.io' in the commit message, and argues F5 should have issued a disable-HTTP/2 PSA in that window.
- [puszczyk](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50549563): Warns that the widely deployed kubernetes ingress-nginx controller is not covered by these patches, so clusters still running it need to mitigate separately.

## 3. Seven new models at scalable price-performance {#story-3}

Microsoft's MAI lab used Ignite 2026 to launch seven models aimed at cheap, high-volume work: MAI Cyber 2 Flash, MAI Code 2 and Code 2 Flash, MAI Image 2.7, MAI Realtime 1 ($0.01 per audio minute), MAI Transcribe 2 (powering Dragon Copilot across 10 million patient encounters a month) and Decision 1.1. The headline claims are MAI Code 2 hitting 98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's coding quality at ~40% of the price and 96% of Claude Opus 5.5's at 5% of the price, plus Cyber 2 Flash matching 'GPT-6 Astra' quality at under 2% of cost per task on CyberGym-E2E. **Discussion**: The thread's dominant reaction is skepticism about the benchmarks: commenters call the Opus 5.5 and Sol comparisons cherry-picked, note the 5%-of-price figure likely compares against Opus at Max reasoning while MAI Code 2 carries a 50% promo discount, and mock the '50x faster and 79x cheaper than GPT-6.1 Sol' line for benchmarking a routing/decision model against a general-purpose one. First-hand reports are mixed but warmer than the benchmarks debate — one commenter testing MAI Code 2 calls it 'quite good' and roughly on par with Opus or Sol, another praises the transcription model as among the best they've used, and a third says MAI Image 2.7 Flash at ~1c/image is cheap enough to be a default. A recurring practical complaint is that it's unclear how to even access the models: they live in GitHub Copilot and a limited Foundry preview, are absent from OpenRouter for now, and several models are missing from Artificial Analysis entirely. The framing consensus is that this is aimed at enterprise buyers and at Anthropic's pricing the day before its IPO, not at individual developers.

## 6. DNS resolvers are the wrong place to fight piracy {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [techstackups.com](https://techstackups.com/articles/quad9-leaves-france-dns-blocking/) · 444 points · 146 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50545958)*

A blog post on techstackups.com arguing that France's use of Article L.333-10 of the sports code to force DNS resolvers to block pirate sports-streaming domains is technically and legally misguided. It traces the timeline — Cisco pulling OpenDNS out of France in June 2024, the March 2026 Paris Court of Appeal upholding DNS blocking (RG 24/09372), a €1,000/day penalty on Quad9 imposed 21 October 2026, and Quad9's announcement that it will stop answering French queries on 24 November 2026 — and argues the three options (global blocking, GeoIP blocking, litigating) all fail. The author is listed as 'Al Smith', and the HN submitter later confirmed the piece was AI-generated as an answer to Quad9 CTO John Todd's request for a sourced, well-reasoned argument to send to policymakers.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between the technical/legal specifics and a long, weary copyright-morality argument. Several commenters push back on the article's framing — one argues the 'DNS is just a pointer' and 'others will do it anyway' defenses are weak and would not be accepted for other crimes, and another says the situation is a private civil suit that Quad9 lost rather than state censorship, though replies note the state wrote the law and supplies the enforcement. The most substantive correction is that Quad9's own malware filtering was used against it in court: having shown it can drop a domain for everyone, it could not claim it was incapable of dropping one for France — which is also why the judge rejected the 'no obligation of means' defense. On the mechanics, commenters note that 'leaving France' does not mean perfect geolocation; it means having no legal nexus or assets in France, with a best-effort IP block and a standing 'we do not serve France' answer, while one commenter points out the Lugano Convention still lets Canal+ ask a Swiss court to enforce. A recurring sideline is that the piece is AI-written, which the submitter conceded.

**Worth reading**
- [ritzaco](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50546451): The submitter, ritzaco, admits the post is an AI summary produced by his 'agent' Al Smith, says he read and vetted it, and was surprised it reached the front page.
- [jeremyjh](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50546790): Explains the apparent paradox: leaving France means removing any legal entity or property French courts can seize, while still geoblocking as an imperfect compliance gesture.
- [TZubiri](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50550703): A sustained counter-argument that the article's 'why DNS blocking doesn't work' section is weak, since 'it's just a pointer' and 'others will do it too' would not be accepted as defenses for other crimes.
