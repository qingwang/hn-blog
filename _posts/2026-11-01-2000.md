## 2. Nurses Sound Alarm as 'Uber for Nursing' Apps Push to Deregulate Healthcare {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [nurse.org](https://nurse.org/news/uber-for-nursing-gig-apps-report/) · 72 points · 30 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50328487)*

A nurse.org write-up of the AI Now Institute's report 「Uber for Nursing Part II」 (April 21, 2026), arguing that gig nursing platforms — ShiftKey, Clipboard Health, CareRev, IntelyCare and others — are lobbying in at least 17 states to exempt themselves from healthcare staffing-agency rules, with eight states having already advanced exemption bills. The ten biggest platforms have raised roughly $1.4B (ShiftKey valued at $2B, Clipboard Health at $1.3B) and Clipboard and ShiftKey alone spent over $1.1M combined on federal lobbying, partly via the Coalition for Workforce Innovation alongside Amazon and Uber. The report describes algorithmic pricing (CareRev's 「Smart Rates」),「Quick Bid」 auctions where the lowest rate wins, contractor classification that strips minimum wage, overtime and workers' comp, and a Roosevelt Institute claim that some platforms use data brokers to offer lower wages to nurses with the most debt; New York bucked the trend in 2025 by classifying the platforms as staffing agencies, after which Clipboard Health, Nursa and KARE left the state.

**Discussion**: The thread mostly ignores the report itself and turns into the usual HN argument about why US healthcare is expensive. One camp — including comments pointing to low hospital margins and to traveling nurses already working this way — says provider pay is the cost driver and asks what should give; another insists the real money is in insurers and hospital-group consolidation, and one commenter cites Commonwealth Fund figures putting physician and nurse salaries at only about 15% of healthcare spending. A European commenter argues the premise is wrong and that hospitals simply shouldn't be run for margin, prompting a reply about comparing wait times, pay and outcomes. On the gig model specifically, commenters draw a straight line from Uber's regulatory arbitrage to nursing, flag the debt-based wage-discrimination claim as the most alarming detail, and correct a top-level assumption that this is a nurse-to-consumer marketplace — it is sold to hospitals and facilities to fill shifts. There is rough consensus that undercutting each other's rates is bad for nurses, with little agreement on the fix.

**Worth reading**
- [5mk](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50329060): Pushes back on the 「providers' salaries are the problem」 framing with a Commonwealth Fund link showing physician and nurse pay is only about 15% of US healthcare spending.
- [codeddesign](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50329094): Corrects the common misreading: these apps are marketed to hospitals to fill shifts, not to patients hiring a nurse directly.
- [gambiting](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50328998): Non-US perspective that a hospital having a 「margin」 at all is the absurd part, since it is funded by taxes and the patient pays nothing.

## 3. Patrick Stewart taught us what good men do with power {#story-3}
*#4 on HN · [vox.com](https://www.vox.com/politics/508526/patrick-stewart-jean-luc-picard-star-trek-liberalism) · 209 points · 144 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50327650)*

A Vox essay by Ian Millhiser, published after Patrick Stewart’s death, arguing that his Jean-Luc Picard was the embodiment of 1990s institutionalist liberalism: a captain commanding a ship with enough firepower to destroy planets who more often used it to deliver vaccines. Millhiser reads episodes like The Measure of a Man (Data’s legal personhood) and The Drumhead (an admiral’s witch hunt) as portraits of trustworthy institutions responsive to reasoned argument, contrasts them with the more cynical Discovery and Star Trek: Picard, and leans on Acemoglu and Robinson’s Why Nations Fail to argue that inclusive institutions still require a strong central state.

**Discussion**: The biggest fight is over credit: many say the utopia belongs to Gene Roddenberry, others to TNG’s writers (who, one commenter notes, wrote Data’s trial episode as a spec script during a writers’ strike), while a third camp insists Stewart’s gravitas is what sold a vision that would otherwise read as camp — an unconvincing actor, they argue, turns morals into caricature. A second cluster disputes whether fiction teaches leadership at all: skeptics say scriptwriters have no leadership credentials and that tidy 45-minute morality plays train people to see the world as good vs. evil, while defenders compare it to Shakespeare, Homer and allegory, and one commenter offers a cautionary tale of a colleague who ran a worksite like “their ship.” A large side thread takes up Data and AI: why audiences root for Data’s personhood but resent today’s models — answers center on Data being a one-off research project rather than a corporate product, and on AI as a proxy for runaway power inequality. Elsewhere people swap essential-episode lists, note that Picard was modeled on Horatio Hornblower, and argue over accountability for those in power.

**Worth reading**
- [Veliladon](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50328179): A close reading of The Measure of a Man: Picard and the judge use he/him for Data while Maddox uses it/them, and Maddox only switches to he after the ruling — a small detail that carries the episode’s argument.
- [R1D3R](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50328502): Corrects the writers’-strike thread: Measure of a Man was a speculative script picked up for production during a strike, and it is one of TNG’s first great episodes.
- [miiiiiike](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50328443): A strong counter-anecdote: the worst person the commenter ever worked with drew their entire leadership style from Star Trek and The West Wing and called the site “their ship” — inspiration, yes; teaching, no.

## 4. Copper – A minimal low-level programming language with OOP and generics {#story-4}
*#5 on HN · [tibleiz.net](https://tibleiz.net/copper/) · 74 points · 37 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50308754)*

这是作者个人开发、以替代 C 为目标的一门小众语言的主页，主打极简：没有头文件、简单的 import、名字不全塞进全局命名空间，语法接近 Ruby，类带隐式 self。页面只有一段简短示例和语言速查表、标准库源码浏览入口，采用 BSD 许可；作者自称语言简单到「像我这样脑力有限的人也能完全理解」。文章几乎没有介绍内存模型、编译目标、FFI 等关键信息，这也成了评论区的焦点。

**Discussion**: 最集中的质疑是文档缺失：多位 commenter 追问内存如何释放、是否有 GC、编译到什么、能否与 C 做 ABI/FFI、以及 Unicode 与 16 位字符串怎么处理。有人翻标准库源码后指出并没有 GC 的痕迹，而是提供分配器接口，free 藏在 std 的 allocator 里，但示例一个都没提，这被认为是最该补的一块。语法上有人认为像 Crystal、Ruby 或 Python/Dart 的混合体（官网自己写的是 Ruby-style syntax，且没有 Python 的缩进敏感），也有人称赞其克制。另一条支线争论自举是否是好主意：有人称自托管是「bootstrapping 的死亡」，也有人认为这种问题被夸大，而有 C 后端是稳妥做法；还有人实测在 Linux 上编译不过。末尾一段讨论则声称人类已不再亲自选语言、语言选择交给 LLM，被多人反驳。

**Worth reading**
- [christophilus](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50328534): 指出 std 里确实有带 free 的分配器，说明要手动调用，但奇怪的是所有示例都没覆盖内存释放。
- [stevefolta](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50328162): 实际动手的反馈：在 Linux 上编译不过，且认为没有字符串插值、没有 kebab-case 命名。
- [bogdanoff_2](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50308755): 发帖人补充作者博客与 changelog 链接，可以看语言设计思路的演变，例如曾加入又移除异常机制。

## 5. Police chief unplugs Flock cameras after company ignores contract cancellation {#story-5}

Police Chief Eric Fadden then had staff disconnect the cameras' power cords. **Discussion**: The dominant thread is anger at Flock — commenters compare it to Uber's ignore-local-regulators playbook, argue a company with no contract shouldn't keep drawing on public infrastructure, and swap ideas for consequences (theft-of-service charges, per-device trespass fines, RICO, small-claims suits, shower caps and spray paint). ## 6. Mathematics has changed. Let's act like it {#story-6}
*#7 on HN · [terrytao.wordpress.com](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/01/mathematics-has-changed-lets-act-like-it/) · 64 points · 76 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50328251)*

A guest post by Frank Calegari on Terence Tao's blog arguing that OpenAI's October 6 release of over 700 research papers was a watershed: AI can now make genuine contributions across many areas of mathematics, even if those papers are badly written, sloppy about attribution, and in some cases wrong. Calegari compares the moment to the classification of finite simple groups — announced by Daniel Gorenstein in 1983 (with the quasithin case finished two decades later by Aschbacher and Smith) — which solved the field's famous problem and thereby cooled interest in it, and he argues mathematicians should respond by valuing problem selection, structural understanding and broad programmes, and by using hiring and journal acceptance rather than rhetoric to reward that. He also warns that the arXiv is filling with rushed AI-assisted papers chasing priority while the Leiden declaration is honoured 「in name more than in spirit」, and tells tenured senior mathematicians that if they fear being scooped by AI they are not being ambitious enough.

**Discussion**: Commenters largely accept the framing that results matter less than digestion — choosing problems, understanding proofs, and drawing others in — with several comparing mathematics to software engineering where writing the code is now the cheap part. The sharpest disagreement is about who gets to play: one commenter notes that solving major problems currently costs roughly $10–22 million in API spend, which would make mathematical credit a function of capital rather than talent, while others argue the shift is simply craft turning into industry, and a counterpoint holds that mathematics has no internal profit motive, so this industrialisation is entirely exogenous and imposed by companies. A long subthread fights over Lean: whether 700 machine-checked proofs should count as solved, whether a Lean bug could be responsible (most think a bug that fakes 700 diverse proofs is implausible, but mis-specified statements and human-written formalisations are the real risk), and whether any of it addresses understanding. Sceptics predict professional mathematicians have little future, and that AI will beat humans at any problem including choosing them, while others insist mathematics is about machinery and comprehension and that no AI has yet produced a genuinely new conjecture or paradigm shift; several defend Calegari against charges that his periodic-table analogy is naive.

**Worth reading**
- [georgemcbay](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50329138): Points out that at roughly $10–22 million in API costs per major result, credit for AI-assisted breakthroughs becomes a function of funding, giving well-capitalised but weaker mathematicians a structural advantage.
- [pfdietz](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50329020): Claims several of the OpenAI proofs have already been accepted and dissected, that nobody seriously doubts the quasi-Riemann Hypothesis result, and that the 11/12-constant proof was an unexpected combination of known techniques that human mathematicians' narrowness had missed for 20 years.
- [QuesnayJr](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50328851): A careful rebuttal: the classification of finite simple groups arguably cuts against Calegari's thesis, and the possibility he ignores is that a machine may also be better at forming conjectures — at which point there is no human role left.

## 7. Proving and improving the Platonic Representation Hypothesis with JEPAs {#story-7}
*#8 on HN · [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39302) · 84 points · 43 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50325454)*

An arXiv paper by Randall Balestriero and Yann LeCun that turns the Platonic Representation Hypothesis into a theorem for Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures. It proves that JEPAs trained separately on different modalities learn the same representation up to a rotation when their positive pairs are good views of one scene, and argues that next-token prediction is a JEPA with bad views, which is why language models align with vision only at scale. Experiments spanning 145k captions and 940M web images produce an alignment scaling law: good views need 42 times fewer samples, a 109M-parameter text JEPA aligns with DINOv2 and CLIP image encoders better than language models up to 72B parameters and better than CLIP's text encoder, and one rotation turns separately trained encoders into a captioner scoring CIDEr .838 on COCO.

**Discussion**: Commenters split between finding the hypothesis profound and finding it a restatement of the obvious: one calls it an optimal dimensionality-reduction claim, another says it matters because it could let smaller models be trained, and a third recasts it as compressed data representations and classifier decision boundaries. Much of the thread drifts into philosophy, asking whether two internally consistent but incompatible world models could exist, with politics, religion and human tribes as examples, and whether objective reality or human judgement decides which representation wins. Technical objections are raised and partly answered: big-endian versus little-endian is not a counterexample because it is equivalent up to rotation or permutation, while a lossy mapping such as INT16 to FP16 is where compatibility gets interesting, and a physicist notes that coordinate choices are task-dependent before conceding that the paper defines its claim narrowly enough. The sharpest critiques concern scope and evidence: one reader suspects the theorem covers one architecture across modalities rather than human minds or arbitrary models, another asks how JEPAs could obtain web-scale good views instead of the unstructured corpus that makes next-token prediction work, and a third reports that every example in the paper's Hugging Face demo is mislabeled. A smaller camp is bullish on JEPAs, citing a text JEPA matching Qwen2.5-3B and 7B with fewer samples, while another subthread complains that theory papers dress standard math in hype terminology.

**Worth reading**
- [suddenlybananas](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50326165): Questions whether the theorem really supports the broad PRH claim, which was meant to cover different architectures and human minds, not just one model family across modalities, and suspects strong assumptions do the work.
- [dominotw](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50328084): Reports that every example in the paper's Hugging Face demo has a wrong caption, such as a cake described as a sheep sitting on it.
- [theptip](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50327939): Asks whether JEPAs can assemble a web-scale supply of good views, since next-token prediction's advantage is mining unstructured web text.

## 8. Bidirectional charging during grid outages {#story-8}
*#9 on HN · [jefftk.com](https://www.jefftk.com/p/bidirectional-charging-during-grid-outages) · 57 points · 71 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50319749)*

Jeff Kaufman argues that using an EV as a solar-charged home backup — the car charging from panels when it is sunny and powering the house when it is not — is nearly impossible to buy today. He walks through the options: GM's V2H bundle is a discharge-only mode that ends when the grid returns, Ford's Charge Station Pro is no longer sold (the F-150 Lightning is discontinued), Kia's Wallbox Quasar 2 cannot charge and discharge at once, and Enphase's bidirectional charger is only 「targeting volume production beginning in Q4 2026」 and depends on automaker enablement. The only working path he finds is Tesla Powershare with a Cybertruck and a UL 1741-listed solar system on the backup panel, so he will likely install a smaller emergency-only battery instead.

**Discussion**: The thread splits away from bidirectional charging into a long batteries-versus-generator argument. One camp (bob1029 and allies) says batteries cannot ride out a week-long hurricane outage that requires continuous 5-ton HVAC — roughly 800kWh, or near half a million dollars — while a low-RPM generator on an automatic transfer switch costs about 15% of that and is 「undefeated from an engineering perspective」; others counter that this describes only the Gulf Coast, and suggest running AC only during solar production, downsizing loads, or using thermal mass. Several practitioners give real numbers: 18kW of solar plus 30kWh of batteries running a 5-ton AC indefinitely while the sun shines, backed by a generator-driven charger; and a winter ice-storm calculation of 600W furnace draw at 30% duty, about 4.3kWh a day, or roughly $10k for a week on cheap 4.8kWh batteries. On the article's actual subject, the consensus is that the blocker is standards and vertical integration: commenters say the microgrid interconnect device has no standardized control protocol, NACS/J3400 omits bidirectional power, and warranties plus California's 70%/75% battery-capacity law make automakers cautious. A UK commenter who spent a year trying to buy a V2H install with effectively unlimited budget found no vendor willing, and reports that exporting to the grid (V2G) is easier to arrange than powering your own house; others correct the article's currency by noting Tesla enabled Powershare V2H on new Model 3 and Model Y from October 1, though it needs a Powerwall 3.

Fastmail 官方博客的一篇科普文，逐层拆解邮件加密：连接层的 TLS 1.3 与 PKI/证书透明度（CT），服务器之间的机会性 TLS（Fastmail 自 2009 年入站、2010 年出站支持）及其可被中间人降级的缺陷，以及用来补漏的 MTA-STS 和 DANE。文章明确表态：Fastmail 当下选择 MTA-STS 而非 DANE，理由是 DNSSEC 运维复杂且故障会长时间滞留缓存（举了 2023 年 10 月 Cloudflare 1.1.1.1 根区签名过期、2023 年 5 月新西兰 .nz 密钥轮换导致数天中断）。后半部分讲静态加密（BitLocker/LUKS）的边界，以及 Proton Mail、Tuta 那类零访问加密在密文仍可被入站截获、元数据与主题不加密、浏览器端解密代码可被篡改等方面的局限。

**Discussion**: 评论整体认可文章质量，但补了不少漏洞和反例。有人指出文章谈 OpenPGP 时漏掉了 Proton 等已支持的 WKD（Web Key Directory），它不依赖「信任网」也能自动加密。Signal 的易用性被反复争论：一方引用 2016 年研究称 28 名计算机系学生中 21 人无法建立并维持安全连接，另一方纠正说该研究考的是能否比对安全号以防中间人、而非日常加密本身，且 Signal 这些年 UX 已大幅改进，不过也有人反驳说把双方安全号合并成一个大数字反而更不直观。真正有杀伤力的是一线数据：一位自托管者说八年间没遇到一台拒绝 STARTTLS 的服务器，只碰到两张主机名写错的证书，说明强制加密已相当可行；另一位则指出校验目标 MX 证书但对方没有 DNSSEC 或 MTA-STS 时，你根本不知道那台 MX 是不是攻击者的。最尖锐的一条实测批评是针对 Fastmail 自己：它对外发邮件并不强制 MTA-STS，入站也只有约 0.2% 的域名启用，并给出了查询结果与探测到的 testing 模式策略文件。另有评论质疑「WhatsApp/Signal 自动加密」的说法过于自信，认为无可复现构建就无法证明实际加密，建议改提 Delta Chat 和 SimpleX。

**Worth reading**
- [8organicbits](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50326925): 实测指出 Fastmail 对外发信不强制 MTA-STS、入站仅约 0.2% 域名启用，并贴出 parquet 查询结果和探测到的测试中策略文件，呼吁官方补文档和仪表盘校验。
- [cubesnooper](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50324017): 自托管者八年强制校验证书并拒绝降级：没有一台服务器拒绝 STARTTLS，只有两张主机名错误的有效证书，其中一张报告后即修复。
- [mmooss](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50326476): 纠正那条 Signal 可用性研究的解读：论文考的是用户能否比对安全号以抵御中间人，而不是能否完成端到端加密，多数用户日常零成本即安全。

## 20. Landline phones will switch off across Britland in 100 days, no extensions {#story-20}
*#30 on HN · [theregister.com](https://www.theregister.com/networks/2026/11/01/landline-phones-will-switch-off-across-britland-in-100-days-no-extensions-openreach/5306673) · 72 points · 50 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50326609)*

The Register reports that Openreach, BT's network arm, has written to communications providers warning that the legacy PSTN phone network will be retired on 31 January 2027 and that the deadline will not slip again. Anything still hanging off copper — plain phones, point-of-sale terminals, fire and security alarms, TeleCare pendants — risks disruption; households without fibre can be moved to SOTAP, where exchange-side kit keeps an old analogue line working as before. Openreach's James Lilley says the easy migrations are done and the remainder are complex critical connections, while analyst Paolo Pescatore and Age UK's Caroline Abrahams complain that public awareness is low and providers must chase vulnerable non-responders themselves.

**Discussion**: The dominant objection is power: POTS is fed from exchange battery banks that run for days, whereas a fibre ONT plus router dies with the mains, and several Britons report that Openreach stopped shipping battery backup with newer ONTs altogether (one Canadian says Bell did the same), while Verizon's alternative is a box of 12 D-cells. A sizeable camp calls this nostalgia: almost nobody owns a corded phone any more, the copper plant is decaying for lack of users, and money is better spent on hardened cell sites, deployable towers and satellite SOS — to which others reply that much of rural California and rural Britain has no mobile coverage at all. A subtler point raised is that POTS carried legally enforceable quality-of-service obligations while VoIP and cellular are 「best effort」, though several commenters correct the implication that 「best effort」 is meaningless — it is a legal standard with teeth, and California's CPUC applies service-quality rules to all of it. One rural commenter deflates the alarm with first-hand detail: they were told they had switched over, but are actually on SOTAP, with analogue phones, CLI and exchange-provided power all still working. A running side thread mocks the headline's 「Britland」, which most readers had never encountered and which some say made them click purely to find out where it was.

**Worth reading**
- [gumby](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50327677): Explains the reliability crux: POTS lines are powered from huge -48V battery banks in the exchange office, whereas a router's backup battery is unnoticed and unserviced, so it is dead within a year — exactly when an emergency call is needed.
- [zh3](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50328724): First-hand report from a rural UK line that was declared 「switched over」 but is actually on SOTAP: analogue handsets, CLI and exchange-fed power still work, so in their frequent power cuts the phone still functions.
- [jordanb](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50327401): Argues the real change is regulatory, not technical: POTS carried legally enforced quality-of-service obligations with penalties, while VoIP and cellular are 「best effort」, giving the phone company a way to shed that liability.
