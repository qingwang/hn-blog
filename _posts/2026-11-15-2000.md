## 1. All AI-generated code should be Open Source {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [jakub.jankiewicz.org](https://jakub.jankiewicz.org/blog/ai-code-open-source/) · 867 points · 459 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50507303)*

A short opinion post by Jakub Jankiewicz arguing that any code produced by AI should be released under an open source license or dedicated to the public domain via CC0. His two arguments: models were trained on scraped open source code and on Stack Overflow/Stack Exchange content under Creative Commons licenses that require attribution, so turning that into closed code is 'license laundering'; and purely AI-generated output has no human author, so under US precedent (the Monkey Selfie case, cited in U.S. Copyright Office guidance) it is not copyrightable — he points to Wikimedia Commons requiring AI images to be tagged public domain, while noting the UK does grant limited protection to computer-generated works. He adds that developers increasingly ship unreviewed model output, which he says should 'definitely' be open source.

**Discussion**: The dominant response is that the argument does not follow: commenters note that most proprietary software is already built on permissive open source, that programmers themselves learned from Stack Overflow and books, and that using a tool has never transferred ownership of the result — several describe long design, testing and real-world-feedback loops around the AI's output as the actual work (anself, tim-projects, saikiran91). The sharpest objection is legal rather than moral: if AI output isn't copyrightable, no license can attach to it, so 'open source' is meaningless and public domain is the real outcome (glitchc, jonathanstrange, ubercow13, InsideOutSanta). Others cite case law directly — Doe v. GitHub (output doesn't inherit the training corpus's license) and Thaler v. Perlmutter (no copyright for pure AI output) — plus a link to the Copyright Office's 2025 report, and one commenter argues the Monkey Selfie precedent actually cuts the other way because the photographer held the rights through 'significant creative contributions'. Practically, people point out that most code is business-specific and secret, so mandatory publication is incoherent, and that trade secret protection survives even without copyright. A minority agrees in spirit and pushes a different demand: open-weight models, a public utility or tax model, or that the prompt is the real source worth publishing.

**Worth reading**
- [blagie](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50507729): An author of widely used AGPL systems says a company that pirated his code, after receiving violation notices, had an LLM clone the system — buggier, but still recognizably his creative content, 'like asking an LLM to rewrite Harry Potter with the same plot but bad dialogue'.
- [jrmg](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50507828): Quotes the U.S. Copyright Office's Copyrightability Report directly: prompts alone don't give sufficient control, pure AI output isn't protected, but human creative selection, arrangement or modification is — the opposite of the post's 'no human, no copyright, therefore open' chain.
- [paulddraper](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50508050): Corrects the article's central legal example: in Naruto v. Slater the monkey lost, and the photographer who set up the shot kept the royalties — closer to a human directing a machine than to nobody owning the output.

## 2. The MacBook Pro's OLED PWM Problem: When a 'Better' Display Becomes Worse to Use {#story-2}
*#3 on HN · [macanorak.com](https://macanorak.com/the-macbook-pros-oled-pwm-problem-when-a-better-display-becomes-worse-to-use/) · 301 points · 169 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50506719)*

A blog post (macanorak.com) arguing that Apple's new tandem-OLED 'PROLED XDR Display' on the MacBook Pro is a comfort regression for flicker-sensitive users. It cites Notebookcheck's lab measurement of 240Hz PWM on the 14-inch model — with flicker detected at 99% brightness and below — against 14,880Hz on the previous M4 Mini-LED MacBook Pro, roughly a 62x drop, even though DC dimming is present at all brightness levels. The author stresses that sensitivity is a spectrum (people who were fine with OLED iPhones are returning these laptops) and asks why display comfort gets less accessibility consideration than features like VoiceOver.

**Discussion**: The biggest thread is a technical dispute over the 240Hz figure itself: legitronics and others read Notebookcheck's oscilloscope traces as showing 480Hz top-to-top, or effectively 960Hz if the two tandem OLED layers pulse 180 degrees out of phase, and jrmg works out that the visible period is ~2ms, matching a 480Hz wave rather than a 4.17ms 240Hz one — with the 240Hz number apparently coming from counting only the deepest dips. Others question the measurement setup entirely (photodetector response, phosphor decay, whether 'DC dimming' is even the right read), and amluto notes that hybrid PWM-plus-DC designs are real in the literature because pixel-to-pixel brightness consistency is poor at low drive current — but that 240Hz fails IEEE 1789 low-risk guidance and floats the idea that this is a firmware mistake ported from LCD backlight logic. A separate camp trades first-hand stories: multiple people returned OLED monitors, Steam Deck OLEDs, iPhone Minis and prior devices for eye strain, headaches and dizziness, while a few report using the new MacBook — or a Pixel 8 at 240Hz — with no issue, and several push back on the article's framing by asking whether this is nocebo or an unmeasured effect, noting there is little peer-reviewed research on flicker above ~100Hz. Side threads cover motion stutter and soap-opera effect on OLED, burn-in longevity, low-CRI flickering LED bulbs, and a clear consensus that Apple shipping 240Hz is uncharacteristically careless — with one commenter pointing out a Lenovo Yoga Pro 9i tandem OLED panel does 1200Hz.

**Worth reading**
- [legitronics](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50507268): legitronics went back to Notebookcheck's raw scope data and argues the real figures are 480Hz worst case and effectively 960Hz, since the two tandem OLED layers appear to pulse 180 degrees out of phase.
- [amluto](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50507253): amluto applies IEEE 1789 guidance, says 240Hz at any meaningful modulation depth fails the low-risk limit, and hypothesizes the behavior is an LCD-backlight-style dimming routine mistakenly applied to an OLED panel.
- [esperent](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50507182): esperent notes the Lenovo Yoga Pro 9i uses a tandem OLED with 1200Hz PWM, evidence that Apple's 240Hz is a design choice rather than a hard limit of the technology.

## 3. 'Birdbrain' Is a Misnomer. Crows Make Decisions Faster Than We Can {#story-3}
*#4 on HN · [nytimes.com](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/15/science/birdbrain-crows-birds-research-decision.html) · 43 points · 25 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50509023)*

A New York Times science story on a study in Science that measured how quickly two carrion crows, Lemmy and Iggy, respond to visual cues. The crows processed and reacted at roughly twice the speed of humans and monkeys, which the researchers describe as the first quantification of the neural speed of avian decision-making. The piece frames this against the old 'birdbrain' insult and a growing body of work showing corvids use tools, count, recognize themselves, plan ahead and show theory of mind.

**Discussion**: Much of the thread is wordplay about whether 'birdbrain' means slow or simply stupid; several native speakers say it means stupid, and some argue folk stereotypes match reality, with crows and ravens clever and chickens dim. That draws a counterpoint that chickens have sophisticated social intelligence, plus links to research on it. The more technical comments argue flight itself demands fast control loops and that avian brains compensate for size with dense, short neurons: one commenter says bird neurons are about half the length of mammal neurons, allowing roughly 8x more per unit volume, while others stress that raw brain size is a poor intelligence measure because corvid neuron density is far higher. A broader theme is that intelligence evolved separately in mammals, corvids and octopuses, with different architectures, which commenters find interesting for AI. There is no strong disagreement on the study itself; the skepticism is aimed at pop-science framing and at size-based comparisons.

**Worth reading**
- [Animats](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50509768): Argues intelligence evolved at least three times — in mammals, corvids and octopuses — and that birds lack a layered cerebral cortex yet still achieve flexible behavior, suggesting more than one architecture for intelligence, with implications for AI.
- [RachelF](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50510061): Offers an engineering explanation: flight requires much tighter control loops than ground movement, and bird neurons are roughly half the length of mammal neurons, allowing about 8x more neurons per volume.
- [fsiefken](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50509614): Pushes back on brain-size comparisons, saying corvid brains pack roughly twice the neurons per volume as human brains and that information-processing capacity, not volume, is the useful measure.

## 4. Things That Turbo Pascal Is Smaller Than (2011) {#story-4}
*#5 on HN · [prog21.dadgum.com](https://prog21.dadgum.com/116.html) · 141 points · 69 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50506894)*

A 2011 post by James Hague noting that the entire Turbo Pascal 3.02 executable for MS-DOS — compiler plus IDE, released September 1986 — was 39,731 bytes, and listing 2011 artifacts that were larger: minified jQuery 1.6 at 90,518 bytes, the yahoo.com home page at 219,583, the white iPhone 4S product image at 190,157, zlib.h from the OS X Lion SDK at 80,504, the OS X touch binary at 44,016, a vim quick-reference PDF, the Erlang R14B02 parser beam at 286,324, and the Wikipedia page for C++. The implicit claim is about how much capability once fit in tens of kilobytes versus how little now fits in hundreds.

**Discussion**: The thread splits three ways. One camp piles on modern bloat, with the touch binary as the favorite example and several people insisting a few kilobytes should suffice; others push back that the comparisons are unfair because 1986 software had to fit a 360KB floppy and a 640KB DOS limit, and one commenter argues GNU coreutils touch is huge because it contains a full natural-language date parser. A second strand is a Borland/Hejlsberg appreciation thread tracing Turbo Pascal through Delphi, J++, C# and TypeScript, including a detailed argument that C# features like events, P/Invoke and WinForms were inherited from J++ after Sun's lawsuit. A third asks why Pascal died: the most-upvoted answers are the lack of a shared evolving standard (Borland and Apple implementations never interoperated), the shift to web and cross-platform development, and the rise of C via Doom; others note Free Pascal/Lazarus, Delphi and Modula-2 are still alive. There is no consensus — the bloat debate ends in accusations of missing the point on both sides.

**Worth reading**
- [zahlman](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50508795): Corrects the 'touch just makes an empty file' claim: GNU coreutils touch is ~95KB because it handles selectable timestamps, no-create mode, symlink dereferencing and --date string parsing, with a reply quoting the man page's free-format date grammar as the real culprit.
- [MichaelRo](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50509320): First-hand account of running a Pascal compiler, editor, source, compiled output and data all inside the 40KB of usable RAM on a ZX Spectrum, with tape storage meaning a bad pointer cost a five-minute reload.
- [probably_wrong](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50507477): Counters the 'bathtub toys vs jetskis' framing by quoting John Carmack from 2005 that something is deeply wrong when text editing on a 3.6GHz processor is not instantaneous, and argues the last 15 years show what happens when nobody cares.

## 6. Emacs Is My Browser {#story-6}
*#7 on HN · [joshblais.com](https://joshblais.com/blog/emacs-as-my-browser/) · 112 points · 62 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50507553)*

A blog post by Josh Blais arguing that Emacs' built-in EWW (Emacs Web Wowser) handles roughly 85-90% of his browsing, including on a phone via Termux, and that its limits — no JavaScript-heavy sites, no social media, no banking or government forms — are the point, since they curb distraction. He describes his keybinds (R for eww-readable, U to yank a URL, & to hand the page to Firefox/Chromium, D to download images), routing PDFs to Emacs, video links to mpv and gopher/gemini to elpher, and using his own Searx instance as the default search engine. The framing is nostalgic and ideological: use the web 'as if it was 1999' and escape the locked-down frontends of platforms like Discord.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people who accept the trade-off and those who want an actual feature argument. Arubis and others stress that static blogs and HTML work fine but the commercial web (Reddit, Google Docs) does not, with several saying they keep an embedded xwidget-webkit around mainly as a PDF and Jupyter viewer; a side argument about whether Firefox really breaks on modern sites ends with some saying Chrome fails too. The sharpest critique, from ohaodha, is that the post's stated benefit is that the method is less useful than a normal browser, dressed up as mindfulness — and that keybindings alone are available in qutebrowser, Nyxt or via extensions; others counter that cutting distraction is itself a feature and that Emacs' real win is a single program with shared buffers, unified bookmarks and elisp integration (v9v cites gptel, keyboard macros and web scraping). A subthread on ergonomics covers evil mode, Spacemacs/Doom, Caps Lock-as-Ctrl, swapping Alt/Ctrl, home-row mods and god-mode, with defenders saying the 'pinky problem' is mostly bad typing habits. Technical objections about Emacs being single-threaded and blocking draw a detailed rebuttal that async I/O is sufficient for networking and that parallelism inside one process is not needed; a user also notes EWW logs into Hacker News but seems limited to one-line comments, and a C-q RET workaround is reported as not working.

**Worth reading**
- [ohaodha](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50508340): An Emacs user's complaint that 'why I do X in an unconventional way' posts too often reveal the benefit is simply that the method is less capable, and this one offers no genuinely new capability over qutebrowser, Nyxt or Emacs keybind extensions.
- [v9v](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50508381): Concrete counter-list of real EWW advantages: side-by-side code and docs buffers, highlight-and-ask with gptel, keyboard macros for scraping, Emacs keybindings everywhere — plus the Glide Firefox fork as a middle path.
- [armitron](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50508531): Rebuts the 'Emacs is single-threaded and blocking' objection, noting adequate async I/O, multi-process setups, native modules over the C API and the new canvas API, and argues Lem will never match Emacs' ecosystem.

## 9. Understanding Positional Embeddings in Modern LLMs {#story-9}
*#11 on HN · [magazine.sebastianraschka.com](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/understanding-positional-embeddings) · 154 points · 10 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50506376)*

A tutorial by Sebastian Raschka on how LLMs encode token position: the historical path from fixed sine/cosine encodings in the 2017 'Attention Is All You Need' transformer and GPT-2's learned absolute position table through to relative methods like RoPE and to architectures that drop explicit encodings (NoPE). It walks through the mechanisms with diagrams, math and a small PyTorch implementation, then surveys which positional choices 2026 open-weight models make — the author says he wrote it as a reference 'building block' for a forthcoming LLM architecture trends piece. Concrete numbers used: GPT-2 has 1,024 learned position vectors, and its position layer is only 0.30% of GPT-2 Medium's weights at that length but would exceed 22% if the context were stretched to ~100k tokens.

**Discussion**: The thread is small and mostly a meta-argument rather than about the article's content. The top commenter, programjames, argues the post explains implementation but not theory, and reframes positional embeddings as group representations — RoPE as one-dimensional irreps of the translation group ℝⁿ with learnable momentum vectors, extensions to the special Euclidean group, and quadtree attention via affine transformations. Several people push back: augment_me says that is pitched above the blog's audience and that NoPE shows the solution can be structural rather than an explicit representation; monocasa and mathisfun123 call the framing an academic dialect misapplied, with mathisfun123 flatly saying the comment is 'wrong' and 'basic Lie theory shoe-horned where it doesn't belong'. nlitened offers the strongest counter-argument, that this is post-factum rationalization — RoPE won empirically against equally arbitrary alternatives, and theory gets written afterwards; mathisfun123 agrees, saying ML is 'spray and pray' with theory superimposed. Nobody disputes Raschka's technical points; there is no consensus, just disagreement over whether the abstract-algebra framing adds understanding or is decoration.

**Worth reading**
- [programjames](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50508006): Lays out the group-theoretic view in detail — RoPE as representations of the translation group, with an extended example for quadtree attention.
- [nlitened](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50509963): Argues the theory is retrofitted: RoPE was adopted because it empirically beat equally arbitrary alternatives, not because the math justified it.
- [augment_me](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50508554): Notes that the target audience of the blog is not abstract algebra grad students, and that NoPE-style structural solutions need no explicit position representation.
