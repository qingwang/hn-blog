Moonshot AI's launch post for Kimi K3.1 Pro, a post-trained version of Kimi K3 with much larger post-training compute, pitched as a 3T-class open-weight model with a 1M-token context window; a Highspeed variant claims 2-3× the speed. **Discussion**: A large share of the thread is about the cyber-capability nerf: defenders argue they need offense-grade models to audit their own code (one engineer says GLM-5.3 is the only model that will do adversarial review without refusing), while others call the removal sensible pacing that avoids regulation, and cynics read it as a PR shield or a signal that the un-nerfed checkpoint exists for governments. ## 4. UUIDv7 doesn't have the properties you think it does {#story-4}
*#5 on HN · [alexsci.com](https://alexsci.com/blog/uuidv7-is-weird/) · 66 points · 47 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50211746)*

A blog post by alexsci arguing that UUIDv7's advertised benefits — an embedded creation timestamp and monotonic ordering — are not guaranteed by RFC 9562, which explicitly says implementations MAY alter or advance the timestamp and makes no promise about clock accuracy. It shows CPython's uuid7 implementation, which pushes the timestamp forward to preserve ordering, can run away arbitrarily far ahead of real time if the clock jumps backwards while IDs are generated faster than one per millisecond, and notes that Postgres's uuid_extract_timestamp warns the extracted time isn't exact and that no implementation it surveyed persists the last-used timestamp across restarts. The author concludes only uniqueness and collision resistance are strictly promised, warns the timestamp leaks creation time in URLs, and gives a checklist of questions to ask when picking an implementation.

**Discussion**: Most commenters push back on the framing: they say the point of UUIDv7 was never extracting creation time or global monotonicity but index locality — keeping temporally adjacent rows on the same B-tree pages so inserts don't scatter and cause page splits and fragmentation, which matters once an index no longer fits in RAM. Several report first-hand use, including one company that hit MySQL performance problems with random IDs around 2015 and wrote its own UUIDv7-style generator still in use, and another citing tables with billions of rows. A counter-current concedes the article is technically right and that 「kinda sorta ordered」 claims made without asterisks lead people into bad designs for things like cursors, streaming and chat ordering, while others call it nitpicking and note nobody sane decodes the timestamp to save a column. Side debates cover whether plain serial/auto-increment keys are better (hot spots in distributed databases, versus data locality for the vast majority running one centralized database) and reminders that clock synchronization was shown impossible long ago, with Spanner's atomic clocks cited as the expensive exception. Rough consensus: use UUIDv7 for uniqueness plus write clustering, but don't treat its timestamp as truth or its ordering as strict.

**Worth reading**
- [pkulak](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50213562): Says the index-locality benefit is 99% of the value and is not theoretical — his company hit performance problems with fully random IDs in MySQL back in 2015 and rolled its own UUIDv7 generator that is still in production.
- [nitwit005](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50213206): Quotes the CPython source showing uuid7 simply sets timestamp_ms = _last_timestamp_v7 + 1 when the clock goes backwards, linking the commit — exactly the behavior you want for ordering, but only on a single non-restarting process.
- [roughly](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50213203): Argues the real issue is documentation: UUIDv7 delivers only roughly time-ordered IDs, which is fine for most uses but disqualifying for forensic accounting or banking risk systems, and marketing that omits the asterisk invites bad design decisions.

## 5. The Real Kevin Bacon {#story-5}
*#7 on HN · [pudding.cool](https://pudding.cool/2026/10/kevin-bacon/) · 135 points · 39 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50169499)*

An interactive Pudding.cool data story that computes a 「remoteness」 score — average degrees of separation via shared film credits — across a dataset of 169,000 actors, using roughly the top 10,000 films. It finds Kevin Bacon is not the center of Hollywood at all: he ranks 175th with a remoteness of 2.28, while Samuel L. Jackson is #1 at 2.09, followed by Willem Dafoe (2.13) and Robert De Niro (2.14); women hold only 16 of the top 100 spots, with Nicole Kidman highest at #21. A slider runs the ranking back to 1980 (Bacon peaked at #108 in 1996, and John Candy was #2 in 1993), and a 10,000-run Monte Carlo simulation of film count and co-star prestige picks Chloë Grace Moretz as the most likely Gen Z successor, winning just over 10% of runs with a median remoteness of 2.19 and 66 projected films against Jackson's 116.

**Discussion**: The dominant objection is prior art: commenters point to the much older Oracle of Bacon project, which uses the full IMDb dataset rather than a subset and puts Eric Roberts first and Samuel L. Jackson only fifth — a discrepancy nobody in the thread resolves, though others note the article never mentions Roberts. Several people push back on the piece's framing of connectedness as a fun metric, arguing that once almost everyone is 2.6 moves apart the number stops meaning much, and one commenter flags that Frank Welker's high historical ranking suggests voice acting (cartoon animals, often without lines) should be treated as a separate variant. Others explain the mechanism rather than dispute it: Marvel, Star Wars, Fast and Furious and Kevin Smith's films act as giant hubs (Harrison Ford, Vin Diesel, Bruce Willis, Natalie Portman) that collapse distances, and being in a Marvel movie is close to a cheat code. A large secondary thread is about the format — readers got stuck in tap-to-continue loops on iOS Safari and Firefox and found the navigation painful, while defenders note Pudding.cool has used this format since long before LLMs and that there are 19 screens of content. Smaller notes: surprise at Dafoe's ranking (resolved by listing his genre-hopping filmography), curiosity about a Bollywood equivalent (Shah Rukh Khan, formerly Amitabh Bachchan), and a question about whether deceased actors like Christopher Lee are excluded.

**Worth reading**
- [ameliaquining](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50213440): Cites the older Oracle of Bacon project, which uses full IMDb data and produces different results — Eric Roberts first, Samuel L. Jackson fifth — directly undercutting the article's headline ranking.
- [bena](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50213163): Explains the structural reason Jackson and others are central: Marvel, Star Wars, Fast and Furious and Kevin Smith's View Askewniverse act as hubs, with Ford, Diesel, Portman and Willis linking huge numbers of actors in a few steps.
- [quickthrowman](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50211949): Reports the interactive is broken on iOS Safari, looping forever, echoed by another commenter on Firefox — practical warning before you click.

## 7. Canadians are running out of time to oppose Bill C-22 {#story-7}
*#9 on HN · [eff.org](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/10/canadians-are-running-out-time-oppose-dangerous-c-22-surveillance-bill) · 168 points · 66 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50202114)*

An EFF action alert on Bill C-22, the Lawful Access Act, which has already passed the House of Commons and is now before the Canadian Senate. EFF argues the bill would let the Minister of Public Safety order telecoms, messaging apps and other services to build surveillance backdoors so long as they don't create a 「systemic insecurity」, force metadata retention for up to a year, and widen data sharing with foreign police — including a pending Canada–U.S. cross-border agreement that would give American police access without Canadian court oversight. It points to a CCLA/Citizen Lab legal analysis calling for Part 2 of the bill to be withdrawn outright, and to a CCLA petition urging Canadians to pressure senators.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between procedural realism and fatalism. One camp notes the Senate almost never kills a Commons bill and that the realistic best case is amendments trimming the worst parts, while another points to a formal e-petition (e-7416) tabled in the Commons as evidence that engagement is not pointless; a Canadian who says their own MP crossed the floor calls their influence 「absolutely fuck all」. Several expect little immediate industry reaction — providers will wait to see whether the government actually makes demands before pulling out of Canada, though some predict major messaging services will block the country. A recurring factual fight concerns claims that Canadian media is 「state funded」 and shills for the bill: several reply that only the CBC is publicly funded, most outlets are U.S.-owned or right-leaning, and the pro-government framing is invented rather than real. Commenters also dig into the text itself, arguing its limits are weasel words that ban compelling retention of content-identifying data but not real-time wiretaps or future production orders, with gag orders and undefined rule-making authority; law professor Michael Geist is repeatedly named as the best-known opponent. Practical privacy advice (Bitchat, cash, avoiding Five Eyes providers) is offered, but most agree it is a technical patch on a political problem.

**Worth reading**
- [donmcronald](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50214186): A close reading of the bill's text: the restrictions are written like big-tech ToS, barring compelled retention of content-identifying data while leaving real-time wiretaps and future production orders untouched, with gag orders and no transparency mechanism — and it will mostly catch low-level criminals, not organized crime.
- [wmchen](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50213365): Explains Senate practice: it generally defers to the elected chamber, is unlikely to kill the bill, can only propose amendments the Commons may ignore, so the realistic soft landing is amendments curtailing the worst provisions.
- [p_l](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50214369): Cites FATCA as evidence that promised reciprocity in data-sharing deals with the U.S. does not materialize in practice.

## 8. The Missed Opportunities of Sonic 06 {#story-8}
*#10 on HN · [isaacmarovitz.com](https://isaacmarovitz.com/posts/sonic-06/) · 47 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50211729)*

A technical post by a developer working on Marathon Recompiled, a project that recompiles the Xbox 360 version of Sonic 06 for PC, examining why the game looks so flat. Much of the cut visual content is still in the shipped game but disabled for performance: the most-used shader, Billboard02, supports only per-vertex lighting, no cascaded shadow maps and no point lights, while point lights are capped at two per object even though stages such as Flame Core and Dusty Desert request eight or more. The author also documents reduced shadow cascades, quarter-resolution planar reflections, static cubemaps replacing reflections in multiplayer, and the Lua render graph that makes restoring these features difficult.

**Discussion**: Commenters accept the rendering analysis but argue it addresses the wrong problem: one says Sonic 06's gameplay and glitches are far more damaging than its graphics and points to Project 06, a near-complete Unity rebuild. Another sees the same pattern in Sonic Adventure 1, whose lighting and textures degraded with each port from Dreamcast to GameCube to PC, and links a video comparison. A third shifts the blame to Sega management and Yuji Naka, citing his later securities-fraud arrest and the allegation that he withheld the Nights engine from an American team building a Saturn Sonic game. A final comment asks what the target frame rate was and where that figure comes from, but receives no answer in the thread.

**Worth reading**
- [rjh29](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50214313): Argues that gameplay and bugs matter more than graphics here and highlights Project 06, an almost-finished Unity remake started by a developer with no prior programming experience.
- [minimaxir](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50213036): Adds a precedent from Sonic Adventure 1, whose lighting and textures visibly degraded across the Dreamcast, GameCube and PC releases, with a video documenting the changes.

## 9. Air Purifiers as White Noise Machines {#story-9}
*#11 on HN · [jefftk.com](https://www.jefftk.com/p/air-purifiers-as-white-noise-machines) · 26 points · 25 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50212507)*

Jeff Kaufman's blog post argues that a small air purifier is strictly better than an electronic white noise machine: instead of a fan spinning in free air, the same fan pulls air through a filter, so you get the same fan-in-a-box sound plus smoke and infectious-particle removal. He cites a $47 purifier with a 6.5-inch fan doing 34 CFM CADR at 45 dB, versus his AirFanta 3Pro which moves roughly 6x more air at the same noise (12x at ~56 dB) for $165 and a bulkier 12.6-inch body. In a 10ft x 8ft room with typical 0.5 air changes per hour of leakage, he calculates even the small unit cuts indoor particulate levels to 16% of outdoor levels — AQI 200 down to 72, or 100 down to 31.

**Discussion**: Commenters largely agree with the premise and go further: the fan noise is deeper and more sleep-friendly than the tinny speakers in dedicated white noise machines, and several describe a Pavlovian sleep response or simply using purifiers as dual-purpose bedroom appliances. The main technical fight is over DIY and PC-fan purifiers — one camp insists axial fans lack the static pressure for real filtration, while others point out that the linked units draw 30+ watts with server-style fans and that you can trade filter surface area for pressure drop. A separate objection is behavioral: tying filtration to noise means the air only gets cleaned at night or when the noise is wanted, though owners reply that low settings are already near-silent and can run continuously. A subthread pushes back on the idea that smoke and infectious particles are exotic concerns, listing Bay Area wildfire seasons, the US West, Canada, Europe, and Michigan fires degrading Chicago's air 350 miles away; a tangent on allergy relief claims sleeping in heavily filtered air reduced baseline inflammation.

**Worth reading**
- [schiffern](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50214405): Argues the “axial fans can't overcome filter pressure” conventional wisdom is wrong if you pair them with high-airflow MERV 13 filters like 3M 1900 instead of high-resistance HEPA.
- [kube-system](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50214250): Notes the PC-fan purifiers are really high-static-pressure server fans drawing 30+ watts, and that you can compensate for low pressure by buying more filter area — which just moves the cost to filters.
- [kccqzy](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50213979): First-hand owner of the Austin Air HealthMate says it handles odors and VOCs well but barely moves his PM2.5 meter, contradicting its reputation as a strong particulate filter.

## 11. Reforming English Spelling {#story-11}
*#13 on HN · [blog.oup.com](https://blog.oup.com/2026/10/reforming-english-spelling/) · 8 points · 6 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50211084)*

A post by Anatoly Liberman, the Oxford Etymologist and honorary president of the English Spelling Society, published on the OUPblog after the Society's congress in London in October 2026. He recounts the movement founded in 1908 and backed by Shaw and Skeat, notes a 「Devil's Advocate」 motion to disband it after more than a century of no progress, and argues for a minimalist, decades-long program: drop letters nobody misses (co(m)munity, su(f)ficient, dumb→dum, occupy with one c), regularize g before i/e toward j, and win over teachers, ESL instructors and media rather than legislators.

**Discussion**: Only about half a dozen comments, mostly amused rather than analytical. One commenter offered 「queue」 — five letters for the sound of the first — as Exhibit A for the absurdity of English orthography, and another pointed to Quikscript, a purpose-built phonetic shorthand whose inventor was named Read. A third noted the resemblance to Tolkien's Tengwar. The thread's centerpiece is a pasted copy of the famous staged 「Year 1 … Year 15」 spelling-reform satire, which most people attribute to Mark Twain; a moderator-type reply corrected the attribution to Dolton Edwards in Astounding Science Fiction, September 1946, and linked an earlier HN discussion.
