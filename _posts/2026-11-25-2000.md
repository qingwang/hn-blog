## 1. 1Password Security Incident {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [1password.com](https://1password.com/blog/update-on-nov-2026-security-incident) · 559 points · 241 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50644817)*

1Password's own incident update says that on November 19, 2026 an attacker took over an employee's corporate GitHub account, used it to read private source code and push malicious code to backend services, and from there downloaded administrative customer records: names, email addresses, account and user IDs, creation and last-sign-in times, public keys, SRP parameters, internal metadata, plus invite/provisioning/recovery tokens present in some emailed links. 1Password says no vault contents or vault keys were accessed, that the attacker also reached the CI/CD system (which holds infrastructure secrets), and that it has rotated exposed credentials, disabled the GitHub account, added 10-character alphanumeric invitation and recovery codes (up from six digits) and brought in outside security firms. One update notes a small number of users in the provisioning-token state were individually notified.

**Discussion**: The thread is dominated by Thomas Ptacek's argument that compromised employee laptops pushing code into CI/CD is now the standard SaaS breach pattern, that line-by-line review of a fast-moving codebase is no longer realistic, and that the real question is how to establish change provenance — a claim many commenters flatly reject, arguing that a password manager of all companies should be able to afford deliberate review and that 'change velocity is a force of nature' is a choice driven by VC pressure. A second cluster of concern is the client software rather than the servers: several people argue E2EE is moot if the attacker can ship a malicious app, browser extension or 1password.com JavaScript bundle that exfiltrates decrypted secrets, and others ask whether encrypted vaults themselves were copied for a harvest-now-decrypt-later attack — 1Password's blog does not say clearly, though a customer email quoted in the thread asserts no vaults or keys were obtained. Skeptics also press on how a single account could push to production, and replies enumerate ways GitHub branch protection and rulesets get bypassed (org admins disabling rulesets, first commits to a nonexistent branch, stale approved PRs, bot approvals). Long-time users mourning the end of local vaults plug 1Password 7, KeePassXC, Vaultwarden and self-hosting, with others countering that self-hosting is only safer if you actually patch it, and the rough consensus is that 1Password handled this about as well as the industry standard allows while the underlying trust-me-with-my-updates problem remains unsolved.

**Worth reading**
- [loginatnine](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50645123): A detailed list of concrete ways GitHub rulesets and branch protection get bypassed — admin ruleset toggling, blank-repo first commits, stale approved PRs, bot approvals, protected 'master' vs 'main' tricks — useful for anyone auditing their own repo settings.
- [tptacek](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50644937): Ptacek's calibrated read: 1Password is serious relative to the industry and this architecture was never one SQL query away from vault plaintext, though he has qualms about the specific mechanism.
- [ChrisHardie](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50646442): Points out the concrete exfiltration vector that E2EE does not cover — 1Password.com can decrypt and display vault items in the browser, so a malicious JS bundle update could ship secrets out.

## 2. Holy Shit Holy Shit Holy Shit Holy Shit [Algorithmic Complexity Lower Bounds] {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [scottaaronson.blog](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10379) · 141 points · 68 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50646108)*

A Scott Aaronson blog post reporting, in ecstatic and deliberately over-the-top prose, that OpenAI and Anthropic have in quick succession published proofs of VP ≠ VNP, of ω = 9/4 as the exact exponent of matrix multiplication (a matching lower bound to an upper bound OpenAI gave in an earlier 'Mathocalypse' drop in October), and of an N(log N)^(1/2+o(1)) gate count for the Fourier transform — again with a matching lower bound, beating the FFT. He also mentions an Alpöge/Anthropic proof of the Riemann Hypothesis and a Yupan Liu preprint showing QIP(2) = PSPACE, and says he is 'virtually certain' the labs are sitting on cryptographically relevant bombshells he won't describe because he can't verify them. The post's argument is that Lean-checked AI proofs are now arriving faster than the community can absorb them, and that P ≠ NP no longer feels distant.

**Discussion**: The thread splits less over the math than over what to believe: several commenters push back hard that the results are unverified, citing a Riemann Hypothesis proof described as 'one big mess', a Navier–Stokes result that looks largely human work, mismatches between Lean and informal write-ups, and papers that just say 'Claude' with no attribution — one calls the whole thing marketing-flavoured 'slop' from both humans and AI. Others accept the premise and argue that Lean certificates make checking cheap, so verification being easier than understanding is the whole point; a related camp says the work is really 'AI in a harness' writing Lean4, and that fields only benefit once they have a programming-language-like formalisation, which is why biology and medicine are much further off (trials, no bio-Lean). A large crypto subthread asks whether the world is ready for broken cryptography, with corrections that 'cryptography' can't break wholesale — one-time pads remain, symmetric ciphers only lose to Grover — while broken PKI would be a huge, centralising mess; one commenter argues a real breakthrough would be classified the way the NSA suppressed IBM's differential cryptanalysis in the 1970s, and others counter that once a model is released the knowledge is effectively public. Side threads complain the title is childish and the piece is a breath-dump rather than good prose, ask whether ω = 9/4 yields an actually usable algorithm (nobody answers), and wonder about Millennium Prize eligibility now that prompting, not humans, does the work.

**Worth reading**
- [tete](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50646676): A detailed skeptic's inventory of specific problems: Navier–Stokes apparently mostly human work, the Riemann Hypothesis proof a mess nobody has seriously audited, Lean/paper mismatches, and papers crediting only 'Claude' — evidence, they argue, of marketing outpacing disclosure.
- [RunSet](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50646534): Raises the sharpest philosophical objection: a proof too complicated for any human to understand is not meaningfully different from claiming divine revelation, answered in reply by the claim that machine verification is easier than human understanding.
- [xoa](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50646626): Useful correction to the crypto panic: one-time pads cannot break, symmetric ciphers face only Grover-style speedups, and the real damage would be to PKI — disruptive but not a fall to zero, and likely to push further centralisation.

## 4. Britain's ghost stations {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [spectator.com](https://spectator.com/article/britains-ghost-stations-are-fascinating/) · 35 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50645074)*

A Spectator column by a writer who deliberately rode to Achanalt in the Highlands, one of only 352 passengers to use it in the April 2025–March 2026 count. The least-used station in Britain is Reddish South, near Stockport, with 94 passengers; last year's winner, Elton and Orston in Nottinghamshire, jumped from 68 to 102 and dropped to third because station-visiting enthusiasts chase the title. The piece divides ghost stations into two kinds: Beeching-era survivors kept alive by a token Saturday service (Reddish South gets one train each way, with five minutes before the return), and fully served rural stops like Achanalt, four houses, or Altnabreac on the Far North Line, which logged zero passengers in 2024/25 while a locked gate and legal fight blocked platform repairs, then 172 last year — all while Dudley (335,000 people) and Mansfield (114,000) lost their rail service.

**Discussion**: The thread is small and mostly fond rather than argumentative. One commenter points out these stops cost almost nothing to keep once the line itself is running — Achanalt is almost certainly a request-only stop on the Dingwall–Kyle of Lochalsh line, a line that outlived its original purpose when the Skye bridge opened in 1995 — and recommends the Inverness–Wick route and Forsinard for the Caithness silence. A second describes deliberately detraining at Lapford, having to ask the guard to stop and then flag the train down 'like a taxi' the next day, and concludes such stations are historical curiosities rather than transport. A third, who grew up near Beeching-hit lines, agrees the closures were geographically perverse and adds that former trackbeds became recreational walkways, with housing and entrenched NIMBYism now making reopening impossible — another commenter guesses the Downs Link. The only sour note is an off-topic complaint about The Spectator's cookie-consent wall and its '5403 technology partners'.

**Worth reading**
- [andyjohnson0](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50646497): Explains the economics — the line runs anyway, so Achanalt likely survives as a request stop — and notes the Kyle line lost its ferry-to-Skye rationale when the bridge opened in 1995.
- [fishtoaster](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50646394): First-hand account of using Lapford: the train only stopped because it was requested, and had to be flagged down like a taxi on the return.
- [fidotron](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50645995): Argues the Beeching cuts removed large towns while sparing hamlets, and that converted trackbeds plus new housing and local opposition now make reopening effectively impossible.

## 5. Google releases Cogentic papers on integer factorization, discrete logs and more {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/google-research/google-research/tree/master/cogentic_research_papers) · 371 points · 287 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50642086)*

The linked page is a GitHub directory in google-research collecting ten papers produced by Cogentic, described as Google's automated proof-discovery effort. The batch includes Unconditional Integer Factorization in Time L_N[1/3, O(1)], a deterministic subexponential factoring result, discrete logarithms in fixed-characteristic finite fields in sub-quasipolynomial time, a polynomial-time 1/5-approximation for Nash equilibria in bimatrix games, and results on the log-rank conjecture, graph edge reconstruction, prophet inequalities, fictitious play, VC-class compression and correlated equilibria. The listing itself gives only titles, an Apache 2.0 note and a disclaimer; no abstracts or Lean formalizations appear in the directory.

**Discussion**: Much of the thread reacts to a rumored 'factoring is in P' result and worries that RSA is broken; commenters link a tweet and a Scott Aaronson blog comment, and debate whether a lab would publish such a result or quietly tell the NSA. Several readers push back with the actual complexity numbers: the new factoring bound is L_N[1/3,105] versus the heuristic L_N[1/3,1.923] of GNFS, and the deterministic version is exp(O((log N)^(3/4))), so the constants make it useless against real RSA keys. Others note that ECC and post-quantum schemes are not directly affected by factoring advances, while a separate strand discusses AI-driven math discoveries, whether LLM proofs can be trusted without Lean formalizations, and how automated proof search is changing academic careers and incentives.

**Worth reading**
- [thomasahle](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50642719): Gives a compact table comparing each new bound with the previous established one, including L_N[1/3,105] versus L_N[1/2,1] for randomized factoring and the log-rank exponent improving from 0.5 to 0.499995.
- [gukoff](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50644906): Explains that the factoring result is built on GNFS but replaces the parts depending on unproven heuristics, paying a large complexity tax instead of proving those conjectures.
- [dgacmu](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50642784): States the practical effect is none: the constants are enormous, GNFS remains the algorithm to use, and the paper's use of AKS rather than a GRH-based primality test is an amusing detail.

## 6. Windows Loudness Equalization, reverse-engineered and ported to PipeWire {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/xoxorwr/pw-loudness-eq) · 273 points · 162 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50641171)*

A GitHub repo (xoxorwr/pw-loudness-eq) that reimplements Windows' 'Loudness Equalization' as a PipeWire module, reverse-engineered from WMALFXGFXDSP.dll, plus a port of Windows' always-on output limiter (CAudioLimiter in AudioEng.dll). The loudness part is a broadband AGC — 25 log-spaced bands at 100 Hz–24 kHz, loudness L = 1.72·Σ max(E_b − T_b, 1e-32)^0.25, target gain min((1300/L)^1.6, 10) (max +20 dB), release time 2–7 with 4 as the Windows default; the limiter is a 64-frame look-ahead design hard-coded to a 0.985 peak. It requires PipeWire ≥ 1.4, ships a GTK3 toggle app, supports per-app exclusion via pw-metadata, and does not process JACK clients, >8-channel streams or native-PipeWire Flatpak apps.

**Discussion**: Several commenters object to the framing legally and ethically: the code was produced with an LLM from a proprietary DLL, and they argue this is 'just piracy' that poisons the well for anyone who later writes a legitimate loudness normalizer; others counter that algorithms aren't copyrightable, that Microsoft has little to lose, and that Microsoft's own Copilot copyright pledge makes a suit unlikely. A recurring correction is terminological — this is loudness normalization/AGC, not equalization, and its deliberate susceptibility to pure tones is why the limiter exists. Audio people split on whether the feature is desirable at all: purists say a composition's dynamic range exists for a reason, while others say they want it for laptop speakers, podcasts, noisy rooms, quiet Zoom participants and mixing several apps at once. Experienced users point to alternatives (EasyEffects with Digitalone1 presets, Calf Studio Gear, loudmax, Equalizer APO), though one says the EasyEffects presets aren't close to Windows' behavior and another says the hard part is the tuning, not the algorithm. Two concrete technical notes came up: Windows applies a shared-mode limiter (about -0.13 dBFS) on everything except WASAPI exclusive and ASIO, which surprised the metering people who found it, and an audio engineer active on Linux objects that the limiter being always-on and system-wide is a real problem for production work.

**Worth reading**
- [dintech](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50643693): Explains that Windows already runs a limiter on the shared-mode WASAPI/DirectSound/MME render path after mixing, so loopback audio is subtly compressed (~-0.1 dBFS at peaks) while WASAPI exclusive and ASIO are untouched — something the commenter discovered while building a metering system.
- [vrganj](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50641874): Argues the deeper harm isn't the code itself but that its existence makes every future loudness-normalization project look like possible legal exposure, so no serious project can upstream anything similar.
- [ephaeton](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50641395): An audio engineer doing production work on Linux asks that the always-active system-wide limiter not be adopted by PipeWire or enabled by default, since hidden system-wide compression is a genuine problem for that use case.

## 7. Predicting LoRA size for any dataset {#story-7}
*#7 on HN · [engram.com](https://engram.com/blog/lora-size) · 77 points · 8 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50635783)*

The post is an Engram research blog post deriving a way to size a LoRA adapter from the number of bits needed to encode an SFT dataset under the base model. It gives a capacity formula based on the top singular values of a random Gaussian matrix — roughly linear in rank at low rank, then plateauing — and reports about 378k bits at full rank for a 512×1024 v_proj in an 800M Qwen3.5 model, or 0.48 bits per parameter, rising to 1.3–1.4 at low rank. It generates synthetic datasets of exactly N bits by running arithmetic coding backwards through a model's probabilities, measures full-transformer capacity empirically (1.5 to 3.3 bits per parameter as more layers are adapted), and finds models forget roughly as many bits as they learn.

**Discussion**: Commenters are interested, but the main challenge is definitional: augment_me argues the method measures memorization of a finite dataset — including noise, typos and redundancy — not learning the underlying task, so on reasoning benchmarks such as GSM8K or MATH500 it would amount to massive overfitting and the resulting ranks are upper bounds. That commenter also disputes the 'any SFT dataset' framing and suggests titles like 'Predicting the LoRA rank needed to overfit a dataset on Qwen-0.8B' or 'How much can a LoRA memorize?'. Another commenter asks whether the reported 1:1 learning-forgetting exchange holds for genuinely out-of-distribution SFT data that teaches new domain knowledge rather than a style or format, since the conservation result was validated on data drawn from the base model's distribution. Others ask about usefulness-per-byte metrics, applying the method to image-generation models, and one jokes about confusion with LoRa radio.

**Worth reading**
- [augment_me](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50645749): Argues the capacity measurement conflates memorizing a dataset's noise and redundancy with learning the task, making the predicted rank an upper bound and likely massive overfitting on reasoning benchmarks.
- [keel_dev](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50644795): Asks whether the observed 1:1 tradeoff between bits learned and bits forgotten holds for truly out-of-distribution SFT data that teaches new domain knowledge, not just format or style.

## 8. I checked Clojure's core with Lean and found unreported bugs {#story-8}
*#8 on HN · [s.conao3.com](https://s.conao3.com/blog/2026/e4f733) · 114 points · 42 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50642939)*

A Clojure user applied the hirosassa/lean-verify method — model one small function as a Lean 4 definition, state a property against a naive wider-width specification, and let bv_decide hunt for counterexamples — to the Java and Clojure parts of Clojure's core, treating Java int/long as BitVec 32/64, and checked the big persistent data structures by differential testing against java.util. On Clojure 1.12.7 (released 2026-11-18) with JDK 21 he reports 17 bugs not present in Clojure's JIRA or ask.clojure.org, including an infinite loop in reduce over a dropped vector-of with 65505+ elements (a 0xffe0 mask in gvec.clj that clears bit 16 and above), HashCollisionNode.without passing the caller's hash instead of this.hash so dissoc hides an equal-but-differently-hashed key, removeIf giving different results with and without a type hint, and an int overflow in StringSeq.drop. The HAMT, PersistentTreeMap and vector tree themselves passed 3 million differential operations, including boundary sizes 32/1056/32800/1048608; the bugs clustered in code around the trees and where mechanisms meet, like = versus hashing.

**Discussion**: Commenters largely split three ways. One camp argues the findings are mostly noise: rvprasad says several items are just type misuse the author should have caught by eye — (.indexOf (seq (byte-array [1 2 3])) 2) compares a Long against bytes, and get coercing 1.9 to an index is defensible weak typing, so documenting the behavior would serve users as well as calling it a bug. A second camp, opened by dmead, says a dynamically typed library never promised the guarantees formal verification implies, and that this would only be news for Haskell's stdlib; that drew sharp rebuttals that the reported bugs (an infinite loop, a lost key after dissoc, a wrong result from removeIf) have nothing to do with types, with lo_zamoyski and agentultra adding that even Haskell's types are far weaker than real formal verification and that Idris/Agda/Lean are where the strong properties live. A third and much larger thread drifted off the article entirely to Rich Hickey's anti-LLM stance: nextos links his LinkedIn post calling LLMs 'intrinsic automation' rather than a tool, others report writing millions of lines of Lisp with LLMs and no paren-balancing problem, while a few counter that Clojure is a human-first language ill-suited to machine generation. Smaller notes: gritzko remarks that a JVM-based language is probably unverifiable in practice, and several people ask why Lean rather than Rocq, with the answer being LLM/autoformalization momentum and Mathlib.

**Worth reading**
- [rvprasad](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50645750): rvprasad argues the bug list contains noise — the byte-array indexOf case is a Long passed where a byte is expected, and get's coercion of 1.9 is reasonable for a dynamic language — and asks what agreed-upon semantics the proofs are checked against.
- [nextos](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50644967): nextos links Rich Hickey's LinkedIn essay arguing LLMs are 'intrinsic automation' rather than a tool, and that using them means giving up — the seed of the thread's long AI detour.
- [articulatepang](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50644931): articulatepang pushes back on the 'Lisp is untyped so this isn't a bug' line, pointing out the first finding is an infinite loop in a core data structure at 65,505 elements, which no type system claim excuses.

## 9. Can a TV be too large? An investigation {#story-9}
*#9 on HN · [techcrunch.com](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/22/can-a-tv-be-too-large-an-investigation/) · 14 points · 6 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50608175)*

A TechCrunch first-person review by Lucas, who upgraded from a 55-inch TCL to a 65-inch LG evo C4 OLED (the company's 2024 mid-range model) and got hit by hours of nausea midway through watching 'McCabe & Mrs. Miller' on 4K Blu-ray, followed by a tension headache. He notes Reddit threads full of similar reports, including LG C2 and 85-inch owners, and walks through the usual explanations — motion smoothing (off on his set), sitting too close (he was nine feet back), and brightness (he turned it to ~50%) — before concluding his eyes simply adjusted after a few days. He also complains the TV ships with no real assembly instructions and that a 65-inch panel would have overwhelmed his old apartment.

**Discussion**: Commenters broadly reject the article's size/eye-inexperience theory: people have watched giant cinema and IMAX screens for a century without nausea, and multiple 65-inch OLED owners — plus their flatmates and guests — report no such symptoms. The favoured technical explanation is panel driving rather than size: one commenter points to low-PWM-frequency flicker in Apple's OLED MacBooks and to the aggressive power management big panels need, noting his 65-inch Panasonic plasma could draw over 800W in bright scenes. Others suspect brightness and refresh rate, with a projector owner who runs a 120-inch screen at 24fps and normally suffers motion sickness saying he had no problem — and that TVs are simply far too bright for typical rooms. Nobody lands on a firm cause; the rough consensus is that this is an OLED/driving-algorithm issue rather than a 'screen too big' issue.

**Worth reading**
- [bob1029](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50608454): Argues the real cause is the panel's drive electronics, citing low PWM frequency on the OLED MacBook and the heavy power management large panels require, with his 65-inch plasma pulling 800W+ in some scenes.
- [d3Xt3r](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50608294): Pushes back on the eye-tracing explanation by pointing to cinemas and IMAX, and reports his own 65-inch OLED caused no nausea in himself, his flatmates, family or guests.
- [globular-toast](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50646559): First-hand data point in the other direction: a 120-inch projector screen at similar distance with no motion sickness despite being prone to it, blaming TV brightness and refresh rate instead.

## 10. Deriving Black-Scholes {#story-10}
*#11 on HN · [david.alvarezrosa.com](https://david.alvarezrosa.com/posts/deriving-black-scholes/) · 29 points · 15 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50644814)*

Corporate practitioners describe abandoning defensive registration — Disney's legal team decided to sue infringers instead, and another commenter's employer bought only domains it actually used. The rough mood is that most new TLDs will be squatted, unused or priced punitively, though a few defend namespace expansion in principle.
