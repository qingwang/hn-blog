## 2. Sound – Bartosz Ciechanowski (2022) {#story-2}
*#4 on HN · [ciechanow.ski](https://ciechanow.ski/sound/) · 78 points · 10 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50045147)*

An interactive explainer on what sound is, how it is created and how it propagates, built almost entirely out of in-page simulations the reader can drag, resize and play. It starts from the kinetic theory of air — molecules averaging 460 m/s at room temperature, colliding and exerting pressure on walls — then scales up through 1-micrometer volumes and ~26 million particles to show a moving plate launching pressure waves, compression and rarefaction, and how vibration frequency in hertz maps to perceived pitch. The reader plays notes on a small keyboard widget (mouse or W/E/R keys) throughout.

**Discussion**: The thread is almost purely admiration: commenters compare the experience to reading a good pop-science magazine or encyclopedia as a child, and single out the article's progression from bouncing air molecules to harmonics and interference as its best feature. Several note the visualizations are unusually high quality compared with the wave of similar blog explainers that have appeared recently, and one points out they are all hand-written, vanilla JavaScript — calling the author an artisan. The recurring joke is that this sets a new benchmark for AI: a commenter proposes a “Bartosz test” for general intelligence (give a model a concept, get it explained like this), and it is answered with the Einstein-ish line about being “as simple as possible, but not simpler.” One commenter stresses that the author was writing this way long before AI, which is why the work can be trusted; a lone reply simply notes the piece is from 2022.

**Worth reading**
- [disillusioned](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50082893): Notes that the visualizations are entirely hand-written vanilla JavaScript, no framework or library, and calls the author an artisan.

## 3. Ledger confirms spy chip implant behind wallet crypto thefts {#story-3}
*#5 on HN · [bleepingcomputer.com](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/ledger-confirms-hardware-wallet-spy-implant-behind-crypto-thefts/) · 622 points · 409 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50054092)*

A BleepingComputer report that Ledger confirmed at least one device sold through Malaysia-based Southeast Asian reseller CryptoBilis contained an unauthorized hardware implant, after users reported drained wallets. Researchers including Mark Karpelès and Tibane Labs documented two variants of the implant in Ledger Nano X units: an ST STM32G431KBU6 (Arm Cortex-M4) MCU plus a ~2×2 mm eSIM and shielded LTE module, hidden either by swapping in a smaller battery or in the foam padding under the OLED, capturing display and button data to reconstruct the seed phrase and exfiltrate it over cellular. Ledger calls the affected volume 「limited」, told CryptoBilis to stop shipping, and advises buyers there not to set devices up and to move funds to a new seed — noting its Genuine Check cannot detect added implants when the original Secure Element is intact.

**Discussion**: The thread splits on framing: the top commenter calls this proof that every 「theoretical, far-fetched」 hardware-wallet attack was real and blasts Ledger's practices (firmware/app install after seed entry, no airgapped mode, seed backup service), while replies including socializer and bri3d counter that hardware supply-chain tampering is decades old — essentially a gas-station card skimmer — and is only new in being aimed at retail crypto holders because it is profitable. The most repeated remedy is multisig across different vendors (2-of-2, 2-of-3, 3-of-5) plus airgapped laptops, dedicated phones or dice-generated seeds, with pushback that this just relocates trust and adds loss risk. Several people correct the article's technical framing: the implant sniffs unencrypted SPI pixel data to the display rather than doing OCR, and an eSIM is software, not a chip; others note Genuine Check's blind spot leaves users no practical verification. On detection, commenters argue it is lab-only work needing controlled RF conditions and unlucky timing, not something a home user can spot — though the tell-tale antenna wire was visible on teardown. A commenter who bought a Ledger Stax from CryptoBilis in the last 90 days says he opened it and found the implant, calling the workmanship good enough to pass for stock hardware.

**Worth reading**
- [csomar](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50078739): First-hand corroboration: he bought a Ledger Stax from the same reseller within 90 days, opened it with a knife, and found the implant — and says it looks like original Ledger hardware.
- [tacker2000](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50082762): Technical correction: the implant is not doing OCR on the screen, it intercepts unencrypted pixel data on the SPI bus between CPU and display.
- [hunter-gatherer](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50081850): Someone who did hardware hacking for three-letter agencies says he skipped Ledger back in 2017 precisely because the devices shipped without anti-tamper packaging while Ledger bragged that it didn't matter.

## 5. 900-Page Pre-Pantone Guide to Color from 1692 (With Complete High-Res Scan) {#story-5}
*#8 on HN · [openculture.com](https://www.openculture.com/2026/10/a-900-page-pre-pantone-guide-to-color-from-1692.html) · 133 points · 13 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50053487)*

An Open Culture post (a reprint of a 2019 article) about Traité des couleurs servant à la peinture à l'eau, originally Klaer Lichtende Spiegel der Verfkonst — a 900-page, single-copy Dutch manuscript on watercolour mixing written by the artist A. Boogert in 1692, containing over 700 pages of hand-painted swatches. It builds on Aristotle's system of colour rather than the Newtonian spectrum of Opticks, was intended to teach watercolourists, and the article says there is little evidence it influenced colour theory in its day. The only copy is held at the Bibliothèque Méjanes in Aix-en-Provence, which hosts page-by-page zoomable high-resolution scans; the piece links it forward to Goethe (1810), Werner (1814) and Pantone (1963).

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly enthusiasm plus further reading rather than argument: one commenter recommends Thames & Hudson's Nature's Palette, which reproduces and expands on Werner's 1814 Nomenclature of Colour, paired with Taschen's Ernst Haeckel volume, and another points to the ColorNerd YouTube channel for colour spaces and colour theory. A side debate on whether the swatches are still colour-accurate draws the explanation that pigments were largely mineral and survive well, with varnish being what discolours old paintings and shelved book pages escaping light and air — countered by a commenter who doubts all pigments were mineral and cites Egyptian blue as one that was not available in 1692. The most substantive correction comes from a commenter who went through the full scan and reports that the page featured in the article was cherry-picked because many pages in the document are heavily faded, adding that re-creating the recipes is near-impossible since the once-implied 17th-century context is gone. Commenters also puzzle over how the book went unnoticed until historian Erik Kwakkel found it in a French database, and a late tangent explains what Pantone is actually for (consistent printed colour across printers for branding) and notes that Willis Carrier's work on temperature and humidity control for printing led to air conditioning.

**Worth reading**
- [somat](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50082202): After going through the full scan, reports that the article's striking page was cherry-picked — many pages are badly faded — and that reproducing the recipes is nearly impossible because the 17th-century context they assumed is lost.
- [vanderZwan](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50082131): Explains why the colours lasted: pigments were mostly durable mineral sources and it is varnish that shifts colour over time, while shelved book pages avoid light and air, though the ultramarine blue pages must have been expensive.
- [WillAdams](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50081437): Clarifies that Pantone exists to keep different printers matching a brand's colour, and adds that Willis Carrier's drive for stable temperature and humidity in printing led to the invention of air conditioning.

## 7. I built a tool to track what AI leaders say and how their views change on video {#story-7}
*#11 on HN · [blumify.io](https://blumify.io/people) · 73 points · 47 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50081151)*

The linked page is blumify.io/people, a directory of the ~20 AI figures the tool follows (Elon Musk, Alexandr Wang, Marc Andreessen, Satya Nadella, Greg Brockman, Palmer Luckey, Eric Schmidt and others), with a vote widget to pick who gets a page next. The builder explains in the thread that it turns YouTube videos and podcasts into searchable transcripts, finds interviews where the person actually speaks, and produces a 「What changed」 view pairing a current quote against an earlier one with dates and source links — the showcase example being Sam Altman saying in October 2025 there would 「come a glut for sure」 and in June 2026 that there would be 「a shortage forever」.

**Discussion**: Commenters broadly like the concept but attack two weak points: coverage and accuracy. A recurring suggestion is to point the same machinery at politicians, which draws pushback that a system highlighting every changed position would punish exactly the evidence-driven mind-changing we want from politicians, and a complaint that the site gates everything behind a signup.

**Worth reading**
- [gcsydney](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50081152): The builder's own context: 20 people tracked, daily discovery of new appearances, and an admission that speaker attribution is harder than transcription and that every published comparison is still manually reviewed.
- [FabHK](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50081712): Direct correction that the clip captioned as Dario Amodei changing his mind on regulation is not Amodei at all, with a link to the original (a Kai Lentit video) — the strongest evidence for the accuracy complaints.
- [probably_wrong](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50082982): Counter-argument from Argentine TV precedent: contrasting a politician's current statement with an older clip to paint them as a hypocrite discourages them from ever changing a stated position, so the idea can backfire.

## 8. Recreational Programming {#story-8}

**Discussion**: The thread is split over AI. One camp (several hand-writers and hobbyists) argues LLMs change nothing because the motivation is intrinsic — the deck itself defines recreational programming as intrinsically motivated, so if AI 「killed it」 you were doing it for second-order effects; others add that AI has supercharged their ambition, letting them finish weekend projects, with one commenter using Claude to build a PDP-7 and Type 340 vector display emulator to run the 1960s CAD system PIXIE. The counter-camp says the payoff of sharing is devalued when anyone can generate something similar, that useless projects have lost their point, and one person reports colleagues assuming Claude wrote a data-visualization tool they built by hand. Nostalgia runs alongside: a commenter recounts buying a 4K Radio Shack computer in 1981, mailing Motorola for a free 6809 manual, and selling a disk utility on floppies for $5 to 28 customers — leading to 40 years, three startups and two PC Magazine Product of the Year awards. Separate gripes target the presentation site itself (scroll trapping, no sense of how far along you are; a commenter notes arrow keys and vim-style hjkl work and a progress bar sits on the right) and one reply rejects the deck's 「Problem Solver」 category as breaking its own premise.

**Worth reading**
- [mrandish](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50081303): A 40-year first-hand origin story: Radio Shack 4K machine in 1981, a free Motorola 6809 manual mailed by a sales rep, a $5 floppy disk utility earning $140 from 28 customers, and how it led to three startups and two PC Magazine Product of the Year awards.
- [DonHopkins](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50082991): DonHopkins describes using Claude to recreationally build a PDP-7 and Type 340 vector-display emulator to run PIXIE (the 1960s Cambridge CAD system with the first known radial menus), plus a live Minsky 「ultimate machine」-style demo — a concrete counterpoint to the claim that AI removes the point.
- [bemtevi](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50081541): A nuanced counter-argument: recreational programming for many people is social, and AI diminishes the value others place on what you share — not because you stop enjoying it, but because the audience stops being impressed.

## 9. The United Kingdom generates less electricity per person than in the 1970s {#story-9}
*#13 on HN · [ourworldindata.org](https://ourworldindata.org/data-insights/electricity-generation-per-person-in-the-united-kingdom-has-fallen-by-a-third-from-its-early-2000s-peak) · 272 points · 381 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50072994)*

An Our World in Data insight showing that UK per-capita electricity generation peaked in 2003 and has fallen by more than a third since, returning to late-1960s levels — while global per-capita generation grew over 50% this century. The piece attributes part of the drop to deindustrialization but argues efficiency (LED lighting, appliance standards) matters just as much: IEA sectoral data shows industrial consumption down a quarter since 2005, with residential and commercial falling by a similar amount. It also notes the caveat that net imports rose from 1% of demand in 2010 to 9% in 2025, so some of the decline is offshored supply rather than lower consumption.

Spotify Premium is only needed for the Spotify source — local files, Subsonic/Navidrome, internet radio (30k+ stations), YouTube via yt-dlp, Qobuz and, on macOS, Apple Music all work standalone. **Discussion**: The thread is small and mostly friendly: the top comment upvotes the project purely for its name, and the first real question asks whether the author knows about ncspot, which one commenter has used for years with librespot and no rate-limit trouble (another points out ncspot is already mentioned in the post).
