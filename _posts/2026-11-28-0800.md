## 1. Unkernel: Real Linux kernel, compiled to WebAssembly {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [unkernel.dev](https://unkernel.dev) · 197 points · 36 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50674218)*

Unkernel (unkernel.dev) is a project that compiles a real Linux kernel to WebAssembly so it boots and runs entirely inside a browser tab, complete with a shell, GUI apps and what commenters describe as a very fast Wayland compositor. The linked page itself returned almost no readable text, so details come from the thread: startup is near-instant, networking appears to be relayed through the project's server (a curl to ip-api.com reported Zurich, not the user's own IP), and it relies on JSPI, which on iPhone/iPad means iOS/iPadOS 27 or later (Safari 18). Commenters note it was already posted five days earlier (item 50533945).

*Article not read: page had almost no readable text (probably needs JavaScript or a login)*

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly delighted disbelief — several people report it running smoothly on phones and iPads and starting in a few hundred milliseconds — mixed with a genuine 'what is this for?' strand, answered with 'it's the challenge and the show' and the education angle of free per-student Linux environments, countered by the observation that students have been able to run VMs since 2008 and the real win is skipping two hours of setup. People with adjacent projects push back on the framing: one commenter says he has been building a lighter 'POSIX runtime for wasm' approach (dolly, slopshare.dev) and that most workloads don't need 90% of a kernel, while others link tabc, tabernets, trynix.dev and a PDP-10-in-the-browser emulator as the same genre. There is real first-hand testing: 4 concurrent md5sums finish in the same wall-clock time as one, Chromium can be installed inside Unkernel, and one commenter booted Unkernel inside Unkernel via trynix.dev on a 2026 Mac mini, though a nested Chromium did not work. Corrections land too — an iPad does not have 128 GB of RAM (that's the SSD), macOS-bloat comparisons are challenged, and people ask whether 'real kernel' distinguishes it from anything fake, with 'it's an AI title' as the cynical read. The proxied network exit raised the practical worry that the service doubles as a free proxy.

**Worth reading**

- [logicallee](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50674967): Gives exact click-by-click steps for running Unkernel inside Unkernel — type chromium in the shell, then load a tiny package from trynix.dev in the nested browser — and links a Doom-in-Unkernel demo.

## 2. Google's DeepCrossAttention achieves comparable model quality up to 3x faster {#story-2}
*#3 on HN · [research.google](https://research.google/blog/supercharging-transformer-residual-connections/) · 330 points · 134 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50672565)*

A Google Research blog post by Kyriakos Axiotis and Thomas Fu describing DeepCrossAttention (DCA), an architecture from their ICML 2025 paper (arXiv 2502.06785) that replaces the fixed additive residual connection with learned, per-token routing weights over all previous layer outputs, plus a depth-wise cross-attention (split Q/K/V) that attends across layers rather than tokens. The claims: same quality with up to 5x fewer training steps (up to 3x faster), under 0.2% extra parameters, and on LM1B reaching perplexity under 14 with 30 layers where a standard transformer needed 42 — a 29% cut in depth. Because DCA can initialize to behave exactly like a standard residual net, it can be retrofitted onto a pretrained model; brief continued training dropped LM1B perplexity by 0.19 versus 0.02 for standard continued training. It also positions DCA against Moonshot AI's attention residuals (including the Block AttnRes variant), Thinking Machines' Inkling 2, hyper-connections and manifold-constrained hyper-connections.

**Discussion**: Almost nobody discusses the architecture. The thread is overwhelmingly a subscription argument about Google's Gemini 'Neon' model versus Claude Opus 5.5 — whether Neon is genuinely better, whether it is actually cheaper per real task, and how quickly each burns a quota (several report hitting 5-hour limits in minutes; others report weekly quotas resetting daily). The skeptics are the ones with data: seizethecheese posts timings from a head-to-head 'Agent Duel' dataset showing Neon is actually slower per task than Opus 5.5 and Astra 6.2, and thurn logs quota metrics and blind trials finding Claude delivers 25-35% more value for the same price, blaming Antigravity's 'agy' harness and worse caching — while Neon partisans counter with one-shot successes on hard tasks and praise for its non-coding 'flavor'. The small on-topic minority notes that the paper is 1.5 years old (published Feb 2025, revised July 2025) and wonders why the blog post took so long, flags that 'Densenet anyone?', nitpicks the line-graph-instead-of-bar-chart figures, and speculates DCA could help with cross-layer recall problems like counting letters in 'strawberry'. Recurring meta-notes: that 'faster' here means training, not inference, and the cynical claim that Google publishing a technique implies it isn't using it.

**Worth reading**
- [thurn](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50674837): Reports extensively logging quota metrics and running blind trials on identical tickets: Claude delivered 25-35% more value per subscription dollar, largely because Antigravity's 'agy' harness burns more tokens and caches worse than Claude's, and blinded reviews scored Opus solutions higher.
- [amluto](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50674842): Argues depth-wise cross-attention could be a real fix for token-level counting failures, since early-layer K/V representations of the raw input could be queried again by later layers that understand the goal — while conceding tokenization and the lack of a clean 'count the hits' operation.
- [ACCount39](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50673144): Links the full paper (arxiv.org/abs/2502.06785) and places DCA in the lineage of depth-wise attention primitives alongside Moonshot's better-known AttnRes, suggesting looping layers could amplify the gains from learned layer-to-layer routing.

## 3. The Balatro Timeline {#story-3}
*#4 on HN · [localthunk.com](https://localthunk.com/blog/balatro-timeline-3aarh) · 288 points · 65 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50657346)*

LocalThunk's own development diary of Balatro, written three years in and covering everything up to launch day: the project began in December 2021 as a 'template' folder in his Learning/Lua directory, jumped from an abandoned game called Autohike, and started as an online multiplayer version of a friends' card game before the CHIP × MULT scoring idea and Jokers turned it into Balatro. He worked evenings and weekends from an IT job, quit and gave himself 3–6 months of full-time development when his partner's PhD led to a move out of province, paid $100 for Steam, commissioned the soundtrack from Luis Clemente on Fiverr, picked 'Balatro' from a list of ~20 names nobody else liked, and only played Slay the Spire in May 2024 — after which the first public beta went live to, as he expected, nothing.

**Discussion**: The thread splits hard over how much of Balatro's success was luck. One camp (echoing the article's Dan Gheesling moment) argues the game only took off because a big streamer found it and says 'get featured on a big YouTube channel' is a lottery, citing ~2% of Steam games earning over $10k; the other side counters that thousands of streamer-sponsored games go nowhere and Balatro's immediate, addictive hook is what let the lightning be bottled, with one commenter asking for actual examples of great games that flopped and another offering Cinco Paus and Corrypt only to be told their visuals rule out mass appeal. A separate argument worries that LLM-era cloning kills the slow-burn path Balatro took, answered with concrete cases (mobile ad-driven clones, multiple Find the Needle knockoffs appearing between demo and release) but also skepticism that PC/console indies are affected. Several subthreads correct or digress: that the eight-month gap between streamer coverage and Steam release would be terrifying now, that 'LocalThunk' comes from a Lua variable joke rather than functional-programming 'thunks', and a long, heated tangent about Dan Gheesling's US Army recruitment stream and its effect on his viewership.

**Worth reading**
- [nhinck3](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50675486): Gives a concrete cloning case: Find the Needle's demo went up on 11 September and multiple clones shipped before its own November release, which he argues will make devs think twice about demos.
- [duskwuff](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50675107): Corrects the assumption that Balatro isn't on PC or is DRM-free only elsewhere — the Steam build has no encryption, account binding or login requirement.
- [Fricken](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50675573): Pushes back on the luck thesis: across 1,000 indie games only one or two are worth playing for more than ten minutes, and those tend to rise regardless.

## 4. GPT 6.2 Astra Solves a Historical Mystery {#story-4}
*#5 on HN · [generativehistory.substack.com](https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/gpt-62-astra-solves-a-historical) · 423 points · 263 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50643158)*

A post on the Substack Generative History by Mark Humphries, a history professor at Wilfrid Laurier University, describing his pre-release testing of OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra. Given images of an anonymous 1808 sketchbook at Yale's Beinecke Library (84 pages, 34 with pencil, ink or watercolour drawings, recording a Montreal–Fort William canoe journey), the model answered in 22 minutes and 1 second that the artist was the exiled French aristocrat and British Army officer Louis-Guy-Charles-Guillaume de Rohan-Chabot, vicomte de Chabot (1780–1875), and pointed to a microfilmed Chabot diary held at Cleveland State University that it had traced through a footnote in a 2014 PhD thesis by historian Juliette Reboul. Ordering the diary confirmed it: matching distinctive handwriting, an itinerary aligned with the dated drawings, and an 18 June 1808 entry at Fort St. Joseph reading 'I took a sketch'. The author adds that Fable 5.2 reached the same conclusion from the same prompt in about 35 minutes.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people impressed that a model could connect a sketchbook to an obscure footnote spanning two archives, and skeptics who say this is retrieval over a human-built corpus rather than a replacement for expertise. Several commenters report analogous first-hand wins — one deciphered previously unbroken 1941 German army messages with Opus 5.5, another used Astra to match commercial air fresheners to chemicals named in a French paper — while others counter that the answer may have been latent in pre-training, with the visible 'research trail' reconstructed after the fact; defenders reply that historians and mathematicians also build entirely on prior sources, and that dismissing each result by moving the goalposts is unfalsifiable. Scope is the other fault line: critics argue this is narrow provenance and attribution work, not historical interpretation, and that much of the world's archival material is undigitized paper no model can reach. A side thread worries the same capability implies LLMs could narrow criminal suspects, unmask Satoshi, or turbocharge legal discovery, and a running joke is that 'Chabot' reads as 'Chatbot'.

**Worth reading**
- [Twiin](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50671205): Twiin reports using Opus 5.5 to decipher previously unbroken 1941 German army messages, praising its intuition about which archives and sources to consult — a parallel first-hand case.
- [_bent](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50672915): _bent argues the discovery may not be autonomous research: Chabot and the early-19th-century fur trade may already sit close together in the model's training data, so the cited documents could be post-hoc rationalization of a latent hunch.
- [suddenlybananas](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50668723): A commenter the author says he liked argues that provenance-and-authorship forensics is only a precondition for history — the documents do not interpret themselves, and that interpretive work is not like a mathematical proof.

## 5. Paolo Soleri built a utopian eco-city in the Arizona desert {#story-5}
*#7 on HN · [worldofinteriors.com](https://www.worldofinteriors.com/story/paolo-soleri-arconsati-arizona) · 36 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50666724)*

A World of Interiors feature (March 2026 issue) on Arcosanti, Paolo Soleri's 860-acre 'arcology' prototype in the high Sonoran Desert, begun in 1970 and planned for 5,000 residents. In practice it never housed more than 150 people and now holds about two dozen; under 10 per cent of the masterplan was built, and the Cosanti Foundation's archivist says applications have stalled because the lifestyle is 'harsh and isolating' and nearly monastic. The piece frames Arcosanti as a live experiment in density and co-living relevant to today's housing costs — US average house prices above $410,000 — and carries an editor's note that Daniela Soleri has made allegations of abuse against her father.

**Discussion**: The thread is small and mostly admiring of the architecture and photos, but the sharpest exchange is a correction: one commenter argues the place is not a commune at all but effectively a company town, where residents work for the project at heavily reduced rent, and that this structured relationship with the foundation is the only reason it has survived — a framing another commenter endorses. A visitor from 25 years ago describes it as a small village rather than a city and doubts it can scale, while recommending Soleri's illustrated book 'Arcology: The City in the Image of Man'. The most concrete contribution is local geography: Arcosanti sits off I-17 at Cordes Junction, halfway between Phoenix and Flagstaff, chosen partly because the land is unincorporated and light on regulators, in barren, cactus-free high desert in the rain shadow of the Prescott mountains. Nobody defends the commune model against the isolation argument; disagreement is mainly over what to call the place rather than whether it works.

**Worth reading**
- [yepyoukno](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50667041): Argues Arcosanti is not a commune but a company town — residents work for the project in exchange for low rent — and that this structure, not idealism, is why it has lasted.
- [taxicabjesus](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50675258): First-hand account of the site's geography: off I-17 at Cordes Junction, midway between Phoenix and Flagstaff, unincorporated to avoid regulators, in barren high desert in the Prescott mountains' rain shadow.

## 6. Sydney Fire Ant Infestation {#story-6}

ABC News reports that New South Wales authorities are expanding their response to fire ants in Sydney after surveillance around a treated nest in Auburn revealed a larger, more complex infestation. The incursion was first detected on 23 September at the Enfield Intermodal Logistics Centre in the inner west; both sites are suspected to stem from imported container goods, likely from China, though they are not believed to be linked. NSW Agriculture Minister Tara Moriarty says a task force is being formed, a biosecurity order covering soil, turf, mulch and building materials is expected by Friday, extra officers and detection dogs are being deployed, and more than 900 public reports of suspicious nests have come in. The ants are up to 6 mm long, aggressive, and their stings can cause pustules and, in rare cases, hospitalisation or death; the public is told to report but not destroy nests.

**Discussion**: A large share of the thread is jokes — SimAnt references, an Avatar 'Fire Nation' riff, and a meta-argument about whether such internet tropes belong on HN, with one commenter quoting the guidelines and others replying that HN has always been 'techy Reddit'. The substantive discussion is about Australian biosecurity: several Australians praise the country's aggressive import controls as a legacy of protecting agriculture and native species, and note how lax European border checks feel by comparison. The liveliest exchange concerns Queensland's fire ant eradication program — one commenter blames chronic underfunding of the bait program 20 years ago, then corrects himself with figures (under $150m in 2001, about $1.2b spent to date, still failed), while another attributes the failure to management-style plans, budget pressure and perverse incentives to keep programs running, and points to the 2019 crazy yellow ant find at Airlie Beach and a recent detection in Mackay as evidence of the same pattern. - [anenefan](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50651330): An Australian commenter with local knowledge describes two inspectors covering all shipping for a whole coastal region in the 1990s, argues management plans beat eradication plans because they suit budget cycles and create incentives to prolong programs, and links recent crazy yellow ant detections at Airlie Beach and Mackay.

## 7. Citroën Hydraulic System {#story-7}
*#10 on HN · [citroenet.org.uk](https://www.citroenet.org.uk/miscellaneous/hydraulics/hydraulics-1.html) · 181 points · 57 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50662313)*

A long-standing enthusiast page from citroenet.org.uk explaining Citroën's hydropneumatic ('oléopneumatique') suspension: nitrogen-filled spheres act as the spring while hydraulic fluid, pressurized by an engine-driven pump at 150–180 bar, handles damping, self-levelling and ride-height adjustment. It traces the system from its 1954 debut on the Traction Avant through the 1955 DS 19 to the GS, CX, XM, Xantia (including the Activa's anti-roll jacks) and C6, notes licensing to Rolls-Royce, Mercedes-Benz and Berliet trucks, and credits the mid-sixties switch to mineral LHM fluid with ending the early DS's seal and hygroscopic-fluid failures. It also lists weak points — body roll in the basic system, harshness at low speed, road noise, pitching, and spheres losing nitrogen after roughly 60,000–100,000 km — and details load-proportional rear braking fed from the rear suspension spheres.

**Discussion**: The thread is dominated by owners trading war stories: jacquesm has run a 2CV, three DS/IDs, two Xantias and an XM, and describes loading a DS with concrete tiles until the rear end went into an undamped bouncing oscillation; others recall the car rising on startup, the four ride-height positions, and changing a wheel without a jack. On reliability the thread splits: one commenter argues every active hydraulic suspension has been a maintenance nightmare and dangerous to work on at high pressure, while long-term Citroën owners counter that spheres and pipes are cheap, rarely fail and are easier than chasing broken coil springs and worn shocks — and that cost and volume economics, not fragility, explain why the layout never spread. The Xantia Activa's famous moose-test result is defended by an owner who says failed replication videos used cars whose Activa anti-roll system wasn't working, though he concedes the 'greatest of all time' framing is a stretch and parts are getting scarce. Side threads cover Mercedes' rear load-levelling, Bose's abandoned electromagnetic suspension versus Porsche/BYD active systems, and film appearances (the Petit-Clamart attack on de Gaulle, Louis de Funès, Gattaca, Highlander).

**Worth reading**
- [Svip](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50671877): An owner of a 1974 DS 23 and a 1998 Xantia Activa V6 explains that pre-Xantia cars shared one hydraulic circuit with brakes and steering (a leak could cost all three, likely illegal under modern EU rules), and why the load-sensing height corrector made the mushroom-shaped brake pedal feel strange.
- [KennyBlanken](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50672792): The strongest skeptic case: nearly every car with active hydraulic suspension has been a reliability and maintenance nightmare, parts are expensive, all such systems fail with age, and the high pressures make them dangerous to service.
- [yason](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50675559): A detailed rebuttal from a 25-year owner of several hydraulic and conventional Citroëns: spheres last years, hoses and pipes have been rare and cheap fixes, he has changed far more brake pipes than hydraulic ones, and he'd choose hydropneumatic 10-to-1 over steel springs.

## 9. 9/UX: a Macintosh-inspired desktop for 9front {#story-9}
*#14 on HN · [progrium.github.io](https://progrium.github.io/9ux/) · 117 points · 27 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50672008)*

Jeff Lindsay (progrium) published 9/UX v0.1, a Macintosh/A/UX-style spatial desktop for 9front built the Plan 9 way: a rio fork (rio -u) adds a global menu bar, title-bar chrome and a drag broker under /dev/ux; libux provides windows, menus, dialogs and controls, each app exporting its control tree over 9P (echo activate > .../ctl presses a button), with uxd, uxmeta, resfs and clipd handling aggregation, ndb metadata, classic ICN#/menu/dialog resources and a typed clipboard. It is not an emulator or A/UX reimplementation — stock rio programs (acme, sam, rc, nested rio) keep running alongside — uses a 1-bit, 16-colour palette (butter, cream, olive, greygreen) drawn in whole pixels, ships only original graphics, is MIT licensed, and is tested by embedding h9 headless with injected mouse/keyboard input driving the 9P files. The author warns interfaces will change without notice.

**Discussion**: The thread is broadly warm about Plan 9/9front getting attention, with commenters praising the decision to build h9-based automated GUI testing on 9P and arguing a 20%-more-conventional UI could have changed Plan 9's fortunes; anthk goes furthest, claiming slightly tweaked rio/libdraw would have ended Linux and BSD desktop use overnight, while others who actually tried Plan 9 (three-button mouse and all) say the mouse-chord-heavy workflow is the real barrier, not the look. The sharp disagreement is about AI: one commenter dismisses the project as AI-generated '90s UI' work that violates 9front's no-AI policy, and others pile on the prose ('every single sentence has one of those claudisms'), with a Pangram check reported as 100% AI-generated and the potato-shaped radio buttons held up as evidence. Defenders counter that nobody is forced to use a downstream hobby project, that the 'go away' line is a nod to the 9front FQA, and that reflexive AI dismissal is its own problem. Side threads note that Inferno is still shipping (a release in April of this year), that rio's minimalism is an acquired taste some prefer to anything else, and that classic Mac fans like the look.
