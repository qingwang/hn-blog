## 1. Bleached corals thrive within days when touching healthy neighbour {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03266-x) · 130 points · 17 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50126253)*

A Nature news piece on a Science Advances paper (14 October 2026, Collins et al., Sci. Adv. 12, eaeh1580) describing lab experiments with the coral Pocillopora damicornis. In ten bleached/healthy fragment pairs, the five pairs placed in direct contact regrew their Symbiodiniaceae — symbiont populations doubling every four days by week two, with full recovery within eight weeks, and the bleached branch able to recover on its own after just a week of contact. The five pairs separated by a few millimetres died after 20 weeks. The transfer works through a tissue bridge that only forms between fragments of the same genet, so fragments from different species or even different individuals of the same species did not fuse or heal.

**Discussion**: The thread is small and the practical limitation dominates: the top commenter notes the result only applies to genetically identical clones, so it is of limited use in the wild, and the article quote confirming that different species and even different individuals of the same species fail to fuse is cited back at one commenter who misread the study as merely untested on other cases. Others are more optimistic, suggesting a bank of coral clones tagged to each reef location, and the notion of cloning to restore wild creatures draws general agreement. A separate subthread veers into Nikola Tesla, dark matter and consciousness as an explanation for the recovery; it is challenged by two commenters asking how the corals relate and why a healthy fragment’s frequency would win, and finally shut down by someone stating plainly that Tesla’s resonance theory has nothing to do with dark matter, consciousness or living matter — the poster concedes they are not an expert.

**Worth reading**
- [Okkef](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50134316): Raises the key practical caveat: the healing only occurs between genetically identical fragments, so it does not translate directly to reef restoration.
- [ceejayoz](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50134633): Quotes the article to correct the assumption that cross-genet transfer simply had not been tested — the study found it does not happen at all.
- [aappleby](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50134640): Blunt rebuttal to the Tesla/dark-matter tangent: Tesla’s resonance theory has nothing to do with dark matter, consciousness or living matter.

## 2. Source code of the Estonian online voting system {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/valimised/ivxv) · 80 points · 48 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50127586)*

The repository valimised/ivxv publishes the source code of Estonia's legally binding internet voting system for public review. The README states the repo is not used for active development but is kept in sync with what is actually run in elections, supervised by Estonia's State Electoral Office, and points to valimised.ee for more. Commenters note the code appears to have been rewritten in Go, having previously been Java, and that it has gone nearly a year without updates because the repo is refreshed around elections rather than continuously.

**Discussion**: The thread is overwhelmingly a referendum on whether any electronic or online voting can be trustworthy, not on the Estonian code itself. One camp (cryptonector, atoav, franga2000, mmooss) argues software alone can never be sufficient — the Trusting Trust problem, unverifiable firmware and object code mean you can only check what machines did via paper ballots and timely random-sample manual audits — and that an election's real requirement is publicly agreeable consent, which computers undermine rather than strengthen. The other side (sigmoid10, himata4113) says modern cryptography does solve ballot secrecy plus verifiability simultaneously and calls the mutual-exclusivity claim outdated, citing the vocdoni/davinci-paper implementation; devin adds that ElectionGuard has already been used in real US elections since 2020. Practitioners push back on both extremes: a commenter citing IACR's voided election after a trustee lost a private key illustrates that crypto's failure modes are catastrophic and trust-eroding, while sam1714 and others argue online channels genuinely help overseas, military and disabled voters (25 US states return ballots electronically, 15 allow internet voting for some cases). Estonian commenters defend the local system as part of a high-trust e-identity society, note a separate verification app and OSCE observer reports, and argue that source availability plus verifiable builds is one link in a chain of trust rather than a silver bullet. No consensus emerges; even the mutual-exclusivity claim drawn from the comment threads is directly contested.

**Worth reading**
- [mariofdistrust](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50134715): Recalls that the IACR had to void its own cryptographic election after a trustee lost their private key, illustrating how even experts' online voting can fail catastrophically and destroy trust.
- [juhanima](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50134770): An Estonian commenter argues the source release is one link in a chain of trust and credits Estonia's high-trust e-identity society, while conceding he personally prefers manual paper systems.
- [aramend](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50134582): First-hand account of using Estonia's separate i-vote verification tool to confirm one's vote was registered, plus a link to the OSCE/ODIHR observer report on the last parliamentary election.

## 3. The Commodore 64 hardware simulator: break chips and pins, see the results {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/antirez/c64-hw-simulator) · 80 points · 13 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50100366)*

The linked repo is antirez’s c64-hw-sim, a pin-level simulation of the Commodore 64 motherboard (ASSY 250407) built from bwack’s KiCad board replica — 216 parts and 249 nets — with chip cores taken from floooh’s chips. You can lift a chip leg, short a pin to GND or +5V, pull a chip from its socket or open a resistor, and the resulting garbage on screen is computed from the circuit rather than replayed from a known-fault table; tests/faults.sh covers 31 common faults whose expected symptoms were written down before the simulator was run. It is explicitly not a playable emulator: a real C64 is simulated at 31% of real speed on an Apple M3 Max, 39% on an AMD Ryzen AI Max+ PRO 495 and 48% on an M5 Max, and the analog side (video output, SID filter, power supply, dot-clock PLL) plus bridges and internal chip faults are not simulated.

## 4. Furifont, a Japanese font that shows furigana {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [furifont.antfu.me](https://furifont.antfu.me/) · 153 points · 33 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50100994)*

Furifont (furifont.antfu.me) is a web font project that puts furigana readings above kanji by abusing OpenType ligatures (合字), so the ruby-style annotation happens inside the font/shaping engine rather than in the page markup. The linked page is JavaScript-driven and its text could not be fetched, so the details below come from the title and the comment thread. Commenters describe a demo page with a Japanese and a Chinese version; the practical selling point is furigana everywhere, including on content that does not support ruby tags at all.

*Article not read: page had almost no readable text (probably needs JavaScript or a login)*

**Discussion**: The dominant view is that the engineering is clever but the feature is fundamentally unreliable: Japanese kanji readings are heavily context-dependent, some characters have several readings (two for the modal character, up to six for others, before counting name readings), so a static font dictionary cannot pick correctly, and commenters note Google Translate's own romanisation guides are frequently wrong. A native Japanese speaker praises the font but immediately finds a mistake on the demo page itself, where the compound 合字 only gets じ over 字 and no reading on 合, because the compound is missing from the dictionary and 合 has too many readings to fall back. Others counter that this is a solved capability — you can embed an LLM in a shaping engine, e.g. llama.ttf — while one replies with the Jurassic Park line about whether you should. Several report concrete bugs, notably that furigana goes wrong when a word is split across a line wrap (大学 broken between lines), and there is a long side argument about ruby tags: the skeptics say you would just use <ruby> when you control the content, while the project's defenders argue the point is exactly that it works where ruby does not, such as sideloaded Kindle ebooks and arbitrary web pages, and that automatic beats manual. Rough consensus: a neat demo and a genuine crutch for learners, not a trustworthy reading aid; other threads note a glyph-selection bug where Latin text rendered with Chinese-style forms due to missing language annotation, and the fact that TrueType's hinting VM makes this kind of trick possible at all.

**Worth reading**
- [TazeTSchnitzel](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50134367): The main counter-argument: kanji readings are so context- and name-dependent that no font can do this well without stuffing an entire LLM inside it.
- [shun_dev](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50127385): A native Japanese speaker points to a concrete misreading in the demo itself — 合字 gets only じ over 字 instead of ごうじ, because the compound is not in the dictionary and 合 has several readings.
- [jwolfe](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50133186): First-hand bug report: when 大学 is split across a line wrap, the font emits the wrong furigana.

## 5. Dam removals are bringing fish back across the northeastern United States {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [smithsonianmag.com](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/dam-removals-are-bringing-fish-back-across-the-northeastern-united-states-striking-down-one-tiny-barrier-at-a-time-180989679/) · 56 points · 10 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50126312)*

A Smithsonian Magazine feature arguing that while the demolition of big western dams like the Klamath (four dams, ~$500 million, 400 miles of reopened salmon habitat, free-flowing by August 2024) grabs headlines, the majority of US dam removals happen in the East. It cites more than 2,300 US dam removals since 1912 — most in the last 25 years — and claims 2024 reconnected over 2,500 miles of waterways, while at least 35,000 dams still block northeastern fish runs (9,000 in New York, 5,000 in Connecticut, 3,000 in Massachusetts). Reporting follows UNH fish biologist Nathan Furey on New Hampshire's Bellamy River, where two former Sawyer Woolen Mills dams came out in 2019–2020 and PIT-tag readers now track returning alewives; it also notes six million river herring returned to Maine's Penobscot in 2025, and that New England dams have cost the ecosystem nearly 20 billion pounds of alewives.

**Discussion**: Commenters largely welcome the trend but broaden the frame beyond hydropower. One points to a 2025 development the article misses: Brookfield Renewable agreed to sell four dams on Maine's Kennebec River, potentially reopening spawning grounds for Atlantic salmon, which they call possibly a bigger breakthrough than the alewife restoration described. Others push back on the energy tradeoff — one argues that lost hydro generation is simply offset by fossil-fuel plants, and another counters that most removed dams were in disrepair, geotechnically unsound or silted up and generated little power anyway, so a few dozen rooftop solar installs would outproduce them. A practitioner thread notes small dams and undershot water wheels can coexist with fish passage, that the real culprits are mid-sized turbines cutting off large watersheds, and that entrenched property and water rights make removal fights bitter. The thread also corrects the article's implicit framing that dams are only about electricity: commenters list flood control, summer agricultural storage, and the community identity of reservoirs like California's Lake Pillsbury.

**Worth reading**
- [glenstein](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50134279): Points to a 2025 deal in which Brookfield Renewable agreed to sell four Kennebec River dams, potentially letting Atlantic salmon return upstream — a newer and possibly larger win than the article's alewife story, with a link to Maine Monitor coverage.
- [lukeify](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50134632): Counters the claim that removals sacrifice clean energy: most of these dams were falling apart, geotechnically troubled or silted up and barely generated power, so a few dozen rooftop solar arrays would produce more.
- [metalman](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50129483): A first-hand account of dam-removal fights: small dams and undershot wheels can pass fish, mid-sized turbines are the real barrier, and entrenched water rights make opposition vicious.

## 6. Making chicken transparent using Dorito dust {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [chillphysicsenjoyer.substack.com](https://chillphysicsenjoyer.substack.com/p/making-chicken-transparent-using) · 203 points · 57 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50131213)*

A Substack post from a hobbyist scientist ('casualphysics' of Chill Physics Enjoyer) attempting to replicate the 2024 paper 「Achieving optical transparency in live animals with absorbing molecules」, which made tissue transparent by soaking it in tartrazine, the yellow-orange food dye (E102) used in Doritos. The author soaked 2 mm slices of Tesco chicken breast in ~0.19 M tartrazine solution for 6–12 hours at room temperature — a scaled-down version of the paper's 0.62 M at 40 °C with a shaker — and got only partial transparency: grid lines visible through the sample, which took on a gummy-bear look. The highest concentration tried (~0.56 M) failed when the tartrazine crystallised into a crust; the control in tap water stayed opaque. The explanatory half walks through why tissue is opaque (absorption plus light scattering from refractive-index mismatches) and how tartrazine raises water's refractive index of 1.33 toward the ~1.45 of proteins and lipids.

**Discussion**: Much of the thread is jokes (Doritos-flavoured chicken as the origin story, a chicken that turned transparent and vanished, the Kevin Bacon Hollow Man gag), but several comments add real substance. Commenters correct the framing that this is crushed Doritos: it is pure tartrazine, chosen for its high water solubility and low reactivity, and one quotes the cited paper noting EFSA found no adverse effects up to 2 g/kg body weight. A correction exchange follows on EU rules — tartrazine is not banned, but products containing it must carry the 「may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children」 warning, and one commenter who had claimed E102 was banned in Europe conceded his information dated from 2014. On the science, commenters point out that haemoglobin and retinal pigments are intrinsically absorbing and can never be made transparent, so transparent frogs and fish still have opaque blood and eyes, and that the required dye concentration is roughly 50x that of tartrazine in orange squash, so it is not something you'd drink — gut bacteria also cleave the molecule. The author replies in-thread, thanking one commenter for the sous-vide suggestion (up to 72 hours without turning to mush) and defending the technique as simply changing the refractive index of water. Practical replication tips include partially freezing the chicken before slicing, buying a used vibratome for under $100 on eBay, and matching the refractive index of surrounding water to reduce surface-roughness scattering; a sugar solution was floated as a cheaper alternative but shot down because 80% sugar only reaches ~1.44. A side argument about processed food and the 'naturalistic fallacy' runs underneath, with one side calling Doritos-as-food unsettling and the other noting salmonella kills about 420 people a year in the US, far more than a food dye.

**Worth reading**
- [adrian_b](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50132551): Explains that vertebrate blood (haemoglobin) and the pigmented retina can never be made transparent, so transparent animals still have opaque eyes and vessels — only scattering-based opacity can be fixed.
- [_Microft](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50132255): A detailed technical suggestion: immerse the sample and grid in water and freeze the meat before cutting, since surface roughness is likely scattering light and costing transparency.
- [layer8](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50132157): Corrects the widespread claim that tartrazine/E102 is banned in the EU — it is legal but must carry the child-activity warning label, which is why producers avoid it.

Several people hunted for the source, found it under the Obsidian GitHub org (with a request that open source projects put a GitHub icon on their landing page), and a long subthread jokes about the word 「pele」 meaning mouse in Latvian, skin in Portuguese, a footballer in Brazil and worse in Slovak, with a note that the French 「pelle」 (spade) only matches with an accent on the final é. **Worth reading**

## 10. Development statistics for the 7.2 kernel {#story-10}
*#12 on HN · [lwn.net](https://lwn.net/Articles/1088776/) · 106 points · 76 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50132950)*

An LWN analysis of the 7.2 kernel release (shipped August 17 after Linus Torvalds noted more fixes arriving than he would have liked), drawing on the subscriber-only LWN Kernel Source Database. It reports 16,418 non-merge commits — the second-busiest cycle ever after 6.7, but broader-based — from a record 2,652 developers, including a record 613 first-timers; nearly 600,000 lines added; 249 identified employers; and 48% of commits carrying Reviewed-by versus just over 8% with Tested-by. The LLM angle dominates: 1,111 changes (<7%) carry Assisted-by tags versus 301 in 7.1, 252 patches credit the Sashiko review system, and commits with Fixes tags have jumped sharply to 4,830, which the article reads as the signature of LLMs being turned loose on bug hunting — raising the question of whether a growing pile of kernel code is understood by anyone.

**Discussion**: Many commenters report the pattern in their own projects: agents quietly work around bugs instead of reporting them, so bug counts fall and maintainers get large, hard-to-review AI-generated pull requests; one suggests simply instructing agents to surface found bugs so a human can report them upstream. A large subthread debates Linus Torvalds's stance that competence is about the person, not the tool — his compiler analogy is attacked as invalid because compilers are deterministic, while defenders argue it is unfair to dismiss LLM-assisted work wholesale, and skeptics insist that in practice LLM use is anti-correlated with understanding. Others note that the article offers no control group, since historical numbers reflect changed incentives as much as LLM adoption, and one commenter points to Greg KH's talk on handling the flood of LLM patches. A related thread claims Linus himself submitted a Claude-assisted patch containing a data race caught by reviewers; replies counter that it was a subtle concurrency bug any expert could make and not evidence of vibe-coding. Housekeeping threads are also prominent: HN moderators reverted the editorialized submission title, and one user reports downgrading a Slackware box from 7.3-rc7 to 7.2.10 because /dev/input/events went missing.

**Worth reading**
