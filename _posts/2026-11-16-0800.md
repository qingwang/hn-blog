## 1. How to make almost anything {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [pub.cba.mit.edu](https://pub.cba.mit.edu/863.26/site/) · 147 points · 26 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50514470)*

The page is the bare syllabus for MIT Media Lab's fall course 863 (the long-running 'How to Make Almost Anything' fab course associated with Neil Gershenfeld), fetched from pub.cba.mit.edu/863.26. It is almost entirely a dated schedule: weekday lectures on computer-aided design, computer-controlled cutting, electronics design, networking and interface programming, plus 5-6pm Thursday recitations on version control, parametric design, EDA, debugging, CAM, Edge AI, motor and motion control, machine building, molding and casting, and mesh networks, ending with final assignments and orders on 12/04. There is essentially no prose on the page, which is itself the main complaint in the thread.

**Discussion**: The thread splits cleanly on breadth versus depth. Defenders argue that a survey course is valuable precisely because it tells students what exists — one commenter likens it to The New Turing Omnibus, another says knowing the vocabulary and where to dig is what lets engineers be creative later, and says the point is a BS detector rather than expertise; others note many students arrive non-technical and the real learning happens in the lab, and point to student portfolios as evidence it works. Skeptics, led by mitchweaver, call the syllabus a list of buzzwords that crams something like 50 CAD tools into part of one lecture, and dismiss the lowercase styling and open-source framing as unserious. A separate subthread argues LLMs are underused because people ask them to do tasks instead of build reusable tools, with a counter that the generated scripts are throwaway and there is no vendor incentive to make models generalize; several people report LLMs producing usable STLs and 3D prints. Practical notes: the 2023 iteration has lecture videos, and the hardest real-world gaps named are mechanical DFM, automating QA, and at-scale production know-how that mostly has to be learned by running a line.

**Worth reading**
- [Animats](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50514731): Animats argues the pace is insane and that the projects are less impressive than they look — making your own PC board is easy with the right tools — and compares it to TechShop's glory days, where he took ~13 classes and learned that actually building things took far longer than the machine-operation classes.
- [sampullman](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50515144): A practitioner argues production know-how resists teaching because every factory has different capabilities, and names mechanical DFM and automated verification/QA as the genuinely hard parts of going from prototype to product.

## 2. Universal XSS in uBlock Origin from website-driven element picker {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [iter.ca](https://iter.ca/post/ubo-uxss/) · 298 points · 83 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50513160)*

A writeup on iter.ca documents a one-click universal XSS in uBlock Origin's Firefox MV2 build, with a similar but less severe issue in uBO Lite, that let a malicious site add arbitrary trusted filters and run JavaScript in the context of any site. The attack leaked the per-install moz-extension UUID by hooking the XHR made inside uBO's default no-fetch-if rule for adsbygoogle, embedded the element picker in an invisible iframe, and abused the picker's resultsetDetails opt field with a newline to append a trusted scriptlet filter. It is fixed; the author says GPT-5.6 Sol found the bug, Claude wrote most of the PoC code, and he wrote the prose.

**Discussion**: Commenters largely accept the bug but disagree about where the fault lies: several argue that extensions rendering UI inside the page's DOM is inherently risky, citing password-manager autofill leaks and 1Password's prism.js injection that broke code blocks across sites, and esprehn says he regrets that a Top Layer-based extension overlay API was never shipped. Others say the impact is overstated — one argues the extension UI sits in a sandboxed iframe with the same origin boundary as any embedded page — while another notes the leaked per-install UUID is itself a tracking identifier. Practical replies sort out patching: the advisory lists uBO 1.75.4 as fixed and uBO Lite 2026.1110.1816, some users had to update twice or found themselves still on older builds, and AdNauseam, a uBO fork, appears affected and fixed by a similar commit. A side thread about the AI disclosure splits between praise for the honesty and one complaint that a vulnerability this simple should not have needed AI.

**Worth reading**
- [esprehn](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50514615): esprehn says he regrets not pushing harder for an extension overlay API based on Top Layer, and notes that DevTools uses a separate Overlay Page for exactly this reason.
- [ethersteeds](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50513460): ethersteeds points out that the leaked per-install UUID can serve as a tracking identifier, calling the fetch('/adsbygoogle.js') leak an 'ironic disaster' and linking a prior HN thread on Firefox extension UUID leakage.
- [AlexAplin](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50514082): AlexAplin corrects the version discussion with the advisory link: uBO 1.75.2 and lower are affected, patched in 1.75.4.

## 4. Old Bitly links are being hijacked {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [ransomnews.com](https://ransomnews.com/burger-king-tweet-bitly-link-hijack-2026/) · 259 points · 138 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50511395)*

A ransomnews.com investigation published 15 November 2026 showing that expired Bitly back-halves (the part after the slash) are being re-registered by strangers, so decades-old tweets suddenly point somewhere else. A Burger King tweet from 24 July 2011 whose link (bit.ly/19BFiM) was re-created at 05:32 UTC that morning now opened Wikipedia's list of Nazi concentration camps, and a McDonald's link from 2011 (bit.ly/mLWJO0, re-created 18 minutes earlier) opened the September 11 article; of 97 Bitly links in Trump tweets from 2009-2013, 57 are no longer the original, 49 of them re-created in a 17-minute run on 26 May 2023. Bitly confirmed the issue on X at 19:13 UTC, said it suspended the accounts involved and disabled the affected links, and CNN reported old posts from Bill Gates, Justin Trudeau, Mike Pence, the FBI and the Department of Defense were hit too — contradicting Bitly's own help page, which says back-halves 'cannot be re-used'.

**Discussion**: Most of the thread is a referendum on link shorteners as a category: critics argue they add a rented, revocable failure point that outlives anyone's memory of why the link existed, while defenders point to length-limited media, print and SMS, and click analytics as the real reasons marketing departments buy them. The most-repeated advice is to run a shortener on your own domain — one commenter describes a self-hosted 301 service used purely to keep QR codes small so they scan on bad phones — with others noting marketing teams routinely sign up for outside services without ever talking to the people who could set up DNS. Several commenters are surprised Bitly is big enough to have a Trust and Safety team, and one supplies numbers: 300-400 employees and over $100M ARR as of 2023, mostly from marketing analytics and interstitial ads. Side threads cover paid 'dynamic' QR code services that hijack otherwise-static codes behind a subscription paywall, and the analogous dangling-CNAME attack where spammers buy lapsed domains still referenced by reputable organizations. A recurring complaint is that the article itself reads like AI output that buries a simple explanation — expired link names get freed and re-registered — and there is the usual long digression about calling the site Twitter rather than X.

**Worth reading**
- [wdr1](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50513472): Supplies concrete scale for Bitly: 300-400 employees and, per its last public statement in 2023, more than $100M ARR — which reframes the hijacking as a governance failure at a substantial company rather than a hobby project.
- [101008](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50513319): First-hand anecdote: a well-known author published a book containing an unregistered bit.ly link as a fake tweet, and the commenter caught it on release day and redirected it to a page about the book before anyone malicious did.
- [g-b-r](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50511688): Describes the same class of bug in DNS — lapsed domains still referenced by CNAMEs on reputable sites like universities, later bought by spammers so links pass as safe — with advice to audit and monitor CNAME targets.

## 7. Stop Filming in Log {#story-7}
*#7 on HN · [digitalfilms.wordpress.com](https://digitalfilms.wordpress.com/2026/11/15/stop-filming-in-log/) · 39 points · 23 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50514093)*

A working colorist's blog post arguing that shooting in camera log has become a crutch: videographers underexpose log clips assuming they will 'fix it in post', even though log is not the same thing as film and film negative has no log or raw equivalent. The post traces the history (RED's camera raw in 2007, ARRI's Log-C on the Alexa in 2010) and says the ARRI and Panasonic log implementations grade well while most copycat profiles do not, calls Sony's lineup (S-Log iterations, s709, S-Cinetone, Rec. 709) confusing, and recommends a correctly lit, low-contrast Rec. 709 profile instead. It also argues video HDR is largely a Dolby/Canon display-marketing push that produces painful images unlike human vision, and says not to shoot HDR unless asked to deliver it.

**Discussion**: Most commenters were unconvinced by the framing. Several argue the post never names a concrete technical failure: log can be converted back to Rec. 709 to match what you would have recorded in-camera, so the real complaint is just that people mis-expose when they think post will save them — a point that could have been one paragraph. Others defend log on precisely the author's own terms: a filmmaker says they shot log for wider gamut and skipped in-camera processing while still nailing exposure, and a more technical commenter explains log exists to avoid quantization and clipping, with the inverse OETF applied in the grade and no need to bake in Rec. 709. A 24-bit-audio analogy runs through the thread (log squeezes latitude out of 8-bit H.264 the way high-bit-depth masters preserve headroom), though one reply disputes it because log raises shadow noise. A grain-of-salt sensitometry comment concedes you needn't capture in log — Canon's Wide DR is offered as an alternative — but warns many things, including white balance, are better done in-camera. Side threads note that color management is a minefield of contradictory internet advice, that DaVinci Resolve only recently added automatic color space detection, and that the post itself gets some facts wrong.

**Worth reading**
- [wyager](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50514437): Argues log's purpose is reducing quantization and clipping loss, not a look: apply the inverse OETF of HLG or S-Log3 and grade in linear or ACEScct, so there is no need to squash to Rec. 709 in camera.
- [jiggawatts](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50514869): Notes video formats historically carried no gamut metadata, so log required manually undoing the mapping — and that DaVinci Resolve, a colorist tool, only got automatic color space detection a couple of versions ago, unlike still photography.
- [enneff](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50514522): A filmmaker's first-hand rebuttal: they shot log for wider gamut and no in-camera processing while still trying to nail exposure, purely to keep adjustment latitude without image quality loss.

## 8. Protomaps: Self-hosting map tiles has gotten cheap and easy {#story-8}
*#8 on HN · [bruceroettgers.eu](https://bruceroettgers.eu/blog/protomaps/) · 294 points · 54 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50509837)*

A blog post by Bruce Roettgers, a CS student at ETH Zurich, walking through hosting the Protomaps world basemap as a single PMTiles file — one archive read via HTTP Range requests — on a CDN such as Bunny.net, then rendering it with MapLibre GL plus the pmtiles npm package. His numbers: Mapbox charges $5 per 1k map loads past the free tier, while storing the 139GB world map with 5x replication costs about $5/month, and a Europe-to-city zoom session moves ~5MB (~$0.00005), making per-load costs potentially 100x cheaper. He adds practical hardening: an S3-compatible storage zone (the HTTP API upload timed out after 15 minutes), rclone copyurl to stream the file, CORS referrer restrictions, 'Optimize for large object delivery' so Range requests get cached, and throttling to 10kB/s after 10MB per request to deter whole-file scrapers.

**Discussion**: The thread is largely a chorus of practitioners who already do this: PMTiles on Cloudflare R2 or S3 behind Workers, CloudFront or a small cache, running around $2/month for personal projects (a hiking map with D1 and Workers, Streeter, landstats.com parcel maps, lowpolyearth, bowmaps, aportee.ca, safemap.io, geocode.xyz), with several saying they moved off Google Maps or MapTiler after surprise bills — one cites 150 €/month on MapTiler for Cartes.app, another notes Mapbox has no way to cap spending. Alternatives get named repeatedly: OpenFreeMap, which runs no tile server at all (nginx serving a Btrfs image of ~300 million hard-linked files) and is free with no API keys, plus VersaTiles and Tuiles en Libertés; a caveat is that OpenFreeMap inherits OpenMapTiles' stricter attribution rules and MapTiler upselling. The main pushback is performance: one commenter reports direct-to-S3 Range requests give unpredictable latency because neither browsers nor servers cache ranges granularly, so every tile is a high-latency round trip, and he put a Martin tile server in front instead; others counter that latency varies by provider (100-300ms on Bunny) and that Cloudflare now handles origin range requests, though the author notes Cloudflare's range caching is still limited by overall file size. Scope is also contested — one developer who built a satellite mosaic stopped at zoom 17 because global high-res imagery runs to hundreds of TB, outside 'stick it on R2' territory. A side thread flags a Malwarebytes warning on the author's Plausible analytics endpoint, which he says is just his self-hosted analytics proxy; the flagger concedes it was probably nothing.

**Worth reading**
- [perrygeo](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50510948): First-hand warning that going straight to cloud storage with the pmtiles:// protocol gave unpredictable latency and poor Range caching, so he fronted it with a Martin tile server for better real-world performance.
- [motoroco](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50511852): A developer who built a North America satellite mosaic explains where this approach breaks: storage explodes toward hundreds of TB at high zoom, and the hard part was seamless mosaicking, cloud/haze filtering and seasonal consistency, with tile builds taking weeks.
- [boramalper](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50512020): Points to OpenFreeMap as a free, no-API-key alternative, noting its unusual stack: no tile server, just nginx serving a Btrfs image containing 300 million hard-linked files.

## 10. A short explainer on how El Niño works [video] {#story-10}
