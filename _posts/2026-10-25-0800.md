## 1. We should be more ambitious with the software we build {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [danluu.com](https://danluu.com/ambitious/) · 360 points · 307 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50225695)*

Dan Luu argues that AI coding tools have made previously unreasonable projects cheap enough to attempt, using Michael Malis's pgrust (a Rust rewrite of Postgres) as his example — he says its testing has surfaced real Postgres bugs (an SP-GiST numeric bug, a JSONPath format-cache bug, a pgcrypto bug, dictionary-loader memory-safety bugs, a money overflow bug). Luu lists his own low-effort spinoffs — a regex engine, a ripgrep fork, a Rust interpreter giving ~50x faster cold builds and 2x warm, and an Azul board-game AI that took ~5 hours to reach superhuman — and claims that with GPT-6 Astra, implementing zstd passes registered tests over 99% of the time for about $1. He notes up front that he now writes posts in half an hour without rigorous benchmarking, and admits he didn't look into pgrust himself but trusted Malis personally.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people listing genuinely large AI-assisted builds (a from-scratch JS engine that beats QuickJS and surpasses V8/JSC on test262, a C++ compiler 7x faster than gcc, a Python interpreter in Rust, a retro BASIC OS for ESP32, a 1995 game remake) and skeptics who say faster ripgrep or a Rust interpreter is not what 「ambitious」 means — the real bottleneck is ideas, taste and post-MVP product work, not code volume. Several practitioners report a characteristic failure mode: models churn without progress once codebase complexity crosses a threshold, and default to local patches and deep if-else nesting instead of simplifying; one recounts Opus 5.5 building a fragile log-scraping hack, another that it proposed a Python load balancer where haproxy won outright. The dominant correction, however, is that pgrust was removed from ClickBench days earlier for allegedly hand-rolling per-query code, caching results and being unable to run queries outside the benchmarked set — commenters call Luu's trust-based defense hand-wavy, and Malis shows up to argue the hand-rolled paths were test-only, that a PGRUST_GRAVITY_REPORT flag proves the general query path was used, and that the real limitation was narrow SQL support. A separate subthread mocks the site's ~full-width text and trades CSS one-liners (max-width, bookmarklets, reader mode).

**Worth reading**
- [anonymous908213](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50226382): Lays out the ClickBench cheating allegation in detail — per-query hand-rolled code, caching, inability to run untested queries — and argues Luu's footnote defense is 「I know he would never cheat」 rather than evidence.
- [malisper](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50227869): Malis himself rebuts: the shipped binary logs every query's code path via PGRUST_GRAVITY_REPORT, all benchmark queries take the general path, no results cache is used, and the genuine flaw was only supporting scans, group-bys, sorts and filters.
- [malisper](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50226514): Malis describes his actual verification stack — a compiler from C and Rust to Lean plus LLM-written equivalence proofs covering ~70k lines of C, and says proof context is growing superlinearly, so he is looking at sharding approaches.

## 2. Cerebras Deploys Trillion-Parameter AI Models {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [cerebras.ai](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/how-we-deploy-trillion-parameter-frontier-models-on-cerebras) · 129 points · 42 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50227356)*

Cerebras' engineering blog walks through serving a roughly 3-trillion-parameter open model, Kimi K3, on its WSE-3T wafer-scale chips. Because each wafer has only 44 GB of on-chip SRAM, the 4-bit weights are sharded by layer: two linear-attention layers per wafer, one dense layer per wafer to leave room for KV cache, for 59 wafers in a pipeline connected by RDMA over Ethernet at about 2 microseconds per hop. Cerebras claims 1,000–2,000+ tokens per second with 256k-token requests, and cites Kimi K3 scores of 88.3% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and 91.2% on BrowseComp.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between fascination with the architecture and skepticism about whether anyone outside large enterprises can use it. A former subscriber says Cerebras' 1,000 tokens per second was unique but that its hosted Qwen 3.8 is now beaten on cost and performance by Mercury 3, leaving custom enterprise deals as the main reason to choose Cerebras; others note that the fast GPT-5.6 Sol endpoint is not generally available to developers or requires paying. Several commenters attack the benchmarking: the post cites full-precision benchmark numbers while deploying 4-bit weights, and one user says the small models actually exposed through the API are fast but often wrong. Hardware-side discussion covers why chips use square 2D meshes rather than hexagonal tilings, how pipeline parallelism differs from GPU batching, and whether KV cache could live in off-chip DRAM or on dedicated attention servers. Energy and reliability draw scrutiny too: one estimate puts the 59-wafer system near 1.18 MW and about 4.1 joules per token, while another commenter asks how a pipeline spanning 59 wafers handles a single failed wafer, since the model is effectively the rack.

**Worth reading**
- [2001zhaozhao](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50229280): A former Cerebras coding-plan subscriber gives a first-hand market view: the 1,000 TPS was once unique, but Qwen 3.8 is now outperformed on cost and speed by Mercury 3, so only enterprise custom deals make sense.
- [tgtweak](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50229054): Points out that Cerebras quotes full-precision benchmark results for Kimi K3 while actually deploying 4-bit quantized weights.
- [zhyblife99](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50227547): Raises the operational question the post skips: with the model spread across 59 wafers as one pipeline, how does Cerebras tolerate or drain a single failed wafer without taking the whole deployment down?

## 3. The Goose Programming Language {#story-3}
*#4 on HN · [aardappel.github.io](https://aardappel.github.io/goose/) · 361 points · 237 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50224100)*

Goose is a new systems language by Wouter van Oortmerssen (Aardappel, also author of Lobster, Amiga E, Cube 2/Sauerbraten and TreeSheets) whose memory model is a set of compiler-determined data stacks bump-allocated inside large reserved regions of a 64-bit address space, so references stay valid as arrays grow and scope exit is the free. It claims no allocator, GC, destructors, borrow checker, unsafe or undefined behaviour, inline variable-size records, narrow relative references that double as a serialization format, and worker threads that share nothing. The headline benchmark claim: across sixteen benchmarks it runs 3.3x faster than idiomatic C++, 1.16x faster than hand-optimized C++ and 1.12x faster than the best safe Rust, on 1.9x/1.3x/1.2x less memory; it compiles to a single C file, with a libtcc JIT for fast dev builds. The author states much of the compiler and docs were written by an LLM (Claude/Fable) under his direction, building on his Lobster compiler and a distilled style guide.

**Discussion**: The two biggest fights are the AI-written prose and the benchmarks. A large camp says the docs read as machine-generated and they cannot trust or even enjoy reading them; others reply that the tutorial is clear, ideas should be judged on merit, and the author openly documents the AI use — the author says he has already edited it and would rather people try the language than polish Claude-isms. On performance, a commenter notes the C++ benchmark files lack reserve()/with_capacity() and asks where the hand-optimized versions are; the author answers that the real hand-optimized files are private and the repo ones are decoys, which drew immediate pushback. A pointed technical critique argues the no-atomics, memcpy-through-queues threading model must lose badly for large messages (double write, cache misses) and for algorithms that benefit from atomics or locks, so the average-speed claim holds only where the programs do the same thing. On memory layout, practitioners press on how many stacks exist, page-granularity waste, fragmentation and hash tables of arbitrarily sized strings; the answers are pools, grow-only arrays and lifetime-grouped data, with the author conceding the language demands you rethink data organization. Side threads cover prior art (ML Kit region inference, Dart isolates), 32-bit/embedded/Wasm limitations (wasm64 without lazy commit won't work), compile speed (bounded by the C backend, ~3x slower under the JIT), and a dispute over whether the John Carmack tweet in the post text is an endorsement of Goose or just a reply to a concept — the author says Carmack clearly read the repo; others call the framing misleading.

**Worth reading**
- [Aardappel](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50225945): The author confirms the repo's C++/Rust benchmark files are not the hand-optimized ones — the real hand-optimized versions are private, so the 1.16x-over-optimized-C++ claim can't be checked.
- [nextaccountic](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50229056): Detailed argument that message-passing by memcpy forces a double write and that Rust programs using atomics or locks can beat Goose outright, so the speed claim is benchmark-scenario dependent.
- [groundzeros2015](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50225854): Pushback on the submission's framing: Carmack was replying to a concept, not describing experience with Goose, so the implicit endorsement is misleading.

## 4. SpeedCrunch 1.1 {#story-4}
*#5 on HN · [speedcrunch.org](https://www.speedcrunch.org/) · 130 points · 23 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50224210)*

SpeedCrunch 1.1 是这款免费、开源的高精度科学计算器的发布公告，适用于 Windows、macOS 和 Linux。它宣称支持最高 50 位精度、复数、140 多个函数、单位与常量、用户自定义变量、函数和单位、可编辑历史、标签页和分栏式的多会话、40 多种翻译和 40 多种配色方案。这是该项目大约十年来首次发布新版本，此前的版本是 0.12，社区将 1.0 和 1.1 视为长期沉寂后的重新启动。

**Discussion**: 长期用户普遍为这次重新发布感到高兴，好几个人表示自己已经每天使用它多年，主要用于快速草稿纸式计算和位运算与进制转换。主要的批评意见是界面退步：一位十年老用户抱怨输入框被放大到两倍、多出边框和空白，并且出现明显的输入延迟，结果他回退到了 0.12；反驳意见则认为界面总有改进空间，不满意的人可以不升级，而支持者指出新版保留了经典视图选项。有评论者注意到一个长期存在的运算顺序缺陷已修复：1/2(-9.8) 现在得到 -4.9，而旧版本给出 -0.05102040816326530612。另一些人认为该表达式本身就有歧义，应该直接报错，而不是悄悄按新规则求值。其余讨论分散在替代方案上（numbat、Android 上的 Hiper Calc Pro、PARI/GP）、Homebrew 的 cask 被禁用后能否恢复，以及一位用户收到疑似 AI 撰写但很有帮助的 GitHub issue 回复。

**Worth reading**

## 5. Small Programs and Languages {#story-5}
*#6 on HN · [ratfactor.com](https://ratfactor.com/cards/pl-small) · 98 points · 7 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50225674)*

A ratfactor.com card post arguing that miniature programs and tiny languages are not just recreation but evidence of a concept's minimum complexity — Kolmogorov complexity applied to software. It collects examples: Luke Jackson's 25-line ijk DOM-diffing library, Philippe Brochard's 46-byte Forth, Arthur Whitney's 122-line J interpreter （the 「Incunabulum」）, a kOS transcript describing Whitney's k with a GUI and window manager in a 90 Kb bootable image （≈60 lines of C）, William Byrd's Lisp-in-Lisp, Justine Tunney's 436-byte GC'd Lisp, Frank Force's 2 Kb 3D JS game, and Fabien Sanglard's business-card ray tracer. It also surveys small languages — Forth, Lisp, Tcl, Lua （27 pages of reference manual）, C — with Zig called out as deceptively large, quotes David Ungar that 「simplicity trumps expressiveness」, and ends on the psychological appeal of miniatures and 『microworlds』.

**Discussion**: Only seven comments, and they are additive rather than argumentative: nobody disputes the thesis, and the thread mostly becomes a place to post more small things. zzo38computer gives the most concrete contribution, listing uxn/varvara programs he wrote with exact byte counts — a 39-byte hex decoder, 125-byte JSON-to-NDJSON converter, 220-byte DER-to-TER converter, 1024-byte BLAKE2s, and a card game with graphics and sound in 1853 bytes — while noting that skipping error checking is a large part of what makes such programs short. WalterBright counters with a mainstream-history example: Hal Finney wrote a BASIC interpreter for the Mattel Intellivision in about 2K of ROM. shikaan says the post matches how he learns and links his own osle, an operating system that fits in a bootloader, as an exercise in tight constraints; bryancoxwell says the piece re-piqued his interest in software development. The rest is reading recommendations: rramadass points to Jon Bentley's Little Languages and Randy Kaplan's Constructing Language Processors for Little Languages, and anthk links t3x.org and JimTcl.

**Worth reading**
- [zzo38computer](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50227735): First-hand inventory of uxn programs with exact sizes — 39-byte hex decoder, 125-byte JSON-to-NDJSON, 1024-byte BLAKE2s, a 1853-byte card game — plus the honest caveat that omitting error checking is what buys most of the brevity.
- [WalterBright](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50228943): A piece of computing history: Hal Finney's BASIC interpreter for the Mattel Intellivision fit in roughly 2K of ROM.
- [shikaan](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50229144): A reader who works this way himself, linking osle, an operating system small enough to fit inside a bootloader.

## 6. Why Gopher in 2026? {#story-6}

**Discussion**: Commenters are broadly sympathetic but split on Gopher's substance. **Worth reading**

## 7. America has lost 200k of jobs in media, film and the arts. How much is AI? {#story-7}
*#8 on HN · [apricitas.io](https://www.apricitas.io/p/ai-and-the-fall-of-the-creative-class) · 328 points · 323 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50226419)*

An Apricitas essay by a writer with 80,000+ subscribers arguing that US creative industries have shed more than 200,000 jobs in four years, about 50,000 of them in the last year, the worst stretch since the 2001 and 2008 recessions. Movie and sound recording alone lost over 100,000 jobs — nearly a third of the sector — with employment at its lowest since 2008, while written publishing is down 70,000 and newspaper payrolls have fallen roughly 85% from their peak. The piece uses the Napster/Metallica era as a precedent: piracy devalued recordings, streaming replaced sales, and musicians survived on tours and merch — a live fallback Hollywood lacks. It notes 32% of arts/entertainment/recreation workers use generative AI versus 62% across all industries, and concedes the exact AI share is unprovable, closing that AI art's main impact will be cultural rather than macroeconomic.

**Discussion**: The dominant counter-argument is that AI is a scapegoat: commenters point to offshoring (production moving to Europe, Canada, Atlanta), state tax-incentive cuts, the 2023 strikes, the end of zero-interest-rate money, studio consolidation such as the Warner/Paramount merger, and above all the migration of eyeballs to TikTok and YouTube. Several cite a Stanford SIEPR policy brief finding AI has had minimal measurable macroeconomic effect so far, while others call that cope and point to Getty Images' bankruptcy and Stack Overflow's collapse. People with direct industry exposure split the difference: an Atlanta resident says film work simply went to Europe after Amazon and Netflix trained local crews during the pandemic, and one commenter reports VFX bids that were $500k now coming in at $50k. A recurring complaint is that the article never quantifies AI's share — one top comment asks outright whether it says how much — and that it ignores influencers, creators and the oversupply of content. Long side debates cover whether efficiency gains reach ordinary people (dueling FRED wage charts) and whether audiences have simply abandoned prestige film for short-form video.

**Worth reading**
- [echelon](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50227384): A Los Angeles-born commenter living in Atlanta says the jobs moved to Europe, not to AI: Amazon and Netflix built infrastructure and trained local crews there during the pandemic content boom, while Gen Z eyeballs went to TikTok.
- [alephnerd](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50227036): Argues the collapse is policy-driven — the 2023 strike, theatre shutdowns, and aggressive UK/Ireland/Poland/India production and VFX subsidies just as California, Georgia and New York cut theirs — and that revenue held up while costs became untenable.
- [ortusdux](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50227411): Anecdotal but concrete industry claim that SFX and animation job counts held steady while bids collapsed, with $500k jobs now quoted around $50k.

## 8. I Built a Stereo Vision Board for Hardware Hackers {#story-8}
*#9 on HN · [rootkitlabs.com](https://rootkitlabs.com/2026/10/06/i-built-a-stereo-vision-board-for-hardware-hackers/) · 65 points · 11 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50172432)*

A blog post by the maker of a cheap stereo depth-sensing board built around the Rockchip RV1103 — a single-core 1.1 GHz Cortex-A7 with 64 MB of DDR2, MIPI CSI-2 and a QFN-88 package — on a four-layer PCB with two OV9281 cameras. The firmware is a Buildroot image (vendor BSP kernel, Rockchip TPL/SPL/U-Boot chain) booting from an SD card, and the depth processing leans on the SoC's Rockchip Video Engine SAD (sum of absolute differences) block-matching accelerator via the proprietary librve.a. The author is gauging interest: if more than 20 people sign up he will order 10 revised boards to sell, with SLAM and YOLO planned next.

**Discussion**: The thread is broadly positive about the hardware but skeptical of the depth results: hex4def6 says the demo video looks bad and suspects the images are raw and unrectified, i.e. the intrinsics/extrinsics calibration step was skipped, and points to OpenCV's stereoBM tutorials as the way to do it properly. throwaway219450 supplies the technical counterweight — SAD block matching runs faster than real time even on a Raspberry Pi, disparity search ranges are small because parallax scales inversely with distance, and only horizontal search is needed after calibration — while agreeing that repeating texture and, worse, blank untextured walls are the real failure modes. Replies dig into whether calibration can be dodged: one commenter hopes to tune constants by hand, another says structure-from-motion/bundle adjustment can recover intrinsics but that PCB flex of a few microns already invalidates calibration on high-resolution sensors. Others ask practical questions that went unanswered — stereo baseline and clock distribution, whether a used PS5 camera would do the same job, and what the BOM costs.

**Worth reading**
- [throwaway219450](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50228336): Explains why SAD stereo is cheap in practice: small disparity search ranges, horizontal-only search after calibration, roughly 64 operations per pixel, and census-transform plus Hamming distance as a more robust alternative.
- [hex4def6](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50228606): Argues the demo likely runs on raw unrectified images with no calibration, and outlines the intrinsics/extrinsics steps needed — a direct challenge to the project's headline result.
- [throwaway219450](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50228877): Notes that structure-from-motion/bundle adjustment can yield intrinsics, but that on a high-resolution sensor even a few microns of PCB flex breaks the calibration.

## 9. Social dynamics of toxic metal industry in medieval Europe {#story-9}
*#12 on HN · [cambridge.org](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/social-dynamics-of-toxic-metal-industry-in-medieval-europe-peak-district-lead-production-ad-6001500/34A2A16AC99B1B12E3EB99CEA1DDB829) · 39 points · 14 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50169450)*

An open-access Antiquity paper by Christopher Loveluck and colleagues at Nottingham, Sheffield and the Castleton Historical Society measuring lead concentrations in tooth enamel — which records childhood exposure between roughly ages four and twelve — from three Derbyshire cemeteries, tied to radiocarbon dates. It pushes the start of post-Roman lead smelting in the Peak District back to the mid-seventh century, with large-scale production and local contamination around Castleton by the mid-eighth century, well before the previously assumed c. AD 760–860 date derived from lead isotopes in weights from Birka, Sweden. The authors also read social structure into the data: high childhood lead burden correlates with premature death, especially among women, and they propose the results as a benchmark for identifying medieval silver- and lead-producing communities elsewhere.

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly reflection rather than critique of the methodology: commenters marvel at the scale — a whole region exposed via dust and fumes for centuries, with fallout detectable in Alpine ice and Scandinavian lake cores — and compare it to the Hinkley, California hexavalent chromium case. The one substantive debate is whether medieval people understood the danger: one commenter guesses the symptoms were blamed on witchcraft or wrong religion, and is sharply corrected with a quoted Roman poem cataloguing gout, palsy, dropsy and sickly offspring, plus an EPA history arguing that what pre-modern and modern societies alike missed was chronic low-level exposure rather than acute poisoning. ## 19. No signs of AI payoff outside the tech sector {#story-19}
*#49 on HN · [apollo.com](https://www.apollo.com/wealth/insights-news/insights/daily-spark/no-signs-of-ai-payoff-outside-the-tech-sector) · 545 points · 566 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50224463)*

这是 Apollo Global Management 的 “Daily Spark” 系列文章，作者为首席经济学家 Torsten Slok，文章用几张图表论证：AI 带来的财务回报几乎全部集中在 Mag 7；Mag 7 的人均营收和利润率持续上升，而 S&P 493 的对应指标基本持平，整体生产率增长也未见起色。文章认为主流企业正把 token 支出从增长引擎重新归类为拖累利润率的成本项，并断言「AI 叙事将由 S&P 493 决定，而非 Mag 7」，理由是商业判断依赖语境、问责、信任与文化，而这些是模型无法承担的。

**Discussion**: 最高票的观点借用历史类比：电气化用了几十年才体现在生产率统计中，引用 1987 年 Solow 的名言「你到处都能看到计算机时代，唯独在生产率统计里看不到」；结论是新工厂和新公司从零围绕 AI 设计，才会真正见效，而不是现有企业改造旧流程。另有一派把原因归到技术特性上：软件之所以先受益，是因为有编译器、测试套件这类自动验证关卡，而法律、医疗之外的领域缺乏「这个结果对不对」的判定器，再聪明的模型也无法闭环迭代。反方则质疑文章的数据与结论：有人说 Mag 7 的利润率上升始于 2020 年，可以用疫情红利和大规模裁员解释，不该全部归因于 AI；也有人指出 S&P 500 里近四成是科技或 IT 公司，把「S&P 493」当作非科技部门本身就有问题；还有人认为企业级工具才刚铺开，9 到 24 个月内不可能看到财务效果。几位从业者提供了正面的一手证据：一位小型上市公司的会计负责人说用工具做内部与公开报告，token 花费的 ROI 是最好的一项，审计问题还减少了；医院与医生端的环境记录与病历生成被反复提到已经常态化。还有一种更尖锐的经济学反驳：生产力收益被 AI 供应商以租金形式截留——他们希望你花掉接近全部人力预算去买 token，所以宏观统计上自然看不到增益。较有共识的一点是：无论乐观还是悲观，现在下结论都太早，而「科技行业自己是否真有回报」同样存疑。

**Worth reading**
- [mbesto](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50225236): 一位前管理咨询顾问逐条拆解文章：Mag 7 的利润率上行始于 2020 年，与疫情和裁员周期吻合，不能归功于 AI；多数非科技公司才刚刚拿到可用的企业级工具，效果要等 12-24 个月；并称其中一张堆叠柱状图是他见过最糟糕的可视化。
- [fwlr](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50225252): 提出反向解释：如果借万亿美金造出一台 1 美分阿司匹林机器对抗 1 美元的仿制药，你只能卖 99 美分——AI 的收益几乎全被供应商以租金形式收走，因此宏观生产率看不到增益是设计使然，而非技术无效。
- [638d127673](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50227232): 一手反例：一家小型美国上市公司的会计负责人称用工具准备内部与对外报告后，截止期更紧、产出质量更高、审计发现更少，token 支出是他见过 ROI 最好的开支之一。

## 20. Dream-1 {#story-20}
*#52 on HN · [dreamlabs.ai](https://dreamlabs.ai/research/dream-1/) · 165 points · 76 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50223785)*

Dream Labs' research post introduces Dream-1, a 「World Action Model」 manipulation policy served as a public API: you point a bimanual, parallel-gripper robot at it and, the company claims, it works with no fine-tuning or robot-specific demos, by predicting end-effector poses and video rather than joint angles. The company is simultaneously coming out of stealth with $90M in seed funding, prices the model at $3/hour ($2/hour for Dream-1-flash, roughly $0.0013 per 1.6-second action chunk), and offers fine-tuning-as-a-service for robots that fall short — 5 minutes of data for near embodiments, 77–98 minutes for far ones like Dexmate's dexterous hands. It cites an independent Robocurve evaluation putting Dream-1 on a new Pareto frontier, beating Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon at 16x lower cost per attempt than Gemini 4 Argon.

**Discussion**: The dominant thread is hands-on disappointment with the public playground: multiple commenters ran prompts and got literalism rather than task success — 「Move the elephant next to the ball」 ended with the ball moved next to the elephant and both objects shoved around afterwards, and several noticed the left arm mostly idling with rhythmic twitching. Veterans of factory and warehouse automation say the page reads as investor material rather than customer material, with a $399 toy Microduck cited as the flagship example of demand, and doubt the whole 「flywheel」 premise works when the demos aren't usable for real tasks. A substantial sub-thread questions why an unknown lab's launch hit the front page with zero comments, floating PR agencies and YC connections before the submitter explained he found the model through Robocurve's benchmark after an X post about Kimi K3.1 Pro doing badly on it; others defend HN moderation and note Saturdays are quiet. The sharpest technical disagreement is over whether zero-shot embodiment generalization matters at all: one commenter argues it is confined to near-identical two-finger grippers, collapses once the hand changes, and that collecting a little data per embodiment is trivial anyway, while the submitter counters that Physical Intelligence's rival API requires per-robot fine-tuning. A long tangent covers AI-written blog prose (many find the post's style off-putting, others note the videos are real) and, separately, labor displacement and RaaS economics.

**Worth reading**
- [modeless](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50224710): Argues the claimed zero-shot embodiment generalization is narrow — two-finger grippers on two stationary arms — and that zero-shot cross-embodiment isn't even the important problem, since a few minutes of data per robot beats it; the hard unsolved part is reliable long-horizon tasks in messy real environments.
- [GaggiX](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50224171): The submitter explains the post's provenance: he found Dream-1 on the Robocurve benchmark after someone on X noted Kimi K3.1 Pro scoring badly there, and posted it because it looked like the first API for an arbitrary bimanual robot.
- [ajjenkins](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50224264): A concrete playground result: the prompt 「Move the elephant next to the ball」 succeeded only in the degenerate sense — the ball was moved to the elephant and both kept being pushed around afterwards.
