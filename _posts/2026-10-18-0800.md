## 1. Show HN: Sphinx – A hook that quizzes you on your AI's code before shipping {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [sphinx-six-theta.vercel.app](https://sphinx-six-theta.vercel.app/) · 71 points · 19 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50137950)*

Sphinx（npm 包 sphinx-understand，Node 22.19+，支持 macOS、Linux、WSL）是一个 Git 钩子，在你推送到受保护分支前暂停，并针对你的 diff 提出一个场景问题——例如一个 webhook 处理器在调用 charge() 之前保存了 Stripe 事件 ID。如果回答正确，推送继续；如果回答错误，你会得到解释并重试。它可与 Codex、Claude Code、Cursor、OpenCode、Pi 或普通 Git 配合使用，通过你的编码 agent、OpenRouter 或任何 OpenAI 兼容端点运行；有一个 `sphinx yolo` 逃生舱，且每次跳过都会被记录。页面声称答案是私密的——它们永远不会到达编码 agent，且 .env 文件和密钥永不被读取。

**Discussion**: 意见分歧很大，且大多偏怀疑。相当一部分人认为问题真实存在，但工具无法解决：几年前的论点是，经常使用 LLM 的人不需要它，而不理解自己代码的人也不会开启它（altmanaltman 和 hypfer 最直接地提出了这一点，hypfer 称其 LLM 使用分为两种模式——他阅读并拥有的代码，以及他有意不阅读的代码——因此这个钩子要么问他已经知道的事情，要么问他不在乎的事情）。辩护者反驳说，大约每三年开发者数量翻一番，因此一半的从业者经验不足三年，一个会说“不要复制你不理解的代码”的机器相当于老开发者曾经给出的同侪压力。几位评论者表示，代码审查已经覆盖了这一点，或者将审查设为强制门槛会制造摩擦，而另一些人则认为它适合 OSS 维护者或应对大量 AI 提交的团队。一位评论者提到自己构建了类似工具（vibecheck.dev），但让理解过程变为可选，而非强制门槛。还有多个指出名称与既有 Sphinx 文档项目冲突，以及玩笑式地担心 agent 最终会代替人类回答问题。

**Worth reading**
- [nkmnz](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50138385): 最有力的辩护：开发者数量每三年翻一番，因此一半开发者经验不足三年，一个提问工具可以充当资深工程师曾经给出的“不要复制你不理解的代码”的警告。
- [hypfer](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50138244): hypfer 区分了两种情况——你真正理解的代码和你刻意不阅读的代码——这表明该钩子要么问用户已知的事，要么问他们不在乎的事，并得到回复说它适用于把一切都当作第二种情况的同事。
- [linkdd](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50138164): 指出该名称与现有 sphinx-doc.org 项目冲突，并讽刺地建议“slop 生成器”应检查名称是否已被使用。

## 3. How the Elizabeth Holmes documentary “You Can See Everything” is deceiving {#story-3}

A post on the newsletter The Profile by a writer who saw Lance Oppenheim and Nathan Fielder's Elizabeth Holmes documentary You Can See Everything in a theater and argues, without spoilers, that the film manipulates the audience in real time through close-ups, editing and the order information is revealed — citing shouts of “Criminal!” from the crowd as proof. It notes that Holmes was acquitted of all four patient-fraud charges and is imprisoned for knowingly misleading investors who put more than $700 million into Theranos, and compares the film's framing shifts to a Canon experiment in which six photographers given different descriptions of the same man produced wildly different portraits.

**Discussion**: Commenters who saw the film largely confirm its three-act structure (deception, sympathy, puppeteer) but reject the article's hypnosis framing — one says he left questioning whether Holmes was a master manipulator or oblivious, not whether she was guilty, and another calls it a film that leaves you with more questions than answers. A vocal group dismisses the piece as breathless and empty: “Local woman discovers Nathan For You,” Rashomon exists, and it never names a single technique or fact. There is a split over Fielder himself — one camp says Holmes is simply a prop in a “Nathan for Nathan” film about his own appetite for deceiving people, while another insists he barely manipulated anyone, just asked blunt questions and watched her and her husband squirm. The thread's biggest fight is legal: one side argues acquittal on patient-fraud charges means she is not guilty and the charges belong to Theranos and Balwani, while others counter that Balwani was convicted on exactly those counts, that a juror explained Holmes's acquittal came because she never dealt directly with patients, and that this is a technicality rather than innocence. **Worth reading**
- [malisper](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50138166): Adds the concrete legal context: a juror said Holmes was acquitted of patient fraud because she never directly interacted with patients, Balwani was convicted by a separate jury on those charges, and Theranos settled suits for roughly $50M before bankruptcy.
- [coldfloor](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50138347): Argues the film is not about Holmes at all but a quintessential Nathan Fielder project in which she is used as a prop, and that the article's wonder at his deception of subjects just reveals the author has never seen his other work.

## 4. Lean now ships with verified kernels {#story-4}
*#5 on HN · [leodemoura.github.io](https://leodemoura.github.io/blog/2026-10-15-lean-now-ships-with-verified-kernels/) · 96 points · 29 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50114758)*

A Lean FRO blog post announcing that Lean v4.35.0 adds the 「lake check」 command, which exports a development and rechecks it with external kernels inside a sandbox (disabled via --inadvisably-no-sandbox). Lean now bundles four extra kernels — nanoda, lean4lean, con-leche and con-ron — runnable together with 「lake check --paranoid」, which also uses a hardened-allocator build of the official kernel. The headline result is con-leche, the first Lean kernel written in Lean and proven consistent (by Claude Fable/Opus under Joachim Breitner's supervision, using Mario Carneiro's set-theoretic model); it runs 0.69x the instructions of the official kernel and checks all of Mathlib in ~100 seconds on eight threads. con-ron is an AI-translated Rust version with a refinement proof via Aeneas, so a shared bug would have to hit both Lean and Rust runtimes. The post follows two August kernel postmortems caused by AI-found vulnerabilities, and flags scaling trouble: Mathlib is 2.5M LoC, Tau Ceti 3M+, Anthropic's FLT 13M+, a Courtade–Kumar repo over 50M, and 1B+ LoC math libraries are being proposed.

**Discussion**: Most of the thread is not about the kernels at all but about whether this year's math breakthroughs came from Lean or from LLMs. One commenter asked what would have happened if Lean had existed 15 years ago with a mass contributor base; the near-consensus from people with formalization experience (AxEy, dwohnitmok, aureianimus) is 『very few or none』, because proof assistants are verification tools, not discovery tools — you need a natural-language proof or at least an idea before you can formalize, and hand-formalizing was 10–1000x more work than writing prose. A dissenting framing from anonymous908213 argues math was effectively 『obfuscated』 by obscurity and that LLMs are mostly a translation machine that deobfuscates it; youoy, froh and senderista push back hard, saying math is not obfuscated, formal manipulation is only one part of research, and the AI contribution is automating that part. aureianimus supplies the 『mirror hypothesis』: Mathlib PRs spiked at the release of Opus 4.6 and OpenAI reportedly does informal reasoning first, so verification progress may be downstream of LLMs rather than the reverse. Elsewhere, people ask what 『verified』 actually means here (sinuhe69), get the answer that it is both formal verification and cross-checking by independent kernels, and worry about the infinite-checker regress and Gödel (s_Hogg, xiaoyu2006, youoy). Minor topics: a nitpick that AI finds 『weaknesses』 not 『vulnerabilities』, and a skeptical subthread on whether Lean could ever be a general-purpose language for GUI-style programs.

**Worth reading**
- [dwohnitmok](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50138392): A practitioner with pre-LLM formalization experience argues none of this year's results would have arrived earlier: mathematicians never start from the formal proof, hand-formalization costs 10–1000x a prose proof, and formalizing one textbook was a multi-year project for a professional.
- [cloudbonsai](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50138012): Lays out the verification chain plainly: con-leche is a Lean kernel written in Lean and proven consistent, con-ron is its Rust translation verified by a Rust-side tool, lean4lean is still awaiting verification — two verified kernels now, one pending.
- [aureianimus](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50137865): Adds the sharpest counter-data point to the 『Lean enabled this』 view: Mathlib PRs spiked at the release of Opus 4.6, and OpenAI claims informal reasoning happens first, supporting a 『mirror hypothesis』 where proof assistants are for checking, not discovery.

## 5. The game industry is broken – can we fix it? {#story-5}
*#6 on HN · [knowledge.edge-online.com](https://knowledge.edge-online.com/p/the-game-industry-is-broken-can-we-fix-it-tim-sweeney-edge-crash) · 20 points · 17 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50138156)*

This is the cover story of Edge issue 428, in which nine industry figures diagnose the current downturn and propose fixes. Amir Satvat, who runs the ASGC Games Industry Layoffs Tracker, projects over 14,500 job losses in 2026 and a five-year total above 58,000 from 2022, concentrated in California, the UK, northern/western Europe and North America, while Japan and eastern Europe have been largely insulated. Epic CEO Tim Sweeney calls it the worst crash since the 1980s, blames multi-causal pressures — budgets that doubled every few years since 2000, from $12M for 2006's Gears of War to ~$100M for Gears of War 3, and now $250–400M — and Raph Koster cites his research that development costs rise roughly 10x per decade, from ~$1M in the mid-90s to $100M by 2015. Suggested remedies: leaner Japanese-style teams with far lower executive pay, smaller games with tight 20-hour environments (Shawn Layden), and accepting that the five-billion-player audience has hit a hard cap.

**Discussion**: Commenters broadly accept the diagnosis but split on the cure: one camp argues the industry should stop chasing 500-person live-service blockbusters when tiny teams ship better games, citing Hollow Knight and Expedition 33 (a ~32-person team), while others worry that dropping to 100-person teams cannot keep the industry healthy. The sharpest pushback targets Sweeney's own prescription — that making an ecosystem game is the answer — with the objection that chasing addictive, monetised ecosystems is precisely what makes modern AAA dull and that most players only need one such game. Several note knock-on factors: good GPUs are getting more expensive and rarer at consumer level; US studios have been in decline for a decade and simply ran out of free money; and that the industry is chronically oversupplied with applicants, like fashion, which keeps pay low and hours brutal. Concrete counterpoints include GTA VI's ~$1.5B development cost as an example of gold-plating rather than improvement, and AI-assisted single-person animation pipelines as a possible way to cut costs.

**Worth reading**
- [bigstrat2003](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50138517): Argues Sweeney's own answer — build an ecosystem game — is exactly the mindset that has made AAA games suck, since players need only one such game and would happily buy something unique instead.
- [Animats](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50138506): Adds hardware-side context: good GPUs are getting pricier and rarer with the shift to laptops and phones, and notes GTA VI's ~$1.5B development cost — 3x the most expensive film ever — plus links to an AI-assisted solo animator pipping Disney-quality output.
- [jdw64](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50138459): First-hand framing: the core problem is that everyone wants to break into games, so studios get away with low pay and brutal hours because the supply of willing people is endless.

## 7. NY announces game ownership bill requiring ownership rather than licensing {#story-7}
*#17 on HN · [tomshardware.com](https://www.tomshardware.com/video-games/new-york-rep-announces-game-ownership-bill-that-would-require-consumer-ownership-rather-than-sale-of-licenses-would-also-require-publishers-to-keep-the-game-functional-after-support-ends) · 227 points · 71 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50136536)*

Tom's Hardware reports that Rep. Ritchie Torres of New York's 15th congressional district introduced the federal Power To The Players Act, which would direct the FTC to write rules making a game purchase confer ownership rather than a revocable license. It would also require online games to offer an offline mode before their servers are shut down; concrete definitions, including transfer rights, have not been released. The article cites Ubisoft's 2024 shutdown of The Crew, NBA 2K25's servers going offline two years after release, and Overwatch 2 replacing the original, and notes Pennsylvania's separate Protect Our Games Act with offline, community-server, or 25% refund requirements.

**Discussion**: The thread quickly corrects the framing: this is federal legislation introduced by a New York member of Congress, not a New York state law, despite the headline. Commenters split on whether ownership can be usefully defined and enforced, with one citing World of Warcraft's shifting patches and expansions, while others argue games shipped offline and with community-server support for decades, so publishers could design for it or release server binaries. A recurring theme is that copyright reform matters more, but one detailed reply argues that DMCA 1201 anti-circumvention, not fair use, is what makes custom servers and emulation legally perilous. The strongest economic objection is that true digital ownership with first-sale rights would create a near-frictionless resale market and gut game sales; supporters answer that regulation can reset the equilibrium and that physical resale did not destroy the industry. Others note the bill will expire with this Congress on January 3, recommend GOG's DRM-free model, and flag the thread as a dupe.

**Worth reading**
- [dabinat](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50136959): Corrects the framing: this is a federal bill introduced in the House by a New York representative, not a New York state law.
- [kmeisthax](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50137735): Argues the real legal obstacle to preservation is DMCA 1201 anti-circumvention, not fair use — custom-server projects were already legal until DRM checks made them effectively illegal.
- [somenameforme](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50137478): Makes the economic counter-case: real ownership plus first-sale for digital games would enable near-costless resale and collapse prices, so shorter copyright terms are the more realistic fix.

## 18. Tell HN: Remember to migrate away from .name domain {#story-18}
*#44 on HN · text post · 148 points · 89 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50089577)*

This is a Hacker News text post, not a linked article: nikanj warns that anyone holding a third-level .name registration such as john.smith.name will have it permanently expunged next year, and asks whether people moved to .com or another TLD. The poster says they are stuck because an Azure administrator email cannot be changed and Microsoft support has been unhelpful. Commenters supply context the post omits: .name originally sold only third-level registrations, including first@last.name email forwarding, before second-level names were added.

**Discussion**: The thread spends much of its energy correcting a widespread misunderstanding: owning lastname.name and using first.lastname.name as a subdomain is unaffected, while directly registered first.lastname.name domains are the ones being dropped. One detailed explanation says .name published authoritative NS records for those third-level names without delegating lastname.name, creating cookie and namespace collisions when the second-level name was later registered; Verisign is resolving that by dropping all third-level delegations en masse. Commenters split on motive: some call it a bait-and-switch to relist valuable lastname.name and sell premium names, while others see it as overdue cost-cutting for bespoke delegation and email-forwarding infrastructure, and argue Verisign should at least offer a transition path or honor prepaid terms. A registrant who lost a .name after a support fight moved to .fr, another second-level owner says they are migrating too, and others worry about refunds for registrations paid through 2040 and about old addresses receiving password-reset mail. The thread also detours into .eu revocations after Brexit and whether .io is still endangered.

**Worth reading**
- [kmeisthax](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50133828): Explains the unusual .name delegation model in detail and why dropping third-level registrations also fixes cookie and namespace collisions with later second-level registrants.
- [nikanj](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50089589): Links the Domain Name Wire report that registrations are being terminated in February even when paid through 2040.
- [toast0](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50132761): Argues for an orderly transition — upgrade sole third-level registrants to the second level and let shared second-level owners run third-level registration privately — instead of a too-bad, so-sad cutoff.

## 20. A story of WiFi secrets, UART lockdowns and a damaged flash memory chip {#story-20}
*#48 on HN · [adepts.of0x.cc](https://adepts.of0x.cc/eufy-homestation2-firmware/) · 86 points · 6 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50119751)*

A write-up from the adepts.of0x.cc crew on tearing apart an Eufy HomeBase 2 (firmware 3.4.2.2h) in the Eufy smart-home ecosystem. They desoldered the Winbond 25Q256JWEQ (WSON8 8.6mm) flash because in-circuit Raspberry Pi SPI0 reads returned mismatched checksums, damaged a 64 KB block in the process, then carved the image with Binwalk and 7z (U-Boot 1.1.3, MIPS32 Linux kernel, JFFS2). The key findings: the management Wi-Fi ESSID is 「OCEAN_」 plus the last three MAC bytes, the password comes from an NVRAM variable (WPAPSK1) handled by a set_wifi_pwd routine in a carved MIPS ELF, and the UART lockdown was beaten by reflashing a backdoored U-Boot image.

**Discussion**: The thread is small and mostly appreciative: commenters single out the article for being written with personality and genuine curiosity rather than as agent-assisted credit collection, and one says the informal, janky tone feels validating. The only substantive critique is that the article's ending is unclear about exactly how the Wi-Fi password is generated — a reader says the piece stands alone fine but never lands that point. Another commenter supplies the link to the required earlier DoorBell article, and one muses about what it's like to be the only technologist in a large extended family, prompting a joke about SWE couples reviewing each other's code.
