## 1. Inkling-2-Mini in 5.4 GB Memory {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/ShinMegamiBoson/lookatmyquant) · 155 points · 23 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50591853)*

This is the README page for LookAtMyQuant, an MIT-licensed quantization compiler that measures how much each stored weight harms output when compressed (KL to the original), then assigns a code per piece so the whole model fits a byte budget with the least damage. Its headline claim: thinkingmachines/Inkling-2-Mini, a 26.4B-physical/5.4B-active 3×-looped MoE with 18.89 GB of text weights, is packed to 5.38 GB (1.63 bits/weight) and 8.87 GB (2.69 b/w) using hashed block-vector trellis codes, a Qwen3.8-27B takes 53.7 GB down to 7.2 GB while still solving 401/540 math problems, and Qwen3.5-4B lands in 1.23 GB solving 330/540 vs 328 for Prism ML's Bonsai ternary 4B. Everything runs through a custom Apple Silicon runtime (lamq / runtime_mlx with Metal kernels), claimed at 73.2 tok/s on an M5 Max for the 5.4 GB file, optionally with a small DSpark drafter.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people intrigued by the compression ratios and people who actually checked the numbers. hugmynutus posts a detailed takedown: at 5.4 GB the Inkling file's KL is worse than Unsloth's UD-IQ1_S (0.0895 vs 0.0808 on English/chat/code, 0.287 vs 0.124 across 13 languages), and argues the reported GSM8K/HumanEval wins are within noise and partly come from post-training the quantized model on sample inputs rather than from the quantizer itself. Several commenters question the premise of re-quantizing a model already trained with quantization-aware training in NVFP4/MXFP8, with gavmor noting a QAT model's tolerance only applies to its own target format, while asaddhamani sees no problem with it. IronWolve asks about non-Apple hardware and Prospernwo confirms the released Inkling files are MLX/Metal-only — the hashed trellis codes and drafter don't run in llama.cpp today, though a GGUF path exists in the repo. syntaxing reports the model itself is very slow on an R9700 (a third of Qwen 27B's speed), unsure whether that's the looped architecture or unoptimized kernels, and a side thread drifts into the frontier-vs-local capability gap.

**Worth reading**
- [hugmynutus](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592929): hugmynutus re-measured the releases and says the Inkling quant is worse than Unsloth's UD-IQ1_S on KL, with the benchmark gains coming from post-training on sample inputs and falling inside noise.
- [Prospernwo](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592907): Prospernwo clarifies that the released Inkling files and drafter are Apple Silicon/MLX-only with custom Metal kernels, so NVIDIA users can't run them despite the repo's GGUF tooling.
- [woadwarrior01](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592509): woadwarrior01 asks why quantize a model already distributed in mixed NVFP4/MXFP8, prompting a debate about whether QAT tolerance transfers to other formats.

## 2. Lean verification of the Riemann Hypothesis {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [leanprover-community.github.io](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/) · 560 points · 428 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50589618)*

A joint statement from the Lean and Mathlib communities, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, certifying Levent Alpöge's November 20, 2026 proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, released as a Lean library of 17 million lines depending only on Mathlib. They argue all four criteria are met: the statement (already in Mathlib as `RiemannHypothesis`) was human-vetted, dozens of people reproduced the build and the same raw proof export, the proved theorem was matched to Mathlib's statement using two independent tools (`comparator` and `con-leche --compare`), and the export was checked by several kernels — Lean's own, `con-leche` and `con-ron` (which carry formal soundness proofs relative to set theory), plus third-party checkers — on an inert export isolated from any malicious code in the source.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people satisfied this is stronger than any peer review and people probing the remaining trust anchors: bugs shared across kernels, the soundness of the type theory or of ZFC plus inaccessible cardinals, and the precedent of a fake Collatz refutation that once slipped past an independent checker. Several commenters answer that Lean now ships formally verified kernels and that the RH proof was checked with them, so an exploit would have to break the checkers' own correctness proofs; others note every checker still rests on assumptions (set theory, the hardware, x86 semantics), leaving 99.9999% rather than absolute certainty. A separate argument concerns credit and understanding: some insist 'Claude did not prove RH, Levent Alpöge did, using Claude as a tool,' and several doubt the result means much until humans digest the new ideas, while professional mathematicians in the thread say they consider RH proved and are eager to learn why it is true. Practitioners also push back on the '17 million lines is unreadable' framing, noting most of the bulk is duplicated intermediate material generated in parallel and can be mechanically shrunk, and that many conditional results now become unconditional.

**Worth reading**
- [TwoFx](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50590587): TwoFx gives the clearest technical explanation of the 'inert export': Lean build steps can execute arbitrary code, so `lake comparator` elaborates the trusted statement and the proof separately in sandboxes, exports both to a JSON term format, and checks that JSON.
- [TwoFx](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50590648): Answers the fake-Collatz-refutation objection by noting Lean now ships formally verified checkers (linked to a Leo de Moura post from October 2026) and that the RH proof was checked with them, shrinking the attack surface to the checkers' own correctness proofs.
- [rithn](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50591755): A commenter mapped the proof's 175k files by imports into 2,647 clusters across 63 themes, finding the main theorem's cluster imports only a zero-free region, primes in progressions and Mertens-type zero sums, with no theme exceeding ~4% of the code.

## 5. PostgreSQL Extensions in Go {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [jespino.github.io](https://jespino.github.io/gopgext/) · 66 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50591260)*

gopgext is an experimental tool that builds PostgreSQL extensions from plain Go source compiled with TinyGo. A //gopgext:function comment turns a Go function into a SQL function, while the toolchain generates the C glue, the .control file and the install SQL; SQL types (including arrays, NULLs, sets, rows, UUIDs, JSON and composite types) map to Go, SPI queries and SQLSTATE errors are supported, and you can write triggers, aggregates, GiST/GIN/SP-GiST/BRIN operator classes, index access methods, foreign data wrappers and query hooks. The author says ports of contrib modules such as pgcrypto, hstore, pg_stat_statements and cube pass upstream's regression tests, but warns it must not be used in production since a bug runs inside the server and can crash it or corrupt data; it follows the same recipe as the author's earlier gokm project for Linux kernel modules in Go.

**Discussion**: The thread is thin — five comments and almost no hands-on evaluation of the tool itself. The most substantive pushback is that LLMs now make it cheap to write plpgsql directly, so shoehorning Go into the server is an unnecessary extravagance, with the blunt corollary that anything too complicated for plpgsql is probably a design mistake. Others point to prior art, notably the older plgo project and the author's own gokm for kernel modules, suggesting a pattern rather than a one-off. The remaining comments are off-topic: one praises the Go gopher as mascot marketing (contrasting it with Rust's Ferris), and one complains that a code snippet's brackets are invisible in dark mode.

**Worth reading**
- [ltbarcly3](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592838): Argues that with LLMs available, learning a Go-to-Postgres toolchain is unnecessary when an AI can just write plpgsql, and that anything too complex for plpgsql signals a bad design.
- [Wicher](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592811): Points to plgo, an earlier and more established project for writing PostgreSQL extensions in Go, as prior art worth comparing.

## 6. My monitor was cutting my download speed by 75% {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [blog.tymscar.com](https://blog.tymscar.com/posts/studiodisplayethernet/) · 351 points · 184 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50587875)*

Oscar Molnar's blog post about a 2.5 Gbps symmetrical fiber line where downloads collapsed to 600 Mbps while uploads stayed at 2.4 Gbps after he routed all peripherals through a 2026 Apple Studio Display XDR. A UGREEN USB-C 2.5G adapter with a Realtek RTL8156B hit 2.3 Gbps direct into the Mac but only ~600 Mbps behind the display; the Realtek vendor driver got him to 1.1 Gbps and enabling Ethernet flow control on his UniFi Dream Router 7 got him to ~2 Gbps. Using the adapter's own frame counters and UDP tests he traced the cap to the USB bulk-transfer pattern: the chip sends 4×1 KB packets per burst (bMaxBurst=3) then waits for an ACK, and the hub latency of ~7.6 µs plus ~8.2 µs to move 4 KB limits drain to ~168,000 frames/s (about 2 Gbps) against 2.5 Gbps ingress, so the buffer overflows and TCP backs off. He fixed it by buying a 5 Gbps UGREEN adapter with an RTL8157 (bMaxBurst=7, 8×1 KB bursts, 2x link rate) that dropped zero frames.

**Discussion**: A large share of the thread argues the headline is misleading clickbait — the culprit is a USB-to-Ethernet adapter with too little data in flight, plus a USB hub; any hub would do it, so blaming the expensive monitor is unfair. Others counter that the monitor is a 100+ Gb/s switch sold at Apple prices and should behave better, while a third camp says the adapter vendor cheaped out on a part that can't sustain its advertised speed through a hub. Several commenters found the latency-versus-bandwidth framing confusing and hashed out whether this is a buffer-size problem or a bandwidth-delay-product problem; the consensus that emerged is that the 4 KB per burst is a protocol-level limit the monitor cannot work around, and the added hop latency makes the drain rate insufficient. A large subthread drifts to io_uring and I/O pressure accounting, where Ghostty's use of io_uring makes Baldur's Gate 3's shader compilation see phantom I/O pressure and throttle to one core for hours; the io_uring maintainer's position, quoted from qemu-devel, is that iowait-style metrics are fundamentally broken rather than something to fix in the kernel. A recurring secondary debate asks whether 2.5 Gbps residential is worth it — most agree symmetric upload changes behaviour more than download, and that the real win is background backups and game downloads not disturbing anyone.

**Worth reading**
- [gh123man](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50589578): A commenter reports the identical problem for over a year with MacBooks and Thunderbolt docks, and notes it disappears when using a plain USB (non-Thunderbolt) cable to the dock — suggesting an Apple-side bug.
- [toast0](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592303): Explains why pause frames are dangerous: a switch can propagate a pause it received from one slow client to a server, stalling that server's traffic to every other destination.
- [nh2](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592931): Corrects the '4 packet buffer' framing: bMaxBurst=3 and wMaxPacketSize=1024 are fixed protocol parameters of the adapter, so the monitor cannot buffer to compensate for its own latency.

## 7. Verification files for the Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis {#story-7}
*#8 on HN · [math.mit.edu](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/) · 47 points · 14 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50590441)*

A page by Andrew Sutherland (MIT) publishing the verification artifacts for a Lean 4 formalization of the Riemann Hypothesis and GRH for Dirichlet L-functions, announced 20 November 2026, with the checking work largely driven by Claude Code. It reports all 175,187 modules compiling with zero errors under Lean v4.33.1 / Mathlib v4.33.1; the five theorems in Main.lean depending only on propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound; and independent kernels accepting the exported proof — nanoda (Rust, 27m42s on 80 threads), con-leche master (1,468,418 declarations in 3m15s) and lean4lean (175,182 modules at default settings, the other 5 after raising its step limit). The headline export is 61.7 GB unpacked (7.7 GB compressed) with published sha256 hashes, though the lake comparator and the single-threaded Lean kernel replays were still running, and per-file .olean hashes reproduce exactly only from the original build path /home/ubuntu/grh/src/lean because linter messages embed absolute source paths.

**Discussion**: The thread barely engages with the mathematics; almost all of it is about trusting a proof produced and verified inside one vendor's stack. The opening commenter — an account minutes old — argues an LLM monoculture (Claude writes the proof, commits to the Lean and con-leche kernels, builds the site, and is then told by that site to verify its own work) could have hidden a backdoored kernel; replies call that a leap, noting the missing '???' step in the argument, dismissing the pile-on as engagement farming, and invoking the Three-Body Problem's corrupted gifted knowledge. The most substantive response argues con-leche's correctness reduces to ZFC, so what actually needs checking is that the checker binary is untampered and that Claude's rendering of the proof into it is faithful — giving roughly 90% odds the result is not fraud while finding even the honest outcome existentially bleak. Others joke about doing this before an IPO and arresting Anthropic's stock slide, suggest cross-checking with OpenAI, Grok and a Chinese model, and propose proving con-leche itself correct in a pre-2022 Metamath to cut LLMs out of the trusted base. No commenter claims to have re-run any of the checks independently.

**Worth reading**
- [1283agf](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592194): Raises the core objection driving the thread: one model wrote the proof, the kernels, and the verification page, which then instructs that model to verify itself — a supply-chain attack surface rather than independent confirmation.
- [OgsyedIE](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592632): The strongest counter-argument: con-leche's correctness is already known modulo ZFC, so the gaps are binary tampering and faithful translation of the proof, and even a non-fraudulent outcome leaves humans as spectators.
- [bumbledraven](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592932): Concrete proposal for removing the LLM from the trusted base: prove con-leche correct inside a pre-2022 Metamath, whose checker has a tiny TCB and no model dependency.

## 8. Show HN: Zero C# – C# without a runtime or GC {#story-8}
*#9 on HN · [onurgumus.github.io](https://onurgumus.github.io/zero-csharp/index.html) · 69 points · 51 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50590279)*

Zero C# is a fork of bflat that compiles C# straight to a small native binary with no .NET runtime and no garbage collector, keeping the syntax, type checking, generics (a specialized copy per type, static abstract members), records, tuples, lambdas and Parallel.For. Memory comes from arenas freed all at once, with a small library providing strings, collections and I/O; exceptions are replaced by unions since throw ends the program, and reflection/async are dropped. Hello world is claimed at 11 KB on Windows and 40 KB on Linux, and the same source is tested on Linux, macOS, Windows (under Wine), WASI via NativeAOT-LLVM under wasmtime, and DOS on a 386 through the HX DOS extender on Bochs.

**Discussion**: The dominant objection is 'why not NativeAOT, Span<T>, unsafe pointers, stackalloc and Marshal?' — several C# practitioners say the language already covers low-level and allocation-free work, and NativeAOT already removes the runtime-install problem (though commenters correct that it keeps the GC, not the JIT). Defenders frame it as C#-the-syntax on bare metal for people who don't want C++'s arcane rules or dependency story, and point at targets NativeAOT can't reach, namely DOS. A second camp argues the GC is a feature and the real complaint is allocation pressure, controllable via GCSettings.LatencyMode and pooling — one commenter cites an industrial camera system on .NET 10 handling 8 streams at ~800 Mb/s with zero over-allocation in 90 days. Sceptics' strongest points are ecosystem breakage (no standard library APIs, no reflection, strings and collections only via arenas) and that manual arenas only free at coarse points, so they'd pick Rust or Zig — or call it a nice POC. Beef (beeflang.org) comes up repeatedly as the closer precedent.

**Worth reading**
- [OnceMoreUntoThe](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50591105): A commenter running a 24/7 industrial camera system on .NET 10 with SustainedLowLatency and stackalloc reports 8 streams at ~800 Mb/s with zero bytes over-allocated in 90 days, as a counter to CLR-GC complaints.
- [YuechenLi](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50591360): Asks what this offers over NativeAOT, noting NativeAOT is often slower than RyuJIT and its only clear win is smaller binaries, while Span<T> and ref structs already allow manual low-level control.
- [Johanx64](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592329): Argues arenas are 'GC done right' — explicit collection — and that memory leaks were never the real bug class; memory corruption from unsafe code was.

## 9. I'm quite pleased by the tech architecture of my art website {#story-9}
*#11 on HN · [klauria.tech](https://klauria.tech/klauria-art/) · 98 points · 19 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50577991)*

A developer who paints as a hobby describes the stack behind klauria.art, an art site built to be static for visitors but dynamic for its single author. Every HTML page is server-rendered with a Cloudflare cache header (30 days), and any write — a new painting, a tag change — simply invalidates all cached pages rather than trying to track per-page dependencies, which the author calls the pragmatic choice to avoid half-updated correctness bugs. Images are downsampled at three sizes in the browser at upload time and stored in Cloudflare R2 alongside originals, with all metadata in a single JSON file in R2 that Workers read to generate tag pages; interactive maps and timelines are client-side JS over that JSON, built with Astro, and the whole thing fits in Cloudflare's free tier (10 GB storage).

**Discussion**: Most of the thread is praise for the paintings and the site's typography and layout, with several readers noting it works fine with scripts blocked and a couple asking for more art than architecture. The substantive counter-example comes from showcaseearth, who solves the same 'static for everyone, dynamic for me' problem with a self-hosted Grist instance plus versioned snapshots addressed by a tiny current.json pointer, so old versions never change and nothing ever needs invalidating. ricardobeat plugs bunny.net as a drop-in alternative with CDN, image resizing and edge functions, quoting an average spend of $0.22/month, while jrm4 notes he has run a static site generated from zim-wiki for roughly 20 years. zzzeek reads the post as a rare case of a physical-medium artist happily using LLMs to build presentation infrastructure, but the author (articulatepang) corrects the premise: he is a software developer who paints for fun, not a professional artist, though he agrees the art world is broadly worried about modern models. Two commenters report the site being down or slow for them, and the author's own top comment riffs on a deleted 'what was the prompt?' joke by linking the painting's prompt-and-traces page.

**Worth reading**
- [showcaseearth](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592415): A painter-developer describes the opposite architecture: a self-hosted Grist catalog publishing immutable versioned snapshots behind a current.json pointer, so nothing ever needs cache invalidation and rollback is just repointing.
- [ricardobeat](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50591898): Recommends bunny.net for the same job — CDN, image resizing, edge functions and a setup CLI — at an average of $0.22/month for small sites.
- [zzzeek](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50592733): Argues the real news is a physical-medium artist using LLMs for website architecture, since visual artists are usually hostile to AI — a framing the author partially corrects in reply.
