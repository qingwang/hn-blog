## 1. Perplexity and Intelligence {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [openai.com](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/) · 266 points · 177 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50601525)*

An OpenAI post by Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki arguing that perplexity (log cross-entropy on held-out data) should become a shared industry metric for tracking AI progress and for setting 'common limits' on development tied to safety milestones. He publishes OpenAI's internal numbers, measured on a fixed snapshot of its internal codebase since GPT-3.5: ~0.99 log-perplexity for GPT-3.5, ~0.91 for GPT-4, below 0.8 now, with a model at ~0.76 reportedly producing 'groundbreaking' results on open math problems, and he extrapolates further transformative capabilities in the 0.68–0.7 window, possibly as soon as 2027 via AI-assisted pretraining research. He concedes the metric's weaknesses: cross-lab comparability, no direct tie to actionable evals, blindness to chain-of-thought gains, and an unknown entropy floor.

**Discussion**: The biggest reaction is surprise that plain pretraining loss still tracks capability at all, given how much recent progress is attributed to RL and post-training; several commenters had assumed pretraining had plateaued. A recurring confusion — whether the chart measures base pretrains or RL-tuned models — gets corrected repeatedly: it is the base checkpoint, before CoT and RLVR, which defenders say sets the ceiling that post-training can elicit. Sceptics argue the graph is pure correlation dressed as causation, that a codebase held-out set is a narrow proxy, and that a 'shared standard' computed on each lab's private dataset is unauditable and convenient for the labs. Others push back on the 'emergence at a perplexity threshold' framing, noting specialized small models and that perplexity says little about tool use or novel reasoning; the strongest defences are that the relationship is imperfect but empirically real, and that the GPT-4.5 outlier (best base model, mediocre benchmarks without CoT) actually supports the base-capability thesis. Side threads cover the Hutter Prize, a recent 3Blue1Brown video on compression and intelligence, estimates of human next-token perplexity, and speculation that the chart implies a 'GPT-6.2 Astra' solved Navier-Stokes.

**Worth reading**
- [tedsanders](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602183): An OpenAI employee (tedsanders) acknowledges the perplexity-to-quality correlation is neither 1 nor 0, says it is imperfect and empirical, and promises the lab would switch metrics if it stops being informative.
- [ranyume](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50603141): Argues the proposed industry standard is one only the labs can compute, on datasets they won't share, so regulators would need audit rights over both data and calculation for it to mean anything.
- [ACCount39](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602837): Explains why RL often raises measured perplexity: it induces 'controlled mode collapse', suppressing capabilities useful for raw next-token prediction but harmful for chain-of-thought, without necessarily destroying the underlying model.

## 2. Show HN: I built a game where you are an LLM's subagent {#story-2}
*#3 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/gulivan/worker755) · 188 points · 47 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50599762)*

Worker 755 is a self-hosted browser game in which a real LLM — Claude, GPT, Gemini, DeepSeek, GLM or Kimi — is wired up like an orchestrator: it has a task queue, read-only access to a machine, and a tool that 'spawns a subagent'. That subagent is the player, who receives instructions with a 90-second clock and must return a report plus a transcript of every command run, after which the manager checks the work, files incident reports and writes a performance review while the fictional user 'Dan' watches his home folder get wrecked. It runs on Node 24 or newer (npm run setup/build/start on port 8755), can be run offline with a scripted manager, and takes either a bring-your-own OpenRouter or Vercel AI Gateway key or server-funded shifts; a full Haiku shift costs a few cents, and sign-in accounts get a starter credit plus top-ups from $10 to $200. Options include a fresh sandbox vs. legacy production with a live database, a scripted or LLM-played 'Dan', and a hidden ground-truth check per mission that tracks 'times the manager was wrong'.

## 3. Open Social Web Alliance {#story-3}
*#5 on HN · [osw-alliance.net](https://osw-alliance.net/en/) · 161 points · 161 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50600511)*

A vision and policy paper from the German Heinrich Böll Foundation (a think tank close to the Greens) proposing an 'Open Social Web Alliance' — a coalition of civil society, research, business and public-sector actors to build shared infrastructure on ActivityPub and AT Protocol, with Matrix for direct messaging, modelled on email. It sets out a roadmap to 2028: a German-government-backed summit in autumn 2026, a charter and publicly funded secretariat, first funding round by spring 2027, and a target of 100 million users, plus a 'Social Web Health Report'. It cites today's baseline of roughly 13 million active Fediverse users across 42,000 instances and about 43 million registered AT Protocol accounts, and names five challenges — infrastructure, governance, funding, appeal and public mandate.

**Discussion**: The dominant reaction is hostile to the writing rather than the goal: commenters call the prose fluffy, content-free and written as if outcomes were already achieved, and several note it reads like a translation of German policy-speak or LLM output. Reading the linked PDF, people flag the €36 million ask (€15M for ActivityPub/AT Proto infrastructure, €10M innovation fund, €10M investment fund, €1M secretariat) and argue it assumes the German government, public broadcasters and creators will all behave exactly as wished. A long subthread rejects the premise that a cleverly engineered tool can 'fix' democracy, with the counterpoint that Taiwan's pol.is/vTaiwan shows tools can work when wrapped in a real social movement. Another large argument is about free speech: some read 'strengthens democratic discourse' as a euphemism for censorship, and defenders of X fight it out with people who say X is bots and rage bait rather than a marketplace of ideas. Practitioners push back on the design too — small, focused communities with barriers beat a 100-million-user network, there is no plan for the creator economy or moderation, and ATProto/ActivityPub already exist but lose on network effects; nostr fans object to any central governance at all. The closest thing to consensus is that the diagnosis is real (advertising-driven incentives, bot armies, network lock-in) but the proposal is an unfunded wish list with no answer to how it beats the incumbents.

**Worth reading**
- [janpmz](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602299): A close reading of the PDF finds that the proposed publicly funded Secretariat would decide on 'interventions at the infrastructure or application level' and liaise with state authorities and broadcasting councils — i.e. a central body that can override the protocol, which the marketing copy never mentions.
- [1970-01-01](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50600950): Revives the classic 'your idea will not work' fill-in-the-blank form and checks off the boxes that apply: users won't tolerate switching, Meta/Reddit/Trump won't allow it, it needs too much incumbent cooperation and immediate critical mass, and it lacks any enforceable cross-network moderation authority.
- [bloopernova](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50601608): First-hand ATProto user reports a single identity OAuth-ing into mu.social, tangled.org and pckt.blog, praises Blacksky as a fairer space, and notes private spaces were about to launch — the concrete counterpoint to the claim that nothing usable exists yet.

## 4. I joined the NTP Pool, but not with my Stratum 1 Pi {#story-4}
*#6 on HN · [jeffgeerling.com](https://www.jeffgeerling.com/blog/2026/i-joined-the-ntp-pool/) · 71 points · 21 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50600622)*

Jeff Geerling describes finally contributing a public Stratum 2 NTP server (ntp-01.jeffgeerling.com) to the global NTP Pool, fed over a WireGuard tunnel by his home Stratum 1 'Time Pi' — a Raspberry Pi with a TimeHAT v6, an Intel i226 NIC and a u-blox ZED-F9T timing-grade GNSS module, with the NIC's PTP hardware clock disciplined by linuxptp's ts2phc. The public node runs on an Oxtrys VPS in St. Louis for low latency, and he warns that pool membership means a public IP, traffic that lingers for months after leaving, and a multi-hour validation period before a server is useful. He also documents gotchas: Debian's /run/chrony-dhcp sourcedir letting a DHCP-provided server pollute his GNSS time, a 200ms jump that pushed him from phc2sys to chrony's refclock PHC, and a kernel upgrade that rebooted the VPS and cost 6–12 hours of pool score recovery.

**Discussion**: A hardware argument dominates: one commenter recommends FPGA-based time servers over Pis, citing PCIe PTM and TGPIO on x86 as better than ARM GPIO interrupts, but another replies that his own x86 PTM build hard-locked the bus badly enough to need physical unplugging, and Geerling himself notes almost any PPS input gets you under 1µs, with hardware clock control only needed to go lower. A second thread questions whether VPSes belong in the pool at all: one commenter recalls a historical ban over VM timing, others distinguish jumpy OpenVZ-style containers from KVM guests where chrony can actually steer the clock, and Geerling confirms KVM is the workable case. On daemons, people run NTPSec, chrony and the newer Rust ntpd-rs, with Geerling praising chrony's built-in monitoring and hardware timestamping support; others recommend CenterClick boxes or cheap used Spectracom enterprise servers for people who don't want to build their own. A cheap-setup operator notes that running several modest Stratum 1s gives redundancy and that pool monitoring doubles as a free early-warning system for ISP outages.

**Worth reading**
- [geerlingguy](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50603262): Geerling details his accuracy budget: PPS gets you under 1µs, the F9T-disciplined NIC PHC should be within 5–20ns of GPS time, but the sub-20ns figure is only system-clock-to-PHC and ignores kernel transfer delay.
- [unsnap_biceps](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602852): An operator running about a dozen CenterClick NTP 270s says roughly one locks up every six months and a PoE power cycle fixes it — a useful real-world reliability datapoint for the recommended turnkey option.
- [geerlingguy](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602078): Geerling reports his pool bandwidth ladder: 100 Mbit gave 180–250 requests/sec, and 250 Mbit gives 500–600 req/sec, which the Oxtrys VPS handles fine.

## 5. Fascism and Monopoly {#story-5}
*#7 on HN · [repository.law.umich.edu](https://repository.law.umich.edu/mlr/vol118/iss7/2/) · 42 points · 19 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50600521)*

A 2020 Michigan Law Review article by Daniel A. Crane examining whether industrial concentration helped Hitler rise to power. Drawing on business histories and U.S. Office of Military Government Decartelization Branch archives, Crane finds German big business mostly did not back Hitler before he became chancellor in 1933, but that extreme Weimar-era concentration of market power let Hitler seize and consolidate totalitarian control. He concludes the German case supports the idea that extreme economic concentration enables extreme political concentration, yet notes most Weimar conduct that created it would already be illegal under modern antitrust law, undercutting calls for a major enforcement shift.

**Discussion**: The thread quickly drifts from the article's antitrust thesis into a state-versus-corporation argument. One camp says concentrated corporate power is no worse than concentrated state power and that 'voting with your wallet' gives citizens real leverage over companies that they lack over government; others counter that wallet-voting collapses under monopoly or oligopoly, where there is effectively one name on the ballot, and that consumers can't opt out of industries they don't use (the tobacco example). A sub-thread turns on whether shareholders and elections really give comparable oversight, with one commenter arguing from personal experience that individual votes feel meaningless in both spheres, drawing the retort that this is perception rather than fact. A separate tangent invokes the horseshoe-theory claim that fascism and communism converge on a government-run economy, and another commenter questions why the state, the most privileged concentrated power, escapes the article's decentralizing logic.

**Worth reading**
- [delichon](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50603031): Argues the article never applies its own anti-concentration logic to the state, the most dominant corporate power of all, so decentralizing business while centralizing government just entrenches the stronger player.
- [thayne](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50603260): Sharpest rebuttal to 'vote with your wallet': under monopoly or oligopoly there is no competing option to defect to, like a democracy with one name on the ballot.
- [brightball](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50603181): States the pro-market side: consumers can exit a company but not a government, and regulation often raises barriers to entry, further entrenching incumbents.

## 6. A Better Vocabulary for Testing {#story-6}
*#8 on HN · [alperenkeles.com](https://alperenkeles.com/posts/vocab-for-testing/) · 68 points · 19 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50594182)*

Alperen Keles argues that the usual unit/integration/E2E ladder and the Atlassian-style lists of '7 types of tests' are too vague to act on, and proposes replacing them with five mostly orthogonal categories: the scope of the unit under test (function, class, module or system, crossed with stateful vs stateless), the purpose or hypothesis (functional correctness, execution time, security, fault resilience), and the oracle (human judgement, which he calls 'tinker testing'; concrete input/output examples including golden and snapshot tests; and inline assertions tied to design-by-contract). He notes DbC is native in C++26, D, Kotlin and Racket with library support in Python and Rust, and admits the framing is biased toward random testing, which he works on.

**Discussion**: Several commenters fixate on the functional/non-functional split: one wants it renamed 'cross-functional', another replies that 'functional' here is the mathematical sense of deterministic input-to-output mapping (a performance test fails that), and others agree the dichotomy hurts more than it helps across requirements and verification. The most substantive pushback is jerf's: testing is a continuum with no natural clumping, so labels like unit vs integration are barely worth keeping beyond the single step from zero dependencies to one — mocking, stubbing and staging environments all sit on the same sliding scale. FabCH argues the taxonomy misses how developers actually behave, citing their own IEEE paper finding that a large share of real tests use automation frameworks in ways their authors never intended; rao-v read it and adds that fully autonomous agent-written tests mostly fail to catch the regressions agents introduce, and that SQLite's commercial value is largely its regression suite. Others note that every testing book defines terms differently, that the old OOP claim a unit test must cover a whole object caused much of the confusion, and a side thread asks whether vocabulary matters at all now that coding agents can interpret intent.

**Worth reading**
- [jerf](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50601880): jerf's long argument that testing is fundamentally continuous with no natural clusters, making unit/integration labels nearly meaningless past the 0-vs-1 dependency boundary.
- [rao-v](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602797): rao-v reports that fully autonomous LLM-written tests are mostly wasted effort that miss the regressions agents themselves cause, and that SQLite's real moat is its regression suite.
- [FabCH](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602160): FabCH links their IEEE paper on what tests developers actually write, arguing developers ignore theoretical boxes and use frameworks in unintended ways.

## 7. Ask HN: Tips for transitioning from web development to embedded/firmware? {#story-7}
*#11 on HN · text post · 72 points · 54 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50555002)*

A text-only Ask HN post from llmblockchain, who spent the first years of a 15+ year career in native C/C++ development before a decade of fullstack web work, and now wants a job primarily in C, C++ or Rust — assuming embedded/firmware is the natural destination. The post asks how feasible that switch is this late and for advice; it makes no argument beyond the premise that embedded is where low-level work lives. The thread drew 54 comments and split cleanly between 'the doing is easy' and 'the getting hired is not'.

**Discussion**: The most-repeated advice is a blunt 'just do it': build a personal project, put code on GitHub, and lean on the 15 years of general engineering experience — several commenters note the market no longer rewards grinding LeetCode and spamming job boards anyway. The strongest counterweight comes from tptacek, who argues there has never been a better time to actually do low-level work but that paid embedded jobs have always been a bottleneck because there is simply far more high-level work than low-level work; an ex-embedded engineer confirms and says he left for devops, and another estimates low-level jobs are roughly 10x scarcer, offset by a thinner talent pool. A second camp warns about money and working conditions — lower pay, worse hours, 'shit conditions', aging vendor toolchains like GCC forks and QNX Momentics — while others describe embedded as a genuinely different mindset: hard real-time deadlines, no logging or debugger, IRQ-driven concurrency, and a habit of triple-checking because patching firmware is expensive. On AI the thread divides sharply: one camp insists LLM agents already do firmware and it is too late to enter, countered by practitioners saying SoC firmware is nowhere near solved — public code samples are scarce, docs sit behind paywalls, and subtle bugs take far longer to shake out than in web work. Practical tooling advice converges on ESP32 and ESP32-P4 boards, Raspberry Pi Pico/RP2354A (for PIO), Rust with RTIC or Embassy, KiCad plus JLCPCB, and cheap test gear; the rough consensus is that a hobby transition is trivially cheap and a career transition is possible but requires portfolio proof and patience.

**Worth reading**
- [tptacek](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50601894): tptacek separates the two questions — doing embedded work is easier than ever, but good paid embedded jobs have always been scarce relative to high-level work, and AI is not the main obstacle for a web-to-embedded switcher.
- [throwup238](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602933): A first-hand data point on how cheap hardware work has become: a PCB redesign cost under $2,000 for ten assembled boards versus $30,000 fifteen years earlier, and took a week instead of two months, with HALs and LLM-assisted bring-up carrying the load.
- [gortok](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50601340): Direct rebuttal to the 'LLMs solved firmware' claim from someone doing the work: SoC firmware has few public code examples for models to learn from, documentation is often paywalled, and subtle bugs take far longer to debug than on web systems.

## 8. Canada recommits to federal funding for news {#story-8}
*#14 on HN · [niemanlab.org](https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/11/a-government-that-gets-it-canada-recommits-to-federal-funding-for-news/) · 80 points · 61 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602847)*

A Nieman Lab piece on Canada's 2026 budget proposal, which would make the Local Journalism Initiative permanent with $134 million over four years and $41 million in annual ongoing funding, up from its current renewal-by-renewal status (LJI funds about 470 reporting positions across roughly 1,000 communities). The budget also extends the 35% refundable labour tax credit on newsroom salaries through 2028 — up to $29,750 per journalist, at a cost of $501 million over five years — and opens it to radio and TV newsrooms for the first time, while adding $300 million over two years to CBC/Radio-Canada. News Media Canada CEO Paul Deegan says the tax credit plus roughly $17,000 per journalist per year from the Online News Act's Google money works out to about $47,000 per journalist annually, and credits third-party administration of the funds and a federal ban on corporate political donations for the program's political durability.

**Discussion**: The thread splits sharply between skepticism that any government-funded journalism escapes bias or serves incumbents, and an argument that reporting is a market failure that will otherwise vanish. Critics point out the tax credit requires at least two journalists at arm's length from owners (which an advisory board found excludes mom-and-pop outlets), that eligibility runs through government-appointed adjudicators, and that the largest employers capture the largest share — several read the article as closer to a press release than reporting. Supporters counter that this is not public media but subsidized private newsrooms, that the money is trivial against a federal budget of roughly CAD$486.9 billion, and that without it local coverage of cops, courts, city hall and school boards simply disappears; one commenter living next to the US argues Canada needs deliberate support or its media gets steamrolled by Paramount- and Fox-scale American outlets, with 'maple MAGA' politics as evidence. There is a running sub-thread on whether the CBC and European public broadcasters are smug and left-biased (a Slovenian commenter describes his country's public TV that way) versus producing better foreign coverage of the US than US outlets do. Corrections land on cost framing: the $501 million is over five years, about $100 million a year, and CPB funding in the US ran every year from 1969 to 2025.

**Worth reading**
- [asdfasdfasdf222](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50603211): Argues the piece reads like a press release: the credit is not open to everyone, requires approval by a government-appointed council, and its ownership-separation rule excludes small community outlets, making it a subsidy to large chains.
- [jubilanti](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50603108): Puts the numbers in context — Canada's 2025 federal budget was CAD$486.9 billion — pushing back on the '$500 million, didn't know Canada had spare money' reaction.
- [uptrails](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50603107): Concedes the political-risk argument but argues journalism is not financially sustainable against clickbait and AI slop, so subsidizing reporting is necessary or you get only Facebook gossip and paid PR.

## 9. 87% of the Anthropic power will come from gas and 11% from coal in 2028 {#story-9}

**Worth reading**
- [mbgerring](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602593): A commenter who has worked in the energy industry since 2015 says the claim that renewables cannot meet load growth is incorrect, citing grid studies showing 24/7 renewable power is achievable.
- [adolph](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602380): Argues the report's grid data is stale and coarse — the Abernathy campus in Texas is modeled on 2023 eGRID county averages, while ERCOT's live mix shows about 10% nuclear and under 16% coal.
- [adev_](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50602860): Compares carbon intensity by jurisdiction — French nuclear around 5 gCO2/kWh, US gas 300–350, Chinese coal 800–900 — and says time-to-market, not ideology, is why US operators choose gas.

The submitter frames it as a fix for a practical gap: many e-readers have OPDS and WiFi but cannot reach a home server over Tailscale or similar.
