## 1. FM-1 Workbench {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [open.m-vave.com](https://open.m-vave.com/fm1-workbench) · 36 points · 13 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50488325)*

This is the Chinese-language manual for the FM-1 Workbench, a Web MIDI/SysEx editor that runs in desktop Chrome or Edge and manages M-Vave's FM-1 hardware: 8 tracks, six engines (DX7-style FM, 303 acid bass, 808 drums, PSG, Pluck, Sampler), 16 factory projects and per-track loops of 16/32/48/64 steps. It reads and writes projects, per-track timbres and the sample library with read-back verification, imports DX7 single patches and 32-patch SysEx banks, and packs 8 sample slots into a shared ~19.55 s (436 KiB) with WAV input up to 32 MiB / 120 s at 8–192 kHz. It also documents a firmware reflash to factory V15 that restores the 128 factory patches, the .fm1/.fm1t/.syx/.fm1s backup formats, full MIDI CC/clock/MMC behaviour, and notes that the standalone HTML can be downloaded for offline editing.

**Discussion**: The thread barely discusses the Workbench page at all — it is almost entirely about the third-party firmware ecosystem that has grown around the FM-1. Commenters recommend specific alternatives: Jiant as the best workflow so far (with a wish for proper 909/303 emulation), sloop and skiff for groovebox use, and thefirmwarehub.com as a directory of custom firmwares, though nobody firmly answers the poster's question about whether LOOM-8 is the pick. A parent asks what would let a music-obsessed kid compose multitrack tunes, including on an Android tablet on a plane, and gets pointed at browser tools like Beepbox and Chrome Music Lab plus a video survey of DAW-like firmwares. The mood is warm: DX7 patch compatibility plus community firmware turns a ~$50 box into a groovebox and a cheap way into dawless production, and M-Vave is praised for embracing rather than blocking that ecosystem — called a rare bright spot in post-LLM music tech. One commenter pairs enthusiasm for Chinese hardware value (60–80% cheaper than US/EU equivalents) with a caveat about exploited workers.

**Worth reading**

## 2. After 600 experiments, here's what I discovered about JEPA world models {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [haddyalchaer.com](https://haddyalchaer.com/jepa) · 118 points · 24 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50487185)*

A personal blog post by Haddy Alchaer reporting ~600 ablation and planning experiments on LeWM (LeWorldModel), a JEPA world model from a team including Yann LeCun, benchmarked on Push-T block-pushing. Swapping LeWM's learned predictor for the simulator's exact physics barely changed long-horizon success (77% vs 78% at a 75-step goal), while four planner-only tricks — 25-step subgoal chaining, a latent memory graph with a jump penalty, symmetrically transformed demonstration segments, and scoring goals against many pusher positions instead of one image — raised success from 22% to 85% and pushed harder cross-demonstration tasks from 45% to 56%, all without retraining the weights. The author also argues the standard Push-T test is easier than it looks: 84% of standard goals were nearly present in the memory graph, and fresh 250-episode sampling dropped LeWM from 68% to 44%.

**Discussion**: The thread is split between genuine interest and irritation at the presentation. The sharpest critique is methodological: a robotics researcher asks why every test goal is drawn from expert demonstration frames rather than arbitrary poses, warning this bakes in whatever statistical oddities the demo set has. Others push back on framing rather than substance — hierarchical planning, subgoals and action chunking are well-established, so the post reads as a personal learning journey rather than a novel result; a reply argues that expecting scientific novelty from a personal blog post is an unfair standard. A commenter points to an existing Gumbel-MCTS baseline that solves Push-T without training, and the author answers questions in the thread (confirming the memory-graph transform idea is his addition, not part of published LeWM; he also says he toned down the background animation after mobile complaints). One commenter says the post convinced him JEPA is a narrow robotics technique rather than an LLM rival, with a reply arguing JEPA-style embeddings suit fast, intuitive world representations where pixel-level reconstruction is unnecessary. A large share of comments are about the site itself — fonts, a distracting rainbow background animation, and rendering on older Intel Macs and phones.

**Worth reading**
- [robotresearcher](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50488606): A robotics researcher challenges the experimental design: goals taken from demonstration frames rather than independent samples from the underlying task mean the results may reflect peculiarities of the demo set and never demonstrate generalization to arbitrary start-goal pairs.
- [whiplash451](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50488547): Notes that Gumbel MCTS solves Push-T without any training, pointing to an existing repo as a baseline the post doesn't compare against.
- [sashank_1509](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50488478): Says the wall-of-text style reads as AI-generated and links a Pangram detection report to back the claim.

## 3. Reverse engineering my Coway air purifier to ditch the cloud {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [scottjg.com](https://scottjg.com/posts/2026-11-11-reverse-engineering-my-coway-air-purifier-to-ditch-the-cloud/) · 565 points · 159 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50474959)*

Scott Jiang walks through taking a discontinued Coway Airmega IconS off Coway's IoCare cloud and onto Apple Home. After using a Raspberry Pi as an isolated AP with mitmproxy and nftables redirects to grab the fan's traffic, he finds the punchline: the device fetches Amazon's root CA over plain HTTP from an S3 bucket (rootca-fs.iocareus.com), so it trusts whatever CA you serve it — the October 2026 eMudhra weak-key CA incident wasn't even needed. Control then runs over MQTT over TLS on port 8883 to an AWS IoT endpoint, and a bootstrap API call hands back the host, cert server, CA and private key URLs, meaning whoever controls that response controls the fan. The post also notes Claude refused to help set up the MitM rig while ChatGPT complied, and ends mid-build (truncated).

**Discussion**: The dominant reaction is admiration for how much sloppy design LLM-assisted reverse engineering is now surfacing: commenters argue the tedious parts, not the insight, were the barrier, and several report doing the same to Haier ACs (ESP32 boards with exposed UART pads, reflashed to ESPHome), Vornado fans and solar inverters. A substantive correction comes from one commenter who points out that serving certificates over HTTP is normal PKI practice (the AIA field works that way), and the real bug is trusting an unauthenticated certificate at all. Others speculate on why Coway did it — hardware vendors treating firmware as a cheap BOM line item, fear of hardcoded certs expiring with no OTA path — and one engineer counters that Philips got this right, with mTLS and Secure Boot blocking the same attack. A large side argument is about purifiers themselves: Corsi-Rosenthal boxes, MERV-13 versus HEPA, and just running the central HVAC blower, with defenders of bought units citing noise and aesthetics. Recurring themes: why buy a cloud-dependent purifier when Coway's dumb models work, and worry that vendors will use AI to harden devices and that frontier models like Opus 5.5 increasingly refuse this work.

**Worth reading**
- [sippingabonedry](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50488526): Argues the HTTP certificate download is a red herring — PKI is designed to serve certs over HTTP (see the AIA field) — and the actual flaw is implicitly trusting an untrusted certificate.
- [Night_Thastus](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50487081): Long practitioner argument that HEPA is the wrong choice for home purifiers: optimize CADR and noise instead, use MERV 13/14 and off-the-shelf furnace filters rather than custom-fit HEPA that dies with the product line.
- [alnwlsn](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50486260): Points to a teardown of a similar Coway model showing the motor is continuously variable, so all this work ends up exposing only three fan speeds.

## 4. Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026 (1960) {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [science.org](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.132.3436.1291) · 385 points · 267 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50482379)*

A 1960 Science paper (vol. 132, p. 1291) by Heinz von Foerster and colleagues at the University of Illinois' electrical engineering department, which fit two millennia of world population growth to a hyperbolic curve and extrapolated a singularity — infinite population — for Friday, 13 November 2026 (commenters quote the fitted date as 2026.87 ± 5.50 years). The authors framed it as a provocation rather than a prophecy, with a line about the fun of extrapolating that should not be overlooked; the article itself could not be fetched (HTTP 403), so the details here come from the title and the thread. The date was the lead author's birthday, which commenters read as the source of the Friday-the-13th joke.

*Article not read: HTTP 403*

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people citing the paper as proof that extrapolation is folly — world population is nearing its peak and rich countries now fear decline, not explosion — and people pointing out that 「nothing bad happened last time」 is itself a lazy extrapolation, with nuclear weapons and a roulette gambler invoked as counterexamples. A long subthread argues over whether overpopulation or depopulation is the real crisis: Ehrlich's failed 1968 famine predictions and his lost bet with Julian Simon are cited against the doomers, while others counter that Simon's wager covered a decade of metal prices, not aquifer depletion or climate, and that age pyramids and pension systems make shrinking populations genuinely destabilizing. Several commenters make the technical correction that the paper's fit was hyperbolic, so it predicted an actual finite-time singularity rather than merely 「approaching infinity」, and that population growth stopped tracking the curve soon after publication. The rest is date jokes: Marvel's Avengers: Doomsday opens 17 December, and the same-day Outlook and Claude outages were offered as the real doomsday.

**Worth reading**
- [cubefox](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50485300): Explains the math the thread keeps getting wrong: the authors fit hyperbolic growth, which predicts a true finite-time singularity, not an asymptote — and notes population stopped fitting the curve shortly after 1960.
- [pmontra](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50485481): Identifies why the date is a Friday the 13th: von Foerster was born on Friday 13 November 1911, so the doomsday date was a pun (he would have turned 115).
- [bananaflag](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50482717): Links Scott Alexander's 「1960: The Year the Singularity Was Cancelled」, which covers this paper and the broader history of cancelled population singularities.

## 5. Show HN: Isometric SF – A pixel-art map of the SF Bay Area {#story-5}

**Discussion**: 最大的争议在于它是否算像素艺术。几位评论者认为不算：他们指出像素艺术需要调色板缩减和方形网格对齐——每个网格方块纯归为一种颜色——否则 AI 生成的高频噪点和边缘杂色会保留下来；有人把这些图块送进 spritefusion-pixel-snapper 来处理。一位评论者抱怨「pixel art」一词像「vibe coding」一样被稀释成没有意义，并指出人工像素画师会使用可复现的重复图案，扩散模型却做不到。有人提出用代码模型并按网格规则渲染场景，而不是用图像模型，这样直线和平面才能真正平直。一位作者与另一位创作者的对比显示，训练之后，成本分别为 $0.09/km²、$0.40/km²（后者是 tinyvalencia 项目）。较小的抱怨是地图朝东而不是朝北或朝西面向海洋，而且无法旋转视角；在技术问题上，作者确认他先用 Google 的 3D Tiles API 抓取图块，再用 three.js 按选定角度渲染，并指出有人提到每月 1000 次免费 API 调用足以复刻类似项目。

**Worth reading**

## 8. A technical vision for the Haskell ecosystem {#story-8}
*#8 on HN · [discourse.haskell.org](https://discourse.haskell.org/t/a-technical-vision-for-the-haskell-ecosystem/15078) · 137 points · 55 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50484235)*

A Discourse post laying out a proposed technical direction for Haskell, whose stated goals — as quoted and paraphrased by commenters — include making Haskell's onboarding 「the best in the programming language ecosystem」, a commitment to Unix principles of small tools that do one thing well, and fixing compile times and incremental builds. The author (identified in the thread as hasufell) also touches on the developer/debugging experience and on where Haskell should try to be best rather than everywhere. The linked page itself could not be fetched, so the specifics here come from the title and what the thread quotes from it.

**Discussion**: The thread splits in several directions. **Worth reading**

## 18. Concurrent Shell Command Queues {#story-18}
*#22 on HN · [emmer.dev](https://emmer.dev/blog/concurrent-shell-command-queues/) · 12 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50486521)*

A blog post by emmer.dev that builds a small thread-pool-like command queue in Bash and Zsh: queue_start N launches xargs -0 -n 1 -P N reading NUL-separated commands from a FIFO in a private temp directory, queue_submit writes commands over time, and queue_wait drains and cleans up. Subshells inherit the queue through a file descriptor, but output is captured rather than streamed, queue_wait cannot report job failures, and each shell gets only one queue. The author calls it a fun exercise and concedes most people should use Python instead.

**Discussion**: Commenters mostly reached for make: js2 describes generating a Makefile with one curl rule per URL and running make -j, which also skips files already downloaded, while another praises make for free parallelism, interruptibility, restart and incremental updates. js2 notes the article's dynamic-submission requirement is exactly what make cannot handle, putting it in the same bucket as xargs -P and GNU parallel. On the article's aside about shells having poor concurrency controls, kernc lists &, wait and jobs as abundant primitives, while Joker_vD argues they are awkward in practice and offers a subshell + trap 'kill 0' SIGINT + wait pattern, saying the parentheses are essential for Ctrl-C propagation. A final reply quips that this may indict most programming languages rather than shells.

**Worth reading**
- [js2](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50487827): Gives a concrete Makefile alternative where one rule per URL plus make -j provides bounded parallelism and automatically skips already-downloaded files.
- [Joker_vD](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50488020): Argues shell job control is fragile and supplies a compact subshell, trap 'kill 0', and wait recipe, warning that braces instead of parentheses break Ctrl-C handling.
- [kernc](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50487825): Pushes back on the claim that shells lack concurrency controls by pointing to &, wait and jobs as built-in primitives.
