## 1. Hurried Announcement: Alessandro Masullo GRH {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [math216.wordpress.com](https://math216.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/hurried-announcement-alessandro-masullo-grh/) · 559 points · 205 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50579568)*

A short, hastily written post on Ravi Vakil's Math 216 algebraic geometry blog announcing that Alessandro Masullo has a proposed proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, a roughly 940-page draft titled 'The Music Of The Primes'. Vakil says Brian Conrad has been scrutinizing it for months and given extensive feedback that is being incorporated, that the work is entirely human with no AI, and that the plan had been to bring Masullo to Stanford for a year to polish the writing before announcing — but it is being released now as a draft. The rush is widely read as a response to Anthropic's competing, Lean-formalized GRH proof from Levent Alpöge announced the same day.

**Discussion**: The dominant thread is provenance anxiety: many commenters suspect the 12-year human proof leaked into an LLM's training data or was fed to Claude by reviewers, though others note the two approaches are reportedly quite different, and a large contingent argues the real story is simply that AI can now scoop any long-running secret project, so mathematicians should publish early. A second argument concerns verification: defenders of the Anthropic result say a 250k-page Lean proof is machine-checkable while a 940-page paper is not, and others push back that Lean only checks the statement you formalized and can hide gaps unless the statement itself is vetted — while some say the short human proof is the more valuable artifact because it can actually be understood. Commenters report that LLMs (and ex-OpenAI math staffer AcerFur) claim to have found errors or counterexamples in the Masullo draft, with skeptics objecting that pasting a paper into a chatbot is not review; seasoned voices like woopwoop stress that Vakil's post is not an endorsement by Conrad, Sarnak or anyone else, only a signal that serious people are looking. There is rough agreement that neither claim is settled, that priority norms in mathematics are being forcibly rewritten, and considerable irritation at the AI-lab framing of the whole thing.

A short PDF note by mathematician Levent Alpöge announcing a proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, with a Lean formalization linked in a footnote as fourteen tarball parts (alpo.ge/lean.tar.gz.part01–14). He says the formalization is finished but the human-readable argument is a 250,000-plus-page document he has not yet produced, and that Claude outpaced him near the finish line; his dated outline runs roughly Oct 6 to Nov 16, 2026, and commenters note the timing lands a day after Anthropic's IPO. The note also claims implications for the Extended Riemann Hypothesis, and a separate 'hurried announcement' from Alessandro Masullo of a human proof appeared the same day.

*Article not read: not a web page (application/pdf)*

**Discussion**: The thread splits three ways. The technically engaged camp treats the Lean artifact as the real news and moves straight to verification: commenters post download scripts, file and line counts (175,187 files, ~17.37M lines, ~1 GB), live compilation streams on spare AWS cores, and note that the statement introduces no new axioms, so the remaining uncertainty is Lean itself and whether the formalized statement matches the intended one. A second camp is unsettled by the sheer size — if the proof is unreadable, is it knowledge? — and by the distribution of credit, with several people arguing the model, not Alpöge, did the hard part while others insist the framing understates a top-tier mathematician who has spent years on the problem. ## 3. Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21885) · 234 points · 90 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50577265)*

An arXiv preprint (2611.21885, submitted 16 Nov 2026, revised 18 Nov) by Jiajun Fan and 10 co-authors claiming 'self-rewriting': an open 27B model, given its own weights, graded answers and the ability to run itself, diagnoses a failure and edits its weights directly with no gradients, backprop or training run — only forward passes. It reports a 6.6-point accuracy gain on unseen problems, monotonic improvement over eight consecutive editing sessions by testing edits on practice problems, and repair of a deliberately damaged copy back to over 80% of original accuracy without ever reading the original weights; smaller models in the same family either make no edit or break themselves. The authors frame it as a step toward recursive self-improvement, arguing the training/inference split is a limit of our tools, not of intelligence.

**Discussion**: Commenters are largely unconvinced that this is new capability rather than a narrow bug fix. Several read the improvement as one thing: the 27B model (Qwen3.5) shortened runaway reasoning traces to escape the token limit, which they say is essentially the same result as the earlier rikiyat 'weight-space programming' post — and that post found no gains on models without that flaw (Gemma). Others clarify the 'no training' claim just means no backprop: the model writes a Python script that computes aggregated differences between error-state and non-error-state activations and pushes weights along that direction, i.e. it approximates gradient descent by hand. The harshest reading is that this is abliteration-style rank-1 editing that requires no understanding of what the weights do, and that the paper's LLM-flavoured marketing prose ('Welcome to the Era of Self-Rewriting') undermines trust in the results. A counter-thread argues the point is the loop, not this instance — that with weight-editing traces in pretraining data, or RL on the capability, models could learn real weight introspection; skeptics reply that true self-modification would need changes to structure or dimensionality, and that fine-tuning is more principled and efficient. Side threads worry about alignment (the word never appears in the paper) and swapping test-set leakage for self-graded practice problems.

**Worth reading**
- [tb99](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50579868): Walks through Appendix E5: the model finds an error category (too many tokens), aggregates the difference between error and non-error states, and pushes weights along that direction — an approximation of gradient descent, not superhuman insight into weights.
- [fwlr](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50580682): Argues it is literally the same result as the earlier weight-space programming work, with the flawed-and-patched model presented after the smaller siblings to spin the finding as 'emerging at scale', and notes the paper reads as wholly LLM-generated prose.
- [quotemstr](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50579182): Contends the paper does not show direct weight editing at all: clipping deliberately introduced outliers and the loop fix (a rank-1 abliteration tweak) need no understanding of what the edited parameters do.

## 4. Unforced Navier-Stokes Singularity and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [terrytao.wordpress.com](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/unforced-euler-singularity-unforced-navier-stokes-singularity-and-the-generalized-riemann-hypothesis/) · 137 points · 24 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50578358)*

A short post by Terence Tao flagging three AI-assisted results from Levent Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster (with Matei Coiculescu on the first), ordered by how far along they are toward being digested: a human-readable, Lean-formalized proof of finite-time blowup for the unforced incompressible 3D Euler equations; a Lean-formalized proof of finite-time blowup for the unforced 3D incompressible Navier-Stokes equations whose strategy is clear but which still needs a properly written paper; and, far less digested, an enormous Lean-only proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis generated by Alpöge with an internal AI model, now being verified in a Lean Zulip thread. Tao says the GRH proof appears to use the full power of the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry. If the Navier-Stokes blowup result holds, it settles the global-regularity side of a Millennium Prize problem negatively — though Tao himself does not editorialize on significance.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people asking how AI-assisted proof generation changes mathematics itself and people arguing over what the results mean. The most-discussed question, from softwaredoug, is where new abstractions (like the Langlands program) come from if AI only brute-forces proofs over existing ones — QuesnayJr answers that nobody knows and notes Langlands is a 'program' whose special cases already implied Fermat's Last Theorem, while others assume AI will handle abstraction too. A second cluster complains that Tao announces an alleged RH proof without discussing its significance; one reply defends him as being deliberately cautious after his earlier AI-in-mathematics posts drew more (and more hostile) attention than expected, and another argues a proof nobody understands is not yet useful. The most striking counterpoint comes from a mathematician, emil-lp, who feels neither excitement nor terror but 'apathy', calling it '4CT on speed' — i.e. the Four Color Theorem controversy repeating at higher speed — which prompts an explainer of that 1970s episode. Elsewhere commenters report excitement at the pace and deflation at being priced out by the scale of compute involved, and one corrects the assumption that Tao's post is a repost, noting his site carries invited guest posts.

**Worth reading**
- [softwaredoug](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50580358): Asks the deepest question in the thread: if AI is brute-forcing proofs over existing abstractions, how do genuinely new abstractions like the Langlands program get created — and does that still require social agreement within the math community?
- [nater5000](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50580777): Pushes back on the complaint that Tao omitted the significance, arguing readers don't need a PhD to grasp it and that Tao is simply being careful after his earlier AI-in-math posts drew far more attention than he expected.

## 5. We promoted developers into architects without informing them {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [manidoraisamy.com](https://manidoraisamy.com/developer-to-architect.html) · 80 points · 39 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50579331)*

A blog post by the maker of a Google Forms add-on describing how a colleague 'vibe coded' install logging: a try/catch chain that fetched the user's email, then their user ID, then logged an error — each one-liner hiding several Google API calls, so the static welcome screen took 15 seconds to load. The author's argument is that AI tools optimise functional requirements plus security and backward compatibility, never performance or data-model simplicity, and are sycophantic enough never to push back on requirements. His prescription: developers should design the data model first, constrain the AI to that architecture, and review its output — a job nobody announced they had been given.

**Discussion**: The dominant pushback is that this is not a promotion at all: several commenters say pushing back on requirements and owning the data model was always part of being an engineer, and that anyone who only typed what a ticket said was already in trouble. Others split the role differently — some say architects who only draw diagrams and hand off designs are just as exposed as code monkeys, and that AI is flattening architecture and development into one job; a few argue architecture itself will be automated. A separate, more pessimistic thread is about labour rather than craft: that salaries came from having a legible, specialised job and that rationalising it with AI will end badly, with scepticism that engineers can organise the way help-desk workers failed to. Among practitioners, the most concrete counterpoint is that the article describes poor AI use rather than an inherent limit — one commenter says Claude Code now profiles and optimises code well and that the real work is managing the model's recurring bad habits, which have included leaving debug tooling unflagged until an app becomes a keylogger. A few also question the premise itself, asking whether weekly install monitoring is worth blocking a welcome screen for at all.

**Worth reading**
- [bikeshaving](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50580376): First-hand account from the maker of an IPA keyboard that Claude left its debugging tooling enabled in production code, effectively turning the program into a keylogger; argues the real job is managing the model's shifting foibles, that performance is largely solved by Claude Code, and that security remains an endless nightmare.
- [whycombinetor](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50580253): Challenges the article's premise: every SWE job this commenter has held expected them to push back on requirements and design the data model, and in-house architects were often figureheads, so the 'promotion' framing misreads what engineers already do.
- [shimman](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50580820): Labour-history argument that developers are paid well only because the job is comprehensible and specialised, and that rationalising it with metrics and AI has never once benefitted labour — so the pay will fall unless engineers act collectively.

## 6. Finite-time blow-up for the Navier–Stokes equations {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/tristanbuckmaster/navier-stokes/) · 181 points · 57 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50576232)*

A GitHub repository hosting a 79-page paper, 'Finite-time blow-up for the Navier–Stokes equations' by L. Alpöge and T. Buckmaster (version dated 20 November 2026), together with a Lean 4 formalization (Lean v4.33.1 with Mathlib) of its main theorems. The formalization is roughly 2,270 files and 600,000 lines, and the README is unusually candid about what is missing: the repository contains no data, the certificate whose hash the paper cites is not included and is only promised for later release, the interval-arithmetic verification (128-bit precision, Arb via FLINT) is not part of the formalization, and the paper's source is absent. The package also carries a 20 November patch to the cell-layer statements — a bound's weight becomes (|m|+1/2)^2 instead of m^2+1/4, one cell constant may be zero, and the harmonics in a cell are restricted to those that do not vanish there — and was not rebuilt after patching.

**Discussion**: The thread is split between awe and accounting. Several commenters frame this as one of two enormous results in a single day — the other being a claimed proof of the generalized Riemann Hypothesis — and note this is the unforced Navier–Stokes problem, whereas OpenAI's earlier example required a carefully designed external force, making this version more 'realistic'. The loudest reservation is the repository's own disclosure that the certificate, the verification computation and the paper source are all absent, which some read as results announced ahead of the evidence. A long-running argument concerns credit: one camp says the model did the bulk of the work and OpenAI's practice of crediting the model is honest, with sympathy expressed for human formalizers such as Kevin Buzzard; others insist the Riemann and Navier–Stokes work was collaborative and that academic attribution norms already handle this, while sceptics ask whether the model merely recombined ideas already in its training data and complain that transparency (session transcripts) is missing. A separate physics subthread converges on the point that Navier–Stokes is a low-energy effective theory, so a mathematical blow-up would not mean a real fluid reaches infinite speed — cavitation, ionization, phase change and radiative losses intervene long before that.

**Worth reading**
- [binsquare](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50579317): Quotes the repository's own admission that there is no data, no certificate, no verification computation and no paper source, and argues the details are therefore lacking.
- [tancop](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50577963): Draws the key distinction that this is the unforced problem, unlike OpenAI's example which needed a designed external force.
- [evanb](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50578957): Explains that Navier–Stokes is an effective field theory that must break down at small scales, so a singularity says nothing about real fluids.

## 7. Vibecoding an Inference Chip {#story-7}
*#7 on HN · [ankitg.me](https://ankitg.me/blog/vibecoding-inference-chip/) · 39 points · 11 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50579297)*

A blog post by Ankit Gupta describing how he pointed cloud coding agents (OpenAI Codex, Claude Opus, Astra, running in Conductor) at an AWS F2 FPGA instance with 16 GB of HBM and got a ternary-weight LLM running entirely on the fabric. Over several days of mostly low-effort prompting the agent went from a first bit-exact full-model baseline to 213 tokens/s — a 33× speedup — writing roughly 3 million lines of C++ and Verilog across the experiment history while burning about $10,000 in API tokens. He pastes in a long design/verification prompt from the DeepGrove founders; the agent's own retrospective concludes its core architectural ideas were already planned, it rejected all five numerical shortcuts because they broke bit-exactness, but the process advice (skip day-long simulations, iterate with short C tests and logic-depth checks) immediately paid off.

**Discussion**: The thread is small and mostly appreciative, with several people treating transformer-on-FPGA as the new 'hello world' for coding agents. cpldcpu reports having done essentially the same thing with Opus 4.5 and links a public repo, while another commenter asks the author to share the code, which the post does not do. The sharpest factual addition is that the post never names the model, but a trace in it mentions Maple, a 20B model, and commenters judge ~70+ tok/s for that on an FPGA to be genuinely good. The cost claim draws the most skepticism: one reply notes $10,000 of API spend is roughly what a $200/month subscription would cover, and the follow-up joke about who actually pays lands on corporate customers. One dissenting comment argues the result is unsurprising because FPGAs are software-driven and the model can verify its own outputs cheaply.

**Worth reading**
- [cpldcpu](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50580885): cpldcpu says they did a comparable transformer-on-FPGA build with Opus 4.5 and links the smollm.c repo as a concrete point of comparison.
- [datadrivenangel](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50580158): Points out the post omits the model's size, but a trace names Maple, a 20B model, making the ~70+ tok/s figure notably strong.

## 8. If you can't explain something in simple terms, you don't understand it (2017) {#story-8}
*#8 on HN · [kottke.org](https://kottke.org/17/06/if-you-cant-explain-something-in-simple-terms-you-dont-understand-it) · 529 points · 265 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50572355)*

A short 2017 kottke.org post by Jason Kottke collecting quotes rather than making an argument: the Feynman anecdote (from David Goodstein's Feynman's Lost Lecture) about failing to reduce why spin-one-half particles obey Fermi-Dirac statistics to a freshman lecture, and concluding 'that means we don't really understand it'; a John Gruber claim that Apple engineers are expected to explain complex technology simply as proof of understanding; Feynman on not being able to explain magnets in more familiar terms; and the 'What I cannot create, I do not understand' line found on his blackboard when he died in 1988.

**Discussion**: The thread splits cleanly. One camp argues the aphorism is mostly used as an excuse: listeners who won't do the work blame the explainer, and genuine understanding (the thread's running example is the open-cover definition of compactness) requires pencil-and-paper effort the explainer cannot supply. The other camp says understanding and explaining are separate skills, that some knowledge is spatial or embodied (riding a bike, a chef's feel for ingredients), and that many people understand a topic deeply but communicate badly — several commenters also note that 'simple' is meaningless without specifying the audience. A recurring middle ground: simple does not mean short (3blue1brown, Feynman's own 15-minute answers), and the saying is a useful self-check heuristic rather than a law. The sharpest corrections are that the original quote says 'freshman level', not 'simple terms', and that the magnetism example is itself a counterexample to the article's thesis. A thread drift lands on AI: Claude does not 'understand' what it writes, and several people link Claude Code skills (including Jane Street's socratic-tutor skill and a 'Richard Feynman' plugin) meant to force exactly this kind of explanation.

**Worth reading**
- [mr_mitm](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50573815): Argues the article misrepresents its own sources: Feynman said 'freshman level', not 'simple terms', and the magnets quote is an admission he can't map it onto everyday experience, not that nobody understands magnetism.
- [driveby_cat_tal](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50577549): A detailed math counterpoint to the top comment: compactness is better motivated through sequences and infinite dimensions, where closed-and-bounded stops being enough — the abstract open-cover definition is forgettable without the motivation behind it.
- [amzok](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50573709): Corrects a widely repeated wrong simplification in the thread: NP means 'easy to verify', not 'hard to compute' — that is NP-hard or NP-complete.

## 9. Frostbite: A new CPU architecture discovered, designed and verified by AI {#story-9}
*#9 on HN · [architectlabs.com](https://architectlabs.com/blog/frostbite) · 544 points · 291 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50572503)*

Architect Labs, following an earlier AI-designed inference accelerator called Redwood, presents Frostbite: a general-purpose CPU with a new ISA built on interaction nets, where programs are graphs rewritten locally so that independent work is exposed without threads. The company claims 4.59x performance-per-watt versus a modern silicon-proven dual-issue in-order superscalar core, measured over 25 workloads of which 15 were held out until final evaluation. AI agent swarms iterated compiler, ISA, architecture, microarchitecture, RTL, verification and GDS together, driven by an in-house formal specification language called ASL (lowered to 800k+ lines of Lean) and a fast 'Arcmodel' simulator (claimed 660x faster than RTL); out of 58 proposed ideas, 37 were kept. No silicon has been fabricated, and the article was truncated mid-way through the architecture details.

**Discussion**: The thread splits sharply. Sceptics focus on the benchmark's framing: the baseline is an in-order dual-issue core rather than a modern out-of-order design, the kernels are tiny (fib(20), mergesort of 1024 elements, 4096-character JSON parsing), and nothing has been taped out — several note that prior dataflow work (WaveScalar, the 1980s dynamic dataflow machines, TRIPS, CGRAs) explored the same space and went nowhere. Defenders and the poster argue the perf/W comparison is defensible since out-of-order cores do worse on that metric, that interaction nets are a genuinely different model rather than a renamed dataflow graph, and that even a PCIe accelerator niche would count as success; several say the AI framing poisoned the thread, with hot takes about AI and jobs crowding out the hardware (a long subthread drifts into whether modern software quality has declined). Engineers raise concrete design worries: no program counter or call stack, so software would essentially have to be written in the Vine language ported alongside, plus new bug classes (stuck nets, leaked values, debugging with no stack), Amdahl's law, and how the design handles large mutable data structures — the tested kernels are small-working-set and compute-heavy. Others trace pedigree: Architect Labs reportedly raised a $24M seed while Tendrils Compute raised $30M with what commenters call the same approach and the same Vine/Ivy languages, and critics dismiss the whole thing as 'AI slop' whitepaper marketing from a company whose founders did internships.

**Worth reading**
- [barthold123](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50573586): Alleges Architect Labs copied the interaction-net/Vine approach from Tendrils Compute and contrasts the two companies' seed rounds ($24M vs $30M), with links.
- [pizza234](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50572930): A CPU-focused sceptic argues the baseline is an in-order core rather than out-of-order, the workload set is small, and WaveScalar explored dataflow architectures 20+ years ago.
- [mtimpe](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50576846): Notes the architecture apparently lacks a call stack and program counter, so a Vine source-level debugger would be a major project, and describes new bug classes like stuck nets and leaked values.

## 17. The G1 Garbage Collector {#story-17}
*#20 on HN · [dev.java](https://dev.java/learn/jvm/tool/garbage-collection/g1gc-overview/) · 73 points · 30 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50527444)*

A dev.java overview of G1, the JVM's garbage collector, tracing it from an experimental feature in JDK 6u14 through promotion in JDK 7u4 and becoming the default in 'server' environments in JDK 9 (JEP 248), to becoming the default in all environments in JDK 27 (JEP 523) — including single-CPU, sub-2GB machines. It explains the region-based heap layout (eden, survivor, old, humongous regions for objects over half a region), the Young-Only and Space-Reclamation phases of the GC cycle, and the 'garbage-first' efficiency heuristic for picking old regions. Tuning guidance is deliberately conservative: keep -Xmx roughly 25% above the expected live set, match -Xms to -Xmx with -XX:+AlwaysPreTouch, treat -XX:MaxGCPauseMillis as a soft goal, and otherwise let the heuristics work — with Automatic Heap Sizing flagged as an upcoming change.

**Discussion**: Two clusters dominate. The practical one is Kubernetes tuning: brunoborges lists the JVM's default heap fractions (50% of memory up to 256MB, ~128MB between 256–512MB, then 25% above that), warns that the JVM spawns parallel GC and other threads even under a 1,000-millicore limit and so throttles early, and argues a few larger pods beat many small ones — even at equal aggregate CPU and memory. That claim gets pushback: seabrookmx and brunoborges himself clarify that a CPU limit of 1 in Kubernetes is a share of CPU time, not a single visible processor, so the JVM on an 8-core node still sees 8 cores, which complicates the warning that many 1-CPU pods 'have been on SerialGC all along'; others note SerialGC's lower CPU and memory overhead makes it the right pick for pegged single cores or minimal footprint, while G1 wins on pause consistency. On collector choice, commenters say Java 17 is where G1 got genuinely good, that Parallel can beat G1 on pause times for small heaps, and that ZGC keeps gaining features. A separate, larger argument erupts over whether AI-written code makes garbage collection obsolete: one side calls GC a readability-for-efficiency trade that no longer pays; the other (including titzer) counters that manual and ownership-based memory management are not inherently faster — bump-pointer allocation, compaction and live-data-proportional GC work can beat manual frees — and that memory management should stay orthogonal to program logic.

**Worth reading**
- [brunoborges](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50579926): Concrete Kubernetes-era defaults and pitfalls: JVM heap sizing fractions by memory tier, thread pools that ignore small CPU limits and trigger throttling, and the argument that 3 larger pods outperform 6 smaller ones at the same aggregate resources.
- [seabrookmx](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50579643): Corrects the common claim about 1-CPU pods, explaining that a Kubernetes CPU limit caps CPU time, not core count, so Java on an 8-core node still sees 8 processors.
- [titzer](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50580761): Strong technical rebuttal to 'GC is wasteful': bump-pointer allocation is very fast, GC cost scales with live data rather than dead data, and compaction gives locality wins that manual deallocation doesn't.
