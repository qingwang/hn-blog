## 1. Amazon saves millions by replacing Sonnet 4.6 with Strands Decider {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [strandsagents.com](https://strandsagents.com/blog/amazon-saves-millions-replacing-sonnet-with-strands-decider/) · 147 points · 41 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50436640)*

An AWS blog post by two Amazon engineers, Carr and Mehul, describing how they replaced Claude Sonnet 4.6 with Strands Decider — a small open-source decision model, in its E4B Gemma 4 build — for labeling tickets into one of 20 categories in Amazon's internal ticketing system. They claim parity or better label quality at 98% lower token cost: roughly $22,200 down to about $500 per million tickets, with average call latency falling from 4,215 ms to 897 ms and tokens per ticket from 6,724 to 2,010, served on SageMaker ml.g5.xlarge instances (one A10G, ~19 GiB of model weights). They credit a three-way AppConfig switch (BEDROCK / DUAL / DECIDER) that ran both models on live traffic and let them roll back with a config change, and warn that JSON key ordering shifted output probabilities by up to 0.19, plus that the approach only fits closed-choice, high-volume, short-context tasks. It reads in places like a product case study for a model AWS launched a month earlier.

**Discussion**: The thread mostly treats this as a return to classical ML, with several commenters asking why an LLM was ever used to sort tickets into 20 buckets when SVMs, scikit-learn or BERT would do it faster and cheaper — one links a 2021 ICSE paper on an SVM-based log classification pipeline they built. The strongest counter-argument is that classical classifiers need labeled domain data and a data scientist, while an LLM gets you a working prototype in minutes with no training set and tolerates categories being added and removed; the emerging consensus is that LLMs are fine for bootstrapping a feature and should then be optimized into something smaller once the label catalog settles, likened to Electron apps trading efficiency for developer speed. Others poke at the arithmetic — saving millions at ~$21,700 per million tickets implies on the order of 90 million tickets a year — and at the framing, noting Strands is AWS's own open-source project rather than an outside vendor, so the post is closer to an advert using internal adoption as a case study. One commenter running an ML tooling company warns that these decision-model refactors often hit production friction and see rollbacks to LLMs, since the models are less expressive for real application logic.

**Worth reading**
- [armcat](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50436847): Six years ago they built a ticket classification pipeline on SVMs that trained in minutes and classified in milliseconds, and links the Linnaeus paper accepted at ICSE 2021 — evidence the field has looped back to where it started.
- [wodenokoto](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50436989): Argues you cannot train an SVM on a ticketing system without domain data, whereas an LLM needs only someone who can wire up APIs, handles category changes without retraining, and is the right thing to optimize away later.
- [Kaedon](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50436881): Does the arithmetic: at roughly $21,700 saved per million tickets, saving millions a year implies at least ~92 million tickets annually, which is plausible given alarms, security findings and capacity requests all generate tickets.

## 2. Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [anthropic.com](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2) · 500 points · 756 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50429068)*

Anthropic 发布 Claude Fable 5.2 和 Claude Mythos 5.2。两者是同一个底层模型：Fable 面向所有用户，带有较强的网络安全和生物安全防护；Mythos 则通过验证计划向受审查的组织开放。文中称新模型输出速度比 5.1 快约 30%，输入和输出价格分别降到每百万 token 8 美元和 40 美元（可选 fast mode 在价格翻倍的基础上，生成速度提升 2.5 倍）。发布稿还宣称，1000 个 Fable 5.2 agent 用 30 小时完整形式化了费马大定理（Opus 5.5 用同样的设置只完成 26%）；以及一个 agent 花八天时间从空目录构建出能启动的操作系统，并让 Python 服务迁移到 Rust 后启动快 26 倍。文中还写到，模型解决了两个黑洞物理的开放问题，设计了三台电子设备，并在机器人评测上取得了进展。

## 3. USPS now offers official ZIP Code boundary polygons {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [postalpro.usps.com](https://postalpro.usps.com/zcb) · 601 points · 301 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50431058)*

这是 USPS PostalPro 上的一个产品页面，不是文章：它出售「ZIP Code Boundaries」数据集，声称基于投递地址和位置数据给出权威边界，每季度更新四次，分为 Internal、Enterprise 和 Publication 三个层级，并附带许可协议、技术指南和 GeoJSON 示例。页面没有标价，但评论者从订单表上查到最低档为 1 万美元，最高档达 15 万美元，且许可证禁止再分发或转售。

**Discussion**: 压倒性的反应是愤怒：联邦数据理应以近乎零的增量成本公开，而这里却被定成了企业级价格；多位欧洲、荷兰的评论者指出，本国的邮编数据由政府机构免费发布，甚至直接作为 QGIS 图层使用。关于「USPS 为什么缺钱」的争论最激烈，也出现了关键纠正：有人说国会要求它提前几十年为养老金注资，但有人指出那其实是退休人员医保，且 2022 年的《邮政服务改革法》已经取消了这一要求，另外 USPS 自 1970 年代以来基本不拿纳税人拨款、靠邮资和产品销售自筹，同时被要求像企业一样运营。技术派反复强调 ZIP code 从来不是地理区域，而是一组投递路线的标签：PO Box、单栋建筑、跨州、一条街两侧分属不同邮编都真实存在，因此多边形只能是近似，这也解释了为什么 Census 的免费 ZCTA 数据集成为默认替代品，发帖人自己也按这份数据对比过。还有从业者提到 USPS 的地址验证 API 2026 年 8 月也开始收费，以及有人试图用众筹买下数据再公开，会因为许可证和可能的数据指纹而不可行。

**Worth reading**
- [munro](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50432321): 发帖人 munro 把 USPS 的波多黎各样例数据与 Census 2026 年的 ZCTA 做了逐块对比，结论是差异不大、 Census 模型更简单，因此打算继续用免费的 Census 数据，并顺带发现「ZIP Code」是注册商标。
- [shiflett](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50435188): 一位 1999–2003 年在 USPS 工作的评论者说，USPS 的数据历来与处理代码分开授权，还故意掺入假地址（DPV false positive）用于检测滥用，因此「买一次然后公开」这条路走不通。
- [anamexis](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50431916): 解释了法律依据：39 U.S.C. §410(c) 给了 USPS 豁免，使其可以不予披露「商业性质的信息」，所以常规 FOIA 走不通。

## 4. Your TV can see through walls {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [maurycyz.com](https://maurycyz.com/projects/fading/) · 64 points · 18 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50436410)*

Maurycyz's project write-up shows a spectrogram of a local ATSC digital TV station at 490 MHz, captured with an Ettus USRP B200 software-defined radio. Because the direct signal is partially cancelled by a strong frequency-dependent reflection (selective fading), the received signal becomes extremely sensitive to nearby motion: standing up or walking around a neighbouring room produces striped nulls in the waterfall display. Counting those stripes (≈70 px apart at a 150 Hz update rate → 2.1 Hz beat) and applying λ = c/490 MHz gives an estimated walking speed of 1.3 m/s (4.6 km/h), and the author notes any wideband signal — WiFi, LTE — works the same way.

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly an enthusiastic “and it gets worse” tour rather than a critique of the method. Commenters pile on related prior art: numlock86 describes extracting audio from fibre-optic cable by detecting tiny light-path disturbances (limited per link, since repeaters regenerate the signal), several recall Van Eck phreaking and the CRT-emission work that recovered screen contents through walls, and others link the MIT RF-through-wall research plus a startup that estimated traffic flow from 4G path loss and scattering (arXiv 2101.09143). On the practical side, people ask why Home Assistant has no Wi-Fi presence sensor, and two are supplied: the ESPectre Wi-Fi motion-detection integration and a Zigbee-based presence discussion, alongside a sub-$10 ESP32 spectrum-sensing project (ESPSDR/espargos) and a RuView repo for turning commodity Wi-Fi into presence and vital-sign sensing. The privacy angle is raised — someone outside could infer when the house is empty — and largely deflected by the reply that a phone not in airplane mode already discloses that, with security-camera audio streams flagged as a more realistic indoor-motion side channel. A couple of commenters point out the effect is old news to anyone who grew up with analogue antennas, where standing in the wrong place broke up the picture.

**Worth reading**
- [1e1a](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50437203): Links a sub-$10 ESP32-based SDR setup (espargos/ESPSDR) for observing Wi-Fi and LTE spectra, making the experiment accessible without a USRP.
- [sudorm-rf--no-p](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50437315): Answers the “why no Home Assistant integration?” question with an actual ESPectre Wi-Fi motion-detection community integration.
- [cowlevel](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50436990): Blunt counter to the privacy worry: an unairplaned phone already tells observers whether you are home.

## 5. Locating the source of a mysterious beep {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [simonwillison.net](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/9/mysterious-beeps/) · 137 points · 65 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50434310)*

Simon Willison's blog post describes building 「Beep Locator」 after something in his box room started beeping intermittently: a Python web server coordinating several spare devices (two iPads, a phone, a MacBook Pro, a Surface) as browser-based listeners using WebAudio, WebSockets and a 60-second ring buffer with FFT beep detection. Listener clips are aligned by a server-triggered chirp, an approach drawn from the 2007 BeepBeep paper by Peng, Shen, Zhang, Li and Tan, plus a 「rough mode」 for dragging device positions and a clap mode for calibration. It located the beep to within about 40cm, leading him to an ancient carbon monoxide detector that wanted new batteries; the system was built mostly with Opus 5.5 in Claude Code Cloud from his iPhone, then iterated live with Fable 5.2, with a hot-swapped installModule() mechanism to push new JS to listeners without reloading pages and losing mic permission.

**Discussion**: A large share of the thread is a joke chain about solving it with a physical binary search over the boxes — split the pile, listen, repeat — which others extend into Big-O arguments: the moves are O(n) in total (n/2 + n/4 + …), and standing around waiting for a 62-second beep interval adds a brutal constant factor. The more substantive scepticism comes from commenters who tracked down beeps themselves by timing the interval and homing in on the sound, arguing the AI-built system took more effort than the problem deserved and gave no real sense of accomplishment; Willison answers that he would never have learned the audio and WebAudio material otherwise and got a reusable tool in about 90 minutes. Others point at off-the-shelf acoustic imaging cameras as the practical alternative — the Fotric TD2e at roughly $1,000, a $700 eBay Fotric Sonic 3 with a 64-mic array, or a DIY build on the miniDSP UMA-16 — and owners report they work well for air leaks and intermittent sounds. One commenter calls 「ask the model to write problems.md」 the emblem of AI-slop RFCs that skip real reasoning; Willison concedes he wouldn't do it for code others review, but says here the model genuinely did the reasoning for him. Side threads cover why pure-tone beeps are hard to localize (few overtones, no attack; white-noise backup alarms are easier), and a reminder to add a natural-gas alarm alongside the CO detector.

**Worth reading**
- [simonw](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50436355): Willison gives the cost: it fit inside his $200/month Anthropic subscription, and the Fable 5.2 laptop session would have been $31.04 at API prices (517.9k context, 331.4k output).
- [simonw](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50437286): Willison's direct rebuttal to the 「where's the satisfaction」 critique: without agents he would not have learned any of the audio/WebAudio material, and the alternative was learning nothing rather than learning more.
- [radicality](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50435755): A commenter who bought the Fotric Sonic 3 acoustic camera on eBay for ~$700 says its 64-mic array works well and would be hard to replicate at that level.

## 6. E-means: Outperforming k-means in one-tenth the epochs {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [e-means.github.io](https://e-means.github.io/) · 374 points · 34 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50429734)*

该网站是 Faiss 贡献者（Swann Bessa、Hugo Touvron、Hervé Jégou、Matthijs Douze、Pierre-Emmanuel Mazaré 等，arXiv:2611.09501）提出的新 k-means 更新规则，已并入 Faiss，模块为 faiss.contrib.e_means。它用 mini-batch 上的指数移动平均替代 Lloyd 的全量重算，并借助两条拟合出的 scaling law（分别调整 batch size 和步长）自动选择超参数，从而无需人工调参；另加余弦学习率衰减、比例切分复活死簇以及收尾精修阶段。在从 10 万到 1 亿点的 24 个数据集和 234 组配置上，它只需 1.2–3 个 epoch 就能达到 Lloyd 在第 25 个 epoch 时的测试损失，端到端在 GPU 上比 Flash-KMeans 快 10–45 倍、在 CPU 上比 Faiss 快 8–15 倍；作者还声称它更不容易陷入局部最优。

## 7. Horible public key tricks {#story-7}
*#9 on HN · [maurycyz.com](https://maurycyz.com/projects/badcert/) · 138 points · 43 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50434264)*

A blog post by maurycyz.com explaining how to get a trusted TLS certificate whose RSA public modulus, when base64-encoded, spells out a readable message — the certificate shown literally contains 「You+dont+actually+have+to+pick+random+private+keys / No+one+checks+these+things / A+certificate+authority+got+away+with+the+number+4+for+a+year」. The method: pick two random primes, edit the bytes of n to embed the message, set q = n/p, round up to the next prime (usually a few hundred away), and since the message sits in the upper half of n it survives; base64 gives roughly 150 characters of space at RSA-2048 and 320 at RSA-4096, and misalignment usually resolves within four attempts. The author concedes the key loses entropy and that his script doesn't check for close primes or small d, but argues forward secrecy makes a compromised key survivable for a public website.

**Discussion**: The sharpest pushback comes from tptacek, who argues the post's premise is dated: purely passive attackers are extinct, all serious attackers are man-in-the-middle attackers (usually by diverting victims' own traffic, e.g. DNS injection), so an unauthenticated TLS session cannot be called 「secure」. A large subthread debates whether CAs should sanity-check customers' private keys: arcfour insists it is not the CA's job to stop you from being stupid and that brute-forcing keys is infeasible at Let's Encrypt's scale of ~10 million certificates a day, while others note Tom7 found at least one ACME CA that does a cursory check for a tiny prime, and one commenter points to the IETF CSR attestation draft as a real answer. Several people push back on the 「ACME is burdensome」 framing, recalling $1000+/year EV Verisign certificates that required faxing legal documents, though a reply notes TLS simply wasn't needed to publish a site back then. Off-topic branches cover Tom7's 「World's worst TLS server」 paper (also available as a video), resentment at Apple's $99/year notarization fee, and the blog's terminal-style 「tty」 mode. Commenters also noticed the typo in the headline (「Horible」), and the submitter suggests the unusually dense typos may be a deliberate human-authorship signal.

**Worth reading**
- [tptacek](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50434692): tptacek argues passive-only eavesdroppers no longer exist and that calling an unauthenticated TLS session 「secure」 is simply wrong.
- [hxtk](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50435451): First-hand detail that some ACME CAs still sign RSA certs actually brute-force the key to catch a tiny prime — Tom7 hit this and had to switch to a four-prime modulus.
- [Curiositry](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50435797): The submitter notes the post's typo density (yeild, pivate, relevent, chalange) looks intentional, linking the author's own page about typos as a quality indicator.

## 8. Ultorg: The User Interface for Relational Databases {#story-8}
*#10 on HN · [ultorg.com](https://www.ultorg.com/) · 40 points · 9 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50435898)*

Ultorg is a desktop, general-purpose graphical front end for relational databases from Eirik Bakke, the researcher behind the long-running SIEUFERD project, now publicly released after roughly 17 years. It presents real SQL queries as a spreadsheet-like surface, visually laying out arbitrary one-to-many joins, nested groups and subqueries, editing data in place (with review before commit) and auto-deriving formatting defaults, with no cloud component. It connects to Access (.mdb/.accdb) and Excel (.xls/.xlsx) today, with Oracle, Snowflake, SQLite, Athena and Exasol in beta, and the site carries a testimonial from Harmonic co-founder and CEO Tudor Achim calling it the best tool for visualizing and investigating their data.

**Discussion**: The thread is small (9 comments) and mostly admiration for the interaction design — several call it the best SQL admin interface they have seen, better than Metabase — paired with resistance to the commercial model: commenters object to a subscription for a small desktop app and ask for a perpetual license around $500 instead. One person who has actually used it says it shines when the data already follows good practice (consistent unique IDs across tables and sheets), and that it is easier to design work around Ultorg than to migrate existing files into shape; they also worry about losing access to carefully tailored files if they stop paying. Others redirect to alternatives — LINQPad and its .Dump() method, plus two open-source options: a project posted on HN the same day and DBFlux. A question about extensibility (adding custom buttons, e.g. to export an invoice) drew no answer, and one commenter dismissed the visual design outright.

**Worth reading**
- [kragen](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50436679): Kragen praises the 17-year research effort but refuses the proprietary terms, and links his own 5,200-word written notes (sieuferd.md in his pavnotes2 repo) plus Bakke's 5-minute 2016 talk, noting Ultorg implements DML but not SQL's DDL.
- [akshayrajeshwar](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50436824): A prior user reports it is amazing on well-structured data with consistent unique IDs, but that starting a project with Ultorg in mind beats migrating existing files, and that a perpetual license would suit a business better than a subscription.
- [maxloh](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50437094): Points to an open-source alternative posted on HN the same day that the commenter says has a better-looking UI.
