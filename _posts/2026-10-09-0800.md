## 1. Why isn't the industry freaking out about DeepSeek 4.1 Flash? {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [dgt.is](https://www.dgt.is/blog/2026-10-07-deepseek-freek-out/) · 611 points · 480 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50000488)*

A blog post by a developer who has used DeepSeek 4.1 Flash heavily for a month across a dozen projects. The central claim is that the model is effectively frontier-level in day-to-day work — the author says that mid-session they cannot tell whether they are using DeepSeek or Opus — while costing orders of magnitude less, thanks to a KV cache roughly 437x smaller than DeepSeek V1 and a $10/month OpenCode Go subscription that makes usage nearly unlimited ($0.003 instead of $1 for small tasks, rarely over $1 per session). The author argues the labs should be panicking because Chinese distilled models are good enough for unattended, complex work, and that chasing the frontier is now silly for most tasks.

**Discussion**: The thread splits sharply. The dominant counter-argument is that nobody feels an urgent need to switch because OpenAI and Anthropic subscriptions are so heavily subsidized that a $20–$200 plan already beats open-model API prices; several commenters note they burn through $50 of API tokens in days while a subscription covers far more. A second camp insists the article overstates the model: critics say DeepSeek 4.1 Flash is off the cost/intelligence Pareto frontier versus Luna, Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol, that it is a 「hallucination factory」 and 「overthinks like an intern made CEO」, and that the real gap to Opus 5.5 is 6–12 months, not the 「a month or two」 the author claims. Defenders counter with first-hand numbers: one user reports spending $1–2/day running it all day as an orchestrator with Opus doing final review, another says 40B tokens and ~$800 over 30 days, and several describe using it as the lead agent in Pi/OpenCode harnesses at 250–350 tok/s. Notable subthreads correct the article's own numbers — commenters point out the model ships pre-quantized in native NVFP4/MXFP8, that the ~200GB n-gram table lives in host RAM, and that it can run on roughly 300GB VRAM rather than the 1.6TB FP16 figure one commenter cited. A recurring side debate covers providers (OpenRouter accused of overcharging up to 100x versus buying direct from DeepSeek) and data/privacy risk of sending work to Chinese-hosted models.

**Worth reading**
- [wren6991](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50012676): Does the actual arithmetic on the model's memory footprint: the LM backbone is 307.2 GB (286.1 GiB) and the 203.1 GB n-gram table goes in host RAM, so the 「FP16 needs 1,664 GB」 claim is wrong and full-precision/INT8 versions of the released model simply do not exist.
- [user43928](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50012504): Strong counter-argument to the article's premise: argues open models are 6–12 months behind, still fail to beat February's Mythos/Fable 5, and that if progress keeps up the leading labs have nothing to fear.
- [try-working](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50013174): First-hand practitioner data point: over 40B tokens and $800 spent on DeepSeek API in 30 days, concluding it can do most work but only with phased runs, verification and subagents, unlike frontier models.

## 2. Show HN: Quake ported to safe Rust, playable in browser {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [quake-srp.pages.dev](https://quake-srp.pages.dev/) · 110 points · 86 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50016312)*

The linked page is quake-srp (「slop rust port」), a browser-playable port of id Software's 1996 Quake written in safe Rust, software-rendered via WebAssembly with no GPU and no dependencies, bundling the shareware episode (id's original quake106.zip, 9.1 MB). The page documents full keyboard/mouse/gamepad controls, console commands, mission-pack support (Scourge of Armagon, Dissolution of Eternity) and the option to drop in your own Quake .pak files and CD music, all stored in the browser. The repo also ships an 「oracle」 visual-diff harness that compares frames against the original game in an attempt to reproduce it pixel-for-pixel.

**Discussion**: The thread splits hard over whether an LLM-assisted port is worth anything: one camp calls it trivial for an LLM and pointless since Quake's C code was already solid and C2Rust existed long before (one commenter notes every commit is from Claude), while another defends it as a fun side project and points out the real achievement is the decades of browser/WASM/Rust infrastructure, not the translation itself. Commenters also argue over whether 「safe Rust」 (merely no `unsafe`) means much, and over whether Rust's memory safety would actually eliminate the quirks and exploits that gave old games their charm — with disagreement about whether release builds check integer overflow. Several corrections surface: Quake was written in C, not C++ (id's first C++ game was Doom 3 in 2004), and the famous fast inverse square root is from Quake 3, not Quake 1. A recurring darker thread worries that this same technique makes it cheap to clone/spoof trusted systems (banking backends, Amazon logistics, QR parking-payment scams). There's broad agreement the port is genuinely smooth and playable, and one commenter notes a code-frequency graph suggests it was 「human assisted」 despite the LLM framing.

**Worth reading**
- [flohofwoe](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50016844): Correction: Quake was written in C, not C++ — id's first C++ game was Doom 3, around 2004.
- [aroman](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50016508): Praises the repo's bundled 「oracle」 visual-diff harness (linked) as the reason the port could be driven toward pixel-perfect parity with the original.
- [parasti](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50016723): First-hand counterpoint: the commenter spent five years hand-porting Neverball to the web and links the result, illustrating what these LLM ports sidestep.

## 3. Reducing undefined behavior in the C language {#story-3}
*#4 on HN · [lwn.net](https://lwn.net/Articles/1095811/) · 91 points · 62 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50015074)*

A write-up of a Kernel Recipes talk by Martin Uecker, a biomedical-engineering professor who writes free software for MRI scanners, arguing C is still worth fixing rather than abandoning. He walks through how C89's abstract-machine model and its ~100 undefined-behavior cases let compilers do anything (including time-travel), notes the in-progress C2y draft removes 45 of them, and C23 already dropped K&R functions, trigraphs and sign-magnitude support while adding bit-precise integers and checked integer operations. He concludes full memory safety is possible only via expensive runtime checking or formal verification, and that C2y will add case ranges, named for loops and _Countof() but not memory safety.

**Discussion**: The biggest split is over whether UB-driven optimizations should be visible: one camp wants loud compiler warnings whenever a branch is collapsed because of UB, while others (including a self-described compiler writer) say that is infeasible — UB is usually a runtime condition, so checks would either slow C down or flood users with warnings they cannot satisfy. A second thread features the author of Fil-C arguing that Fil-C and CHERI close off memory-safety bugs at the ABI level and are more comprehensive than Rust; a commenter counters that both only attach provenance to allocations, so a buffer overflow can still clobber an adjacent struct field, though CHERI is said to support sub-object capabilities. Several people push back on the framing of C as 「high level assembly」, noting real high-level assembly has no UB and C has UB precisely because of aggressive optimization. Commenters also debate signed-integer overflow (define it as wrapping, trap it, or leave it UB to keep runtime sanitizers meaningful), and whether C2y could make reads of uninitialized malloc memory defined without breaking every implementation.

**Worth reading**
- [pizlonator](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50016256): The author of Fil-C argues Fil-C and CHERI attack memory safety at the ABI level and are more comprehensive than Rust, since Rust's guarantees only cover code actually rewritten in safe Rust.
- [afdbcreid](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50016413): Provides a Godbolt example where reading uninitialized malloc memory followed by a v<0 || v==0 || v>0 guard compiles to a bare exit(1) in Clang -O3.
- [strenholme](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50016207): First-hand report testing the old uninitialized-malloc-as-entropy trick across GCC and Clang (up to Clang 23) and TCC, finding the crypto routine always ran rather than being optimized away.

## 4. Keyboard differences between Windows and Macs {#story-4}
*#5 on HN · [unsung.aresluna.org](https://unsung.aresluna.org/deeper-dive-keyboard-differences-between-windows-and-macs/) · 73 points · 69 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50015515)*

A reference post by Marcin Wichary (unsung.aresluna.org) cataloguing the platform-specific keyboard gotchas that bite cross-platform web apps. It documents that Windows' Backspace is Mac's Delete and Windows' Delete is Mac's Forward Delete, that Windows' Enter is Mac's Return (with macOS also having a separate numpad ⌤ Enter reachable via Fn+Return), and that roughly every Ctrl shortcut on Windows becomes ⌘ Command on a Mac, which gains an extra ⌥ Option modifier. It also covers macOS's Emacs-style ⌃A/E/K text-field keys, ⌥ and Windows AltGr/Ctrl+Alt character layers, symbol legend differences (⎋ ⇥ ⌫), clashing redo (⌘⇧Z vs Ctrl+Y) and refresh (⌘R vs F5) conventions, and warns web apps to avoid ⌥ / Ctrl+Alt shortcuts in text fields and to expect some combos (⌘Q/⌘W/⌘T) to be reserved by browsers.

**Discussion**: The thread mostly treats the post as a useful reference, then broadens into an argument about which platform's scheme is better designed. A recurring claim is that Apple got the Command key right — a dedicated modifier for GUI actions keeps terminal-centric Ctrl free — while Windows got dedicated Home/End/PgUp/PgDn keys right; several commenters wish for both on one system. Linux desktop switchers (spankalee, zdw, wpm) describe the Ctrl situation as the worst of all worlds, with Ctrl-C meaning both copy and abort depending on context, though others counter that this is fixable by remapping and that Ctrl-C meant interrupt for decades before copy. Threads on the article's mock keyboards, external-keyboard Command/Alt swapping (Logitech, Karabiner, the macOS Modifier Keys setting), and the British PC layout for fixing the broken backtick key run alongside. A few people add corrections and history, and the Enter-vs-Return naming draws a small intra-Apple dispute over whether the two keys should be distinct functions.

**Worth reading**
- [disillusioned](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50016662): A 35-year Windows user describes the daily muscle-memory pain of moving to a Mac — e.g. his habitual Ctrl+Shift+Left word-select is Shift+Option+Left on macOS, which he calls hell to relearn.
- [miki123211](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50016586): Adds technical corrections: right Alt is literally mapped to Ctrl+Alt on AltGr layouts, Polish shift+backtick is a dead key that breaks C++ identifiers like cFooManager, and apps should rely on macOS menu selectors rather than handling copy/paste shortcuts themselves.
- [m3galinux](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50016276): Historical account that Ctrl+Del / Ctrl+Ins / Shift+Ins were the original cut/copy/paste, that Ctrl+X/C/V came from the Macintosh and were adopted in the Windows 3.1 era to match Word for Mac, and that this collided with Ctrl+C's older interrupt meaning.

## 5. Man discovers his parents' coffee machine used 1TB of data in 10 days {#story-5}
*#6 on HN · [dexerto.com](https://www.dexerto.com/entertainment/man-discovers-his-parents-coffee-machine-used-1tb-of-data-in-10-days-3416399/) · 607 points · 363 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49995495)*

This is a short Dexerto aggregation of a viral X post by a user called Nomad, who helps manage his parents' home network remotely and spotted on their UniFi dashboard that their smart Keurig coffee maker had moved roughly 1TB of traffic in 10 days. Nomad, who says he has worked in IT for over 10 years, clarified that almost all of it stayed inside the local network rather than going out to the internet, that it saturated his access point, and that he suspects a bug in that particular unit; he concluded it wasn't worth keeping an IoT device connected when it can misbehave like this. The article adds little technical detail, and the original evidence is just a screenshot of UniFi client traffic.

**Discussion**: The thread splits hard between people who think this is a Keurig privacy scandal and people who think it is a broken dashboard. A recurring correction: UniFi's per-client bandwidth accounting is notorious for wildly overstating usage, with several commenters posting their own machines reporting absurd terabytes, so the 1TB figure itself is doubted and nobody has produced a packet capture or interface counter to settle it. A second dispute is about motive: one top comment claims Keurig admits the scans let it sell household data to advertisers, and a long reply argues that is false or misleading, since Keurig's terms cover brewing habits, not network reconnaissance, and that the thread's quote has been widely misread. Others insist that if scans are real they are a negligible-cost bug on Keurig's side rather than intentional backhaul, while the privacy-minded note that probes are locally cheap and a compressed summary is what actually gets sold. Practical consensus, where anyone agrees, is that these devices belong on an isolated IoT VLAN or guest SSID without internet access and without device-to-device visibility, and many commenters simply ask why a coffee maker needs Wi-Fi at all.

**Worth reading**
- [hn_throwaway_99](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50016425): Corrects the thread's central claim: Keurig says it may sell information about coffee brewing habits, not household device data harvested by network scans, and calls the widely repeated quote outright false or misleading.
- [malbs](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50015507): First-hand counter-argument from a UniFi user: the dashboard routinely reports nonexistent terabytes of usage for ordinary clients, with a screenshot of a laptop showing 24TB from email and browsing, so the 1TB reading may be a UI bug.

## 7. I hired an illustrator to draw my house. Now it's my Home Assistant dashboard {#story-7}
*#8 on HN · [antonfrolov.substack.com](https://antonfrolov.substack.com/p/i-hired-an-illustrator-to-draw-my) · 545 points · 96 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49986882)*

Anton Frolov's Substack post describes commissioning an environment illustrator to hand-draw his two-floor house and garden, then turning that artwork into a Home Assistant dashboard built on the stock picture-elements card. The brief asked for PNGs with transparency (WebP for animations), day and night versions of every floor, and per-device on/off frames exported on one shared canvas so layers line up; the article's tricks are state_image to swap a still frame for an animated WebP, a helper plus sunset/sunrise automation to flip day/night, Browser Mod for a custom irrigation dialog, and hosting assets under /config/www. It also corrects the widespread claim that LG's webOS browser can't display WebP (it can — the false claim comes from the TV's photo viewer) and works around webOS's inability to set a default page by running the HAOS Kiosk Display add-on into an HDMI input.

**Discussion**: The most active thread is about credit and the human-vs-AI question: one commenter links the illustrator directly and argues they should be credited far more prominently, others note the artist is credited at the bottom and that a mid-article link is exactly where reader interest peaks, and the spouse of an illustrator applauds hiring a real artist rather than scraping styles into image models. A competing camp argues the result is easily replicable with current AI given how detailed the brief was, while others push back that the human authorship is precisely why it's interesting; several people share first-hand attempts at AI-generated floor plans with visibly mixed results. A separate debate questions whether smart-home tinkering is worth it at all, with defenders citing child-door sensors, off-peak load shifting, water-leak and freeze monitoring, and 40% heating-bill savings. There's also a technical argument over the author's line about AC units switching off when everyone leaves: several commenters say letting a house warm up and re-cooling is always more efficient over long periods (thermodynamics), while others concede it can differ for heating thick-walled structures or very short cycles. Rough consensus: the dashboard is impressive and the detailed artist brief is the real lesson.

**Worth reading**
- [ckozlowski](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50015210): Commenter married to an illustrator praises the author for hiring a real artist and details why a good brief plus an expert's judgment on composition and color produced a better result than AI scraping.

- [overtone1000](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50015626): Detailed thermodynamics rebuttal to the idea that keeping AC running is more efficient, arguing shutdown only costs extra for cycles under about an hour and is always cheaper over longer spans.

## 8. Yes, and {#story-8}
*#9 on HN · [htmx.org](https://htmx.org/essays/yes-and/) · 344 points · 99 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50003796)*

An essay on htmx.org by Carson Gross (recursivedoubts), a computer science professor at Montana State University, arguing that AI does not remove the value of learning to program. He warns that juniors who let AI generate code instead of writing it never develop the ability to read code or control complexity — falling into what he calls the Sorcerer's Apprentice Trap — and rejects the analogy that prompting is to coding as high-level languages were to assembly, because compilers are broadly deterministic and LLMs are not. He recommends using AI as a teaching assistant rather than a code generator, and closes with job-hunting advice favoring the 「four F's」 (family, friends, family of friends) over online job boards.

**Discussion**: Gross himself appears to say the most effective vibe coders he has seen are already excellent developers, and that while hand-written code will decline, understanding how systems work may grow more valuable. A long sub-thread argues over the compiler analogy: several commenters say the real difference isn't determinism but that you can formally reason about how a source change alters a compiled program, while others counter that C++ undefined behaviour means you never know the output until you run it. Practitioners are split on whether coding skill still matters — one reports building complex software for months without reading a line, another says a non-engineer vibe coder out-delivered the team's SWEs, and one says AI used properly is now a better programmer than they are, while a manual-coding holdout insists the speed-versus-quality trade is not worth it. Job-market talk is gloomy: an interviewer says recent college hires struggle with open-ended coding questions, and several note the networking-centric advice shuts out people without industry connections.

**Worth reading**
- [layer8](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50012822): Reframes the determinism debate: the key difference isn't randomness but that you can formally reason about how source-code changes affect a compiled program, whereas any prompt change can only be tested by running the model.
- [jeffreyrogers](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50015761): An engineer who conducts interviews says the current batch of college hires is noticeably worse at an open-ended non-LeetCode coding question, and wonders about the long-term effects of declining coding fluency.
- [dirtbag__dad](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50014953): First-hand account of a non-engineer vibe coder who doesn't know git yet built an app serving enterprise customers better than the team's SWEs, alongside the observation that LLMs still fail badly on brownfield work in old human-written systems.

He read the released preprint (not the enormous Lean code) and calls it unclear, muddled and badly structured, flags out-of-place lemmas (7.4 and 8.1) and citations to unpublished, unrefereed lecture notes, and says it would earn a desk rejection at a journal. He refuses to send Sam Altman the bottle of whisky he had promised on his Problems page, and refuses to spend his time decoding the paper.

**Discussion**: The thread splits: many agree OpenAI's unreadable dump is harmful — one commenter calls it a 「slop grenade」 and notes OpenAI spent roughly $20M on the effort while apparently $20 on the write-ups — while others argue nobody is forced to read it, the Lean-verified proofs are very likely correct, and a younger generation is already building on the results. The strongest context comes from commenter sanxiyn, who says OpenAI asked mathematicians whether to release and was advised to do so, and that the release is really an eval now that FrontierMath is saturated (the batch is 372 of 4000 attempted problems). Counterpoints cite Scott Aaronson's account of his wife Dana using a tool called Astra to understand a new result in her own field and becoming excited by it. A software engineer draws the parallel to reviewing AI-generated code from a non-developer: you cannot tell if it is valid, and the enthusiasm is stripped away. The thread also brawls over whether the author is genuinely 「forced」 to engage and whether poor exposition is unique to OpenAI, with no clear consensus beyond a loose agreement that OpenAI should have collaborated with mathematicians.

**Worth reading**
- [sanxiyn](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50014661): Explains the release is an eval, not a brag: OpenAI asked mathematicians about releasing, was advised to, and now uses open problems to replace the saturated FrontierMath (372/4000 attempted).
- [pelican0](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50014707): Argues the results are still worth engaging, citing Scott Aaronson's post about his wife Dana using Astra to understand a new result in her own field and becoming excited to talk about it.

## 20. ADHD as a circadian rhythm disorder: evidence and implications for chronotherapy (2025) {#story-20}
*#24 on HN · [frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1697900/full) · 270 points · 160 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50011928)*

A December 2025 perspective article in Frontiers in Psychiatry argues that circadian disruption is a prevalent, clinically important phenotype in a substantial subgroup of people with ADHD rather than a universal feature. It cites insomnia in up to 80% of adults and 82% of children with ADHD, delayed sleep-wake timing in up to 78%, and DLMO (dim-light melatonin onset) delays of roughly 45 minutes in children and 90 minutes in adults, alongside blunted/delayed cortisol, reduced pineal volume and attenuated BMAL1/PER2 clock-gene rhythms. Reviewed trials show 0.5 mg nightly melatonin advanced DLMO ~88 minutes with a 14% symptom reduction in adults, 10,000-lux morning light advanced DLMO ~31 minutes, and a 3-week multimodal night-owl protocol shifted DLMO ~2 hours and cut depression scores ~58%; the authors propose a behavioral-first pathway of fixed wake times, morning bright light, evening light restriction and selective low-dose melatonin.

**Discussion**: A self-identified chronobiologist with ADHD (randomImmigrant) leads the skeptical thread: the association is real but causality is bidirectional, many brain processes are clock-regulated, and ADHD lacks the core clock mutations that would make it a circadian disorder in the strict sense — though he calls the link strong enough to justify more research. A cluster of night-owl commenters (TeMPOraL, alterom, parpfish) argue the authors invert causation, treating known consequences of ADHD — delayed sleep phase, staying up late, revenge bedtime procrastination — as causes, and note that forcing sleep does not address the reasons people stay up. Several commenters attack the venue, calling Frontiers a low-quality outlet and citing a July 2025 Retraction Watch report of 122 retracted articles (emil-lp, nickledave). Others dispute whether the paper is novel, noting its main cited studies date from the 2010s (falcor84), while a large side-thread on sleep apnea urges anyone with chronic fatigue to get a sleep study, with one commenter reporting major gains from an oral appliance. A heated sub-thread debates stimulant medication versus behavioral/sleep interventions, with ctolsen and jplusequalt arguing medication is by far the most effective ADHD treatment and sun/sleep advice is only mild mitigation, against gchamonlive questioning how many people are medicated unnecessarily.
