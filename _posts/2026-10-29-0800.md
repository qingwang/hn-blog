## 1. For the first time ever, Google Search is less than 50% of Alphabet's revenue {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [kadoa.com](https://www.kadoa.com/companies/insights/google-search-below-half) · 224 points · 151 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50291945)*

A Kadoa data piece charting Alphabet's revenue mix: in the quarter ending September 2026, Google Search & other brought in $63.4B of $128B total revenue — 49.7%, the first time below half since Alphabet began breaking out Search. Across the prior 31 quarters the share ranged from 52.8% to 62.0%, while Google Cloud climbed from 4.4% to 24.1% ($30.7B). The article stresses that Search revenue still grew; the share fell only because everything else grew faster.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between readers reading it as the decline of search itself — AI answers cannibalizing queries, worsening results, AI Overviews pushing people to Kagi and DuckDuckGo — and those doing the arithmetic, who point out Search revenue is up roughly 12% year over year and the percentage only dropped because Cloud nearly doubled its share. Several note the actual bad news in the earnings was an EPS miss ($2.12 vs. about $3.01 expected, with the stock down 13–15%), attributed by some to AI capex rather than weak operations, and one commenter rebuts that operating income isn't more 「actual」 than EPS. A long subthread argues over an X post claiming Google reworked its ad auction and exact-match/close-variant matching to squeeze more revenue, with replies split between calling it anecdotal nonsense and saying the mechanics of tweaked pCTR estimates and the AdTech antitrust findings (Project Bernanke) make it credible; others counter that advertisers tracking ROAS and CPA would simply shift budget. A separate argument over whether Google has ever made anything itself versus only buying YouTube, Android and Maps ends mostly with the retort that Google built those acquisitions into the products they are. Several comments correct the segment-level confusion about what counts as Google Cloud (GCP, Workspace, Gemini Enterprise) versus consumer subscriptions.

**Worth reading**
- [JumpCrisscross](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50293123): Cites Alphabet's 10-Q to correct the premise: Search & other revenue grew 12% YoY, paid clicks +8%, cost-per-click +4% — the Network decline is a separate, web-only segment.
- [mlmonkey](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50292787): A practitioner explains how search auctions can inflate revenue: the engine computes pCTR itself in the bid_i+1 × pctr_i+1 / pctr_i formula, so tweaking predicted click-through rates directly raises prices paid.
- [happyopossum](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50292243): Clarifies the segment definitions — Google Cloud covers GCP, Workspace and enterprise services, while consumer subscriptions like Google One and Gemini consumer plans sit under Services.

## 2. We're releasing the weights of Mistral Large 4 {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [mistral.ai](https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-large-4-oss/) · 714 points · 186 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50289418)*

Mistral published the open weights of Mistral Large 4 (「Le Chonk」), a 1-trillion-parameter natively multimodal model (1T total, A52B active) trained from scratch on European infrastructure, three weeks after its public preview. The post claims the best open-weight coding model: 19.7% on Cognition's private FrontierCode 2.0, 48% on the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index (tied with GLM-5.3 at 47% fewer tokens per task), and a 13-point jump to 41% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, plus 51% on the AA Cyber Index and a 96.6% refusal rate on malicious cyber requests. It adds three reasoning modes and quotes endorsements from Booz Allen, Cisco, Cloudflare, CrowdStrike, Elastic, ENISA, Fortinet, Orange Cyberdefense, Rubrik and Zscaler.

**Discussion**: Most of the thread is a fight over the word 「open」: the license is a modified MIT that requires a separate commercial agreement for anyone running a Model-as-a-Service business, and many commenters argue this is 「weights available」, not open weight, drawing the Redis/Elasticsearch/Terraform precedent and the OSI's open-source-AI definition (which also demands training code and data composition). Others push back that Mistral never said open source, that internal and commercial non-hosting use is unrestricted, and that only neocloud token resellers need a license; a sub-thread argues the terms may be unenforceable anyway since model weights likely lack copyright. A parallel debate centres on price and performance: one commenter notes the price was cut roughly in half versus preview to $0.70/M input and $2.10/M output ($0.07/M cached), warranting a reassessment, while others say it still loses to DeepSeek and GLM on cost-per-task and intelligence indices, and a 97%-cache-heavy coder calls the cache pricing 10–20x DeepSeek. Practitioners chime in with EU data-residency use (one notes the CLOUD act may defeat that), with US regulated firms barred from Chinese models seeing this as the main self-hostable non-Chinese option, and with quantization math: Unsloth shipped day-zero GGUFs but the smallest quant still needs ~256GB VRAM, and 4-bit NVFP4 weights are ~610GB. Side complaints cover slow thinking mode, weak harness/skills tooling versus Claude, a 10% upcharge for EU regional inference, and a confusing console signup flow.

**Worth reading**
- [maxloh](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50290476): Quotes the license clause requiring a separate commercial license for Model-as-a-Service and argues 「weight-available」 is the honest term, naming Qwen, GLM, Kimi and MiniMax as equally mislabelled.
- [Topfi](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50289701): Flags that pricing was halved from the preview ($1.36/$4.18 to $0.70/$2.10 per M tokens) and says the preview was uncompetitive at the old price, so this release deserves a fresh evaluation.
- [prophesi](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50293101): Notes Unsloth had day-zero access to publish quantizations, but even the lowest quant needs about 256GB VRAM — useful reality check on 「open」 self-hosting.

## 3. H.264 Is Magic (2016) {#story-3}
*#4 on HN · [sidbala.com](https://sidbala.com/h-264-is-magic/) · 136 points · 58 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50291054)*

对 H.264 的一篇常被转发的讲解文章（最初发表于 2016 年，作者 sidbala），讲它如何在四个层面削减数据量：8-bit 4:2:0 之下的量化、离散余弦式的频域掩蔽、色度子采样，以及利用 I 帧／P 帧／B 帧和 16x16 宏块做时域运动补偿。它的核心演示是：苹果主页的一张 PNG 截图重 1015KB，而同一页面的 300 帧、5 秒、60fps 的 H.264 剪辑只有 175KB。文章用信息熵和「为一辆 3000 磅的赛车减重」的类比解释这一切，并强调低通频率掩蔽能在视觉上不明显的情况下把细节压到原图的 2%。

**Discussion**: 大量讨论偏离了文章本身，转向现役编解码器的现状：有人说 AV1 已经「主流」，另一些人指出 Nvidia Shield（最后更新于 2019 年）没有 AV1 解码，而 Apple 硬件直到 2023 年才加入。反驳的一方列举了证据：Roku 约占 43% 的流媒体硬件、Amazon 约占 35%，且智能电视占了大多数观看时长；Tiger Lake 一代的 Intel NUC 以二手价几十美元就能硬解 AV1。另有一条独立线索说 AV1 值得用，甚至不一定需要硬件解码，因为它在 YouTube 上以更小的体积提供了更好的画质。还有个旁支关于图片格式：有人建议换掉 PNG，因为 WebP 和 JPEG XL 的体积都明显更小，但有人反驳说 WebP 解码比 JXL 快得多，且 lossy AVIF 两者都胜过。还有人对文章拿无损 PNG 去和有损视频比划提出异议，辩护者说那只是修辞引子，后面马上就解释了。其余评论讨论 H.264 专利（一条提示 High Profile 的专利几乎全部到期，另一条说「最后一项主要专利」还剩一年）、H.264 对 H.265 的取舍（1080p 选 H.264，4K 选 H.265，需要兼容性就选 H.264），以及有证据表明神经／学习型编解码器是未来方向，但目前速度太慢。

**Worth reading**
- [WhitneyLand](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50291663): 认为文章拿无损 PNG 和有损 H.264 比大小的核心演示是苹果比橘子：「根本说不通」，尽管回复者辩称那只是文章刻意设置的引子（C55），而该对比本身就是衡量有损压缩的合理方式（C54）。
- [ksec](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50292455): 指出 H.264 High Profile 已接近免专利，并链接到维基媒体关于 MPEG-4 AVC 专利到期的页面。
- [Dwedit](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50292601): 给出各图片格式的实际分工：无损 WebP 解码比无损 JXL 快得多，有损 AVIF 则胜过有损 JXL 和 WebP，无损 AVIF 还不如 PNG。

## 4. LLM Voice {#story-4}
*#5 on HN · [martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/LLM-Voice.html) · 92 points · 47 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50290177)*

A Martin Fowler bliki post arguing that readers detect 「LLM Voice」 as a gut-level uncanny-valley reaction — not the em-dashes but 「load-bearing tapestries」 — long before any detection tool could help. His advice: never let an LLM write the draft, since human editing can't scrub the voice out, and treat outlines as the acceptable limit; he discloses his own policy (AI only as a search tool, all facts verified at source) and names exceptions including Jim Highsmith, who relies on LLMs to keep writing despite Parkinson's, plus translation and bureaucratic form letters. He argues writing is how he thinks, so delegating prose means delegating comprehension.

**Discussion**: The thread broadly agrees with the diagnosis but splits on the ethics. The dominant analogy is food: several commenters compare LLM prose to frozen Sysco burritos — fine as private fuel, insulting when served to guests as home cooking, with one noting ~90% of US restaurant food already comes from Sysco/US Foods and diners simply avoid the places that only reheat. Others push back that it isn't either/or and that claims AI makes people incapable of thought are fear-mongering, while a counter-reply argues the real risk is that consuming LLM prose trains people to write in that voice. One poster reports having an HN submission removed from the front page and suspects it was judged on prose rather than technical content; the pointed rebuttal is that LLM-voice signals probable low value, so readers can't be expected to sift for the exceptions, and the original poster accepts the disagreement. A separate subthread argues about whether the tells will vanish — some expect indistinguishability within months or a couple of years, others think the signal is getting worse as models drift toward over-complication, and that humans will shift style to avoid being mistaken for machines.

**Worth reading**
- [martythemaniak](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50293162): Describes a concrete workflow for keeping your own voice: record a voice memo, have the LLM ask clarifying questions and critique drafts, and repeatedly flag where it smooths out your ideas — while arguing impersonal documents can safely be fully AI-written.
- [ctrlt](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50292630): First-hand report that an HN post on a niche technical problem was removed from the front page, which the commenter reads as judging prose rather than engineering insight.
- [dofm](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50293028): Counter-argument to the 「we'll never tell the difference」 pessimism: the tell may get worse rather than better, and humans will change how they write to avoid being confused with AI.

## 5. Zombie stats: Where the numbers AI quotes come from {#story-5}
*#6 on HN · [trakkr.ai](https://trakkr.ai/trakkr-research/zombie-stats) · 86 points · 45 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50290283)*

Trakkr Research Study 013 asked ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude and Google AI Overviews 200 numeric questions through each company's API with web search on, then hand- and model-traced a random sample of 1,285 cited statistics up to five pages deep. Only 40% of trails reached a study that says the same thing, 8% reached the study but with the number or context altered, 40% dead-ended at a source that was paywalled, gone or silent, and 11% led nowhere; 82% of cited pages did contain the figure, but only 21% of those pages were the original research, and more than half were company blogs. ChatGPT scored best at 57% with the fewest numbers per answer (1.3 vs 6.8 for Claude); the other four clustered at 38–46%, and all four statistics quoted by all five assistants had no traceable study. The showcase example is the widely repeated claim that 44% of salespeople give up after one follow-up, which ends at a freelance writer's sample article citing no research.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between people who doubt the study and people who think the finding matches their experience. Several call the post itself slop or a thinly veiled ad for Trakkr, and one mocks the report's punchy sentence fragments as possibly AI-written — though another replies that AI critics who actually use AI will inevitably write with it. On substance, a former Perplexity subscriber says it cited AI blogspam so consistently he cancelled, while others defend per-sentence citation as the right direction and note Perplexity also appears in the study without scoring better. The strongest concrete counter-example is the 「70% of websites use PHP」 figure from php2shell coverage: one commenter traces it to W3Techs' measurement of sites with a known server-side language and offers HTTP Archive's 48.3% as a better baseline, while another argues PHP is harder to fingerprint than assumed. A recurring theme is that the problem is upstream of the models — citogenesis, blogs and roundups — and that users rarely follow citations anyway, with a side argument over whether skepticism toward LLM output is a teachable skill or an attitude that handing kids chatbots erodes.

**Worth reading**
- [cosmojg](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50292498): Checks the viral 70%-of-websites-use-PHP claim against HTTP Archive's September 2026 crawl (48.3%) and traces the higher figure to W3Techs counting only sites with a known server-side language.
- [bjourne](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50292368): Gives concrete examples of Google's AI explaining invented Swedish compound words like 「nagelfis」 and 「tangentbordskanin」 as if they were real terms.
- [rozab](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50292078): Documents how one AI-written php2shell post's offhand statistic spread to every major tech outlet until no original source could be found, illustrating citogenesis.

## 7. Grace Blackwell needs a new scheduler: 27% more usable GPUs from the same racks {#story-7}
*#8 on HN · [skypilot.ai](https://skypilot.ai/blog/grace-blackwell-needs-a-new-scheduler) · 60 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50290912)*

SkyPilot 的一篇博客文章主张，NVL72 机架是一个单一 NVLink 域（每块 GPU 1.8 TB/s，每机架聚合带宽 130 TB/s），因此负载放置方式的影响非常大：Hugging Face 测得，把张量并行从 NVLink 移到 InfiniBand 上会损失 43% 的训练吞吐量，而使用专家并行时，未优化的 all-to-all 通信最多可占用 60% 的训练时间。文章认为把整个机架作为分配单元是个陷阱，并引用 Meta 给出的 4-GPU 节点平均 490 天中断间隔，推算出整机架大约每 27 天就会发生一次中断；按照 SkyPilot 在生产 NVL72 集群上实测的约 2% 不可用率计算，在一个 2,304 节点的集群里，16 节点切片能提供 2,037 个可用节点，而 18 节点切片只有 1,602 个，前者比后者多 27%。文章还报告，NVIDIA 的 GR00T 团队在 512 块 GB300 GPU 上运行 GR00T N1.7 时，采用感知机架拓扑的放置方式将每次迭代从 1.59 秒缩短到 1.39 秒，吞吐量提升 14.4%，这相当于在 10,000-GPU 集群上每小时节省约 1.67 万美元。文章在介绍碎片整理之前就被截断了。

**Discussion**: 评论者总体上认为这篇文章解释了为什么需要感知机架的 gang scheduling，却没有解释调度器实际上是怎么做的。jhgg 说，在公司内部，他们为 NVL72 工作负载构建了类似的自定义拓扑感知调度器，并认为真正有意思的工程细节，比如扩展到数百万块 GPU、把调度周期缩短到毫秒级，恰恰是文章省略的内容，因此这只是“营销文章”。ismailmaj 表示这篇文章感觉像是 AI 生成的：文章花了很多篇幅讲基础知识，对 SkyPilot 自己的调度器却只有两段，还含糊其辞；一位评论者则询问有没有其他博客值得一读。一位可能属于 SkyPilot 一方的评论者（zhwu）为这篇文章辩护说，这篇文章的目的是让读者了解 NVL72 生产环境中的故障问题；他还给出了简要版本：任务被拆成 16 节点切片，每个切片以原子方式放在同一个机架上（机架内每个切片预留两个备用节点，用于热替换）；此外，随后还会有更多文章介绍碎片整理和热替换。novalite 则强烈反对：感知机架的 gang scheduling 自 HTCondor 时代就已存在，几乎每个大型 GPU 部署都在使用，NVL72 并没有带来“新”调度器；这篇文章新增的信息量接近零，像是在替 SkyPilot 做推销。

The linked item is a Stack Exchange Data Explorer query with an attached graph counting new Stack Overflow questions (PostTypeId = 1) per month; the site itself returned HTTP 403, so the numbers here come from the headline and from commenters reading that graph. Commenters put the peak at roughly 207,000 questions/month in March 2014 and about 109,000/month by November 2022, when ChatGPT launched — meaning the collapse to under 1,000/month is the tail of a decline that was already years old. **Discussion**: The dominant view is that moderation hostility, not AI, set the decline in motion: answerers describe spending an hour on a question only to have it closed as a duplicate or rewritten by a moderator, and reviewers point out the old close-vote queue paid a badge point for closing but nothing for leaving a question open. ## 18. Qwenputer: A functioning, deterministic RISC-V CPU inside Qwen3.5 {#story-18}
*#22 on HN · [divagr.com](https://divagr.com/blog/qwenputer) · 666 points · 139 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50282838)*

A blog post by divagr18 describing Qwenputer: a stock Qwen 3.5 4B with a 32-bit RISC-V core (RV32IM plus Zba and a small bignum unit) compiled into 11 extra layers in front of Qwen's 32, running entirely inside the model's forward passes. The trick is turning Gated DeltaNet attention heads into exact RAM by forcing the gate to 1, write strength beta to 1, and using one-hot keys, so each write overwrites exactly one row and each query retrieves an exact value. The author claims it passes the official riscv-tests, matches qemu, and ran 18 million instructions free-running with zero errors; on DeepMind arithmetic sets it scores 97.5-100% (base Qwen gets 55-90%), plus an experimental filesystem of 512 files / ~53k tokens in the DeltaNet state that lifts changed-fact recall from 30% to 90%. Costs: an 18.8 GB checkpoint (mostly zero padding), a custom RvChat driver that boots the firmware and keeps trace tokens out of Qwen's layers, and roughly seconds per large calculation (~3,200 CPU tokens/sec on an L4, single 24 GB GPU).

**Discussion**: Most of the thread is delighted disbelief — DOOM jokes, nested-Qwen-inside-Qwen jokes, jokes about turning LLMs back into RAM — but the sharpest pushback is about framing: one commenter argues the 「no external runtime, not a plugin」 claim is misleading, since a host driver still loads the firmware, boots the CPU to its start state and manually skips Qwen's own layers, and another asks outright whether tool calls are happening or not. A second argument is over determinism: one side holds the demo up as a strong counter-example to the claim that LLMs are inherently non-deterministic, while others reply that the CPU is hardcoded weights bolted onto the architecture, so sampling and production non-determinism are untouched. Someone asks how a calculator that scores below 100% on its own benchmarks is useful, and another reports the inverse experiment — prompting frontier models to emulate RISC-V directly — failing within a handful of instructions while the models kept trying to shortcut to the final answer. Several people ask what it is actually for; the sympathetic answer is that a deterministic compute unit plus persistent memory inside the model is a cheap win for arithmetic and cross-chat facts, and the author turns up in the thread to say it is a free-time project and he is open to work.

**Worth reading**
- [gcr](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50286992): Argues the 「no external runtime」 claim is somewhat dishonest: a custom host runtime still loads firmware, boots the CPU and skips Qwen's layers, which are invasive changes to the inference driver.
- [gspr](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50285274): First-hand account of the opposite experiment: asking Qwen and all frontier models to *be* a RISC-V CPU failed within a few instructions every time, often at instruction decode, and the models persistently cheated by computing the final answer instead of stepping.
- [gavinray](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50287557): Notes that several benchmarks show sub-100% accuracy and asks how a calculator that is not always exact is actually useful.

## 19. We won't "just hang out" {#story-19}
*#23 on HN · [borretti.me](https://borretti.me/article/we-wont-just-hang-out) · 79 points · 130 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50285154)*

A blog post by Fernando Borretti arguing that the standard answer to “what will people do after the Singularity?” — the Culture-style infinite vacation — cannot work. His claim is that hanging out depends on social processes that post-scarcity AI destroys: work, university, cities, and communities of practice. He cites his own path — friends made via IRC while installing Linux and via open-source projects like the Austral language — and says that with Claude Code and LLMs, nobody would have cared about his projects or blog posts, so the next generation has no on-ramp; he also invokes Gregory Conti’s argument that endless leisure produces atrophied, uninteresting people, and notes that pre-AI friendships are a non-renewable resource subject to attrition.

**Discussion**: The dominant response is that the piece describes the author’s unusually narrow social world rather than the future: commenters list care work and ministry, disaster and mutual-aid communities, salsa and bachata, homebrewing, volleyball, running clubs, church bands, craft hobbies and raising children as relationship engines that have nothing to do with employment or credentials. A recurring counter-argument is that humans already value human achievement after machines surpass it — cars beat Olympic sprinters a century ago, chess engines beat Magnus Carlsen, yet both still draw audiences — so learning and mastery would survive on intrinsic grounds. Defenders of the post concede the specific mechanisms really are dissolving (colleagues, classmates, asking strangers on IRC, open-source reputation as a social filter) and point to rising loneliness statistics among young people as evidence the trend is already underway. A separate thread argues status competition and sexual selection guarantee people will still strive, with some predicting it degenerates into violence or AI-designed status games, and others noting that “third places” are vanishing for fiscal reasons — a claim European commenters contested as US-centric (a German commenter said austerity is producing the same effect there). Several readers dismissed the whole thing as doom-mongering or rage-bait, and a moderator intervened to ask commenters to stop sneering at the author.

**Worth reading**
- [tempestn](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50285833): Argues the premise is empirically wrong: we still watch Olympic sprinters despite cars, still know Magnus Carlsen despite chess engines, and mostly don’t care about AI art precisely because no human mind is behind it — so skills and learning would retain value.
- [hexjy](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50285675): A practitioner agrees the open-source on-ramp is the real casualty — many of his lasting relationships started as bug reports on small projects — but says people still gather around climbing gyms, modding servers and church bands, with the open question being whether anyone shows up once staying home is easier.
- [Nursie](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50286257): A detailed rebuttal: every example of connection in the post is technological or career-related, mate-signalling will simply re-route through other channels, and old ladies will still crochet together — the essay says more about the author’s life than about humanity’s prospects.

## 20. Java's new AOT cache feature is in a rough state {#story-20}
*#24 on HN · [xpipe.io](https://xpipe.io/blog/java-aot-cache) · 71 points · 23 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50242509)*

XPipe developer Christopher Schnick walks through the JDK's AOT cache — introduced by JEP 483 in JDK 24, streamlined by JEP 514/515 in JDK 25, extended by JEP 516 in JDK 26 — which records a training run's class loading, linking, method profiles and some cached objects so later JVM starts skip that work. His main finding is that AOT class linking silently enables adapter caching (JDK-8350209), which bakes machine code tuned to the build machine's CPU features into the cache and crashes the JVM with SIGILL on machines with fewer features (e.g. training on a 13th-gen Intel, running on 12th-gen). The escape hatch is the undocumented -XX:+UnlockDiagnosticVMOptions -XX:-AOTAdapterCaching on the cache-generation run; JDK 27 will store the training CPU features and require an exact match, which he argues makes the upcoming JEP 544 code cache nearly useless for software distributed to users without a compatibility switch.

**Discussion**: The thread's biggest correction came from the author himself, who told a commenter quoting JEP 544 that the post is about the older, already-shipped AOT cache (JEP 483), not the JDK 28 code cache. Several readers argued the JVM should detect a CPU feature mismatch at startup and warn or fall back to the JIT instead of segfaulting, with one noting that expectations like 「this is what I want AOT cache to mean」 miss how the feature is being staged. An OpenJDK contributor defended that staging: the cache already earns its keep in production where developers control deployment, machine-code caching lands in JDK 28, and external distribution comes later — a tradeoff another commenter said beats the ossification Java suffered previously. Others complained about ergonomics and what one called the Java community's hostile, inward-looking response to such criticism, countered by people pointing at Python and JavaScript packaging; suggestions included doing the training run at install time per user CPU, and mentions of more mature alternatives (OpenJ9, Azul, CRaC, Android's use since Android 7). A side thread found GraalVM Native Image still awkward for ordinary cases such as java.awt thumbnail generation, while the author noted the AOT cache uniquely allows adding modules and jars to the classpath at runtime, unlike Native Image's closed world.

**Worth reading**
- [crschnick](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50290250): The author corrects a misreading: JEP 544 is the separate, unreleased JDK 28 AOT code cache; the post is about the JDK 24 AOT cache under JEP 483.
- [pron](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50290362): An OpenJDK-side commenter explains the deliberate piecemeal rollout — the cache is already valuable for internal deployments, with machine-code caching in JDK 28 and better external-deployment support after that.
- [crschnick](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50292862): The author details a practical advantage over GraalVM: with the AOT cache you can still load jars and modules at runtime, as long as the boot module path matches the training run.
