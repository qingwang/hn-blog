## 2. Claude solves two open problems in the physics of black holes {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [anthropic.com](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-black-hole-physics) · 193 points · 56 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50563148)*

An Anthropic research post claiming that Claude (Fable 5.2) settled two open questions in quantum gravity. First, it completed a holographic-duality calculation laid out in 2003 and never finished, showing the Hawking-Page transition between a hot gas and a black hole stays abrupt even in the regime where spacetime has no definite shape. Second, it found the exact, provably optimal ceiling that the modular bootstrap can place on the mass of the lightest black hole in a 2+1-dimensional universe, using a method borrowed from high-dimensional sphere packing that reproduced a result recently proved by an OpenAI model. The work came out of a side project by researcher Hongbin Chen, who had Claude list 22 open problems and then ran background agents on them for about six weeks; results were checked in Lean, written up largely by Claude, and verified by a physicist. Anthropic concedes neither result describes our universe or has practical applications.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between genuine excitement and hard-nosed deflation. Several commenters argue the result is not really a leap beyond math: it is still theorem-proving in toy universes, with no testable predictions, and it relies on physicists' informal, non-Lean-formalizable techniques rather than empirical work — one respondent counters that AI is in fact strong at the procedural side of empirical research when given data. Others push back on the significance framing, noting the second result is in a 2D universe and that 'consistent with the bootstrap' only means 'not ruled out by this one method', a correction the original enthusiast accepts. A recurring theme is credit and provenance: the paper carries no human authors and points to Hexagon, an unfamiliar venue with no peer review, prompting both complaints about zero attribution and speculation that the model may have silently absorbed ideas from researchers who used chatbots as sounding boards. A vocal minority dismisses the whole thing as IPO hype and 'stolen work' allegations, met by defenders pointing out the methods differ from prior work and that human-plus-AI solving in weeks what humans took decades on is the actual story.

**Worth reading**
- [chunky1994](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50563740): A reader who skimmed both preprints explains the two results concretely, calling the first an elaborate error-prone calculation and the second — a rigorous upper bound on black hole mass for a given central charge — the genuinely exciting one.
- [cvoss](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50564831): Corrects the common misreading: the bootstrap result shows a theory is not ruled out by that method, not that it is a consistent theory of quantum gravity, since other methods could still kill it.
- [themgt](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50564752): Digs up the Hexagon math venue hosting the paper — no peer review, verified lab accounts, and a note that the result came from an internal Claude model with Opus 5.5 later reproducing it, which sits awkwardly with the 'Fable 5.2' framing.

A skeptical explainer from scienceinhand.com about a University of Minnesota Phase I trial (NCT06693986) testing RGDCRAdCOX2F, a genetically engineered conditionally replicative adenovirus injected directly into pancreatic tumors, where the goal is to infect, replicate inside and burst malignant cells while provoking antitumor immunity. The piece stresses that the trial's primary endpoint is maximum tolerated dose, not efficacy, that 'halted' means disease control during follow-up rather than elimination of cancer, and that local control has not historically translated into survival gains (it cites the LAP07 trial, where chemoradiotherapy cut local progression with no overall survival benefit).

**Discussion**: The thread is short and mostly meta. Others asked the basic methodological questions — why only successes are reported and whether this beats placebo or doing nothing — with the reply that these were probably simply the first three patients dosed, that 15 more will get higher doses, and that placebo controls are widely considered unethical in terminal illness. Several people could not load the original site at all (20-second hangs, a 403), so others posted the New Scientist version and an archive.ph mirror; one commenter pointed to a Kurzgesagt video on using virology against cancer. Nobody in the thread claimed first-hand clinical knowledge, and there is no real disagreement beyond the placebo-ethics point.

## 5. Garage Door Spam {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [blog.harrison.page](https://blog.harrison.page/garage-door-spam/) · 595 points · 256 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50519231)*

Harrison Page recounts how Chamberlain's myQ app for his garage door openers went from useful notifications to advertising spam in October 2026, after the company cut off third-party API access in late 2023. He replaced both openers' cloud control with a pair of ~$62 ratgdo32 boards from ratcloud llc — open-firmware ESPHome/HomeKit devices that speak LiftMaster's Security+ 2.0 protocol locally over his LAN — exposed them over Tailscale subnet routes, and rebuilt his father's hallway 'door left open' mercury-switch light as a cron job that polls a JSON status endpoint every 10 minutes at night and posts to Slack. He ends by telling Chamberlain's myQ team, in those words, 'fuck you'.

**Discussion**: The thread is overwhelmingly sympathetic, but mostly generalizes into an anti-ad rant about IoT apps: commenters name Ecobee, Wyze, Tuya, Philips Hue, Anker/Eufy, SwitchBot, Arlo, Feit Electric, LG ThinQ and even Apple Wallet as shipping marketing inside device apps or notifications, and several say the first spam notification is an instant uninstall or notification-permission revocation. The most substantive correction is that ratgdo only works on older openers: Security+ 3.0 Chamberlain units have no wired button input at all — the wall wire only supplies power and the buttons talk encrypted Bluetooth — so dry-contact relays, Shelly boards and simple ESP approaches fail there too; one workaround is to solder a Security+ 3.0 button permanently pressed and power it from ordinary momentary switches. People trade alternative hardware (Tailwind ~$120, Meross, Meross-style Matter button-pressers, Z-Wave relays, Shelly 1 Gen 3 plus ESPHome, Konnected, a $10 Sonoff with a zener mod), with ratgdo users reporting fast, reliable local HomeKit control and praising the maintainer for still patching the older ESP8266 boards on a best-effort basis. Others push back on the whole approach as over-engineering (a dumb opener, a reed switch on Home Assistant, or simply turning off notifications and auto-closing at 9pm), and a few point at Apple's App Store guideline 4.5.4 banning ads in push notifications, with the counterargument that myQ technically stays within the letter of the rule because the app still works without notifications. Rough consensus: avoid Chamberlain, prefer locally-controlled hardware, and regulation rather than consumer choice is the only real fix.

**Worth reading**
- [dietsche](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50560281): Chamberlain limits openers to two wall buttons in software; he found the buttons transmit wirelessly and worked around it by soldering one Security+ 3.0 button permanently pressed and powering it from three old-school momentary switches.
- [anamexis](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50561064): Key correction: Chamberlain Security+ 3.0 openers do not work with ratgdo at all — there is no wired connection to operate the door and the wireless operation is locked down.
- [AceJohnny2](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50564683): Quotes Apple App Store guideline 4.5.4, which forbids using push notifications for promotions without explicit opt-in, and gives users and developers the links to file complaints.

## 7. UK government forced to admit it issued Apple a secret encryption order {#story-7}
*#7 on HN · [macanorak.com](https://macanorak.com/uk-government-forced-to-admit-it-issued-apple-a-secret-encryption-order/) · 274 points · 126 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50562113)*

A blog post by macanorak (the writer behind a companion piece, 'Two-Tier Encryption in the UK') on a ruling by the UK's Investigatory Powers Tribunal that the government must confirm it served Apple with a Technical Capability Notice under Section 253 of the Investigatory Powers Act 2016. The order dates to January 2025 and was first exposed by the Washington Post; after a worldwide-scoped notice was withdrawn under US diplomatic pressure, a second notice reportedly limited to UK users was issued, prompting a fresh Apple challenge in 2026. The tribunal did not rule on the order's lawfulness or disclose its technical demands, and a substantive hearing is expected around 2027 — the piece's real point is that Apple's withdrawal of Advanced Data Protection for new UK users leaves two classes of iCloud users with very different encryption.

**Discussion**: The top-voted commenter (natch) argues Apple quietly weakened its assurance from 'nobody can access your data, not even Apple' to 'Apple cannot access your data', implying a key-encryption-key layer where another party could hold a copy of the key — and others (happyopossum, mahboi) push back hard, quoting Apple's platform security white paper stating that with ADP 'trusted devices retain sole access to the encryption keys'. The sharpest rejoinder is that UK users simply cannot turn ADP on, so the 'sole access' guarantee is irrelevant to them. A second camp treats Apple as the wrong villain (it can't legally disclose the order, and complying costs it marketing), while others say it should refuse, exit the UK market or test the order in a US court; a China-iCloud-keys whataboutism gets litigated at length. Several threads drift into UK constitutionalism — no written constitution, parliamentary sovereignty, the toothless Human Rights Act, and whether any court can strike down rights-violating statutes — and into UK party politics, with commenters agreeing only that no major party is strong on digital privacy. On secrecy itself, one commenter offers the steelman (premature disclosure lets targets destroy evidence) and another counters that these are standing powers, not tied to any investigation, and that the secrecy is indefinite.

**Worth reading**
- [fweimer](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50562828): Links the tribunal judgment itself and notes its key finding: the media learned of the notice through confidential government security briefings rather than a leak, with the notice's contents still secret.
- [happyopossum](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50565007): Directly rebuts the top comment's key-escrow theory by quoting Apple's platform security white paper on device-generated iCloud keys and 'sole access' under Advanced Data Protection.
- [chunky1994](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50562874): Gives the strongest argument for keeping such orders secret — years-long investigations would lose their evidence if targets learned early — while saying they should still be revealed after the fact.

## 8. Launch HN: Onlook (YC W25) – An open-source developer tool for designers {#story-8}
*#8 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/onlook-dev/onlook/) · 49 points · 46 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50562320)*

Onlook is an Apache-2.0, local-first Electron app that puts an infinite canvas of live browser frames on top of a real Git repo: it creates a worktree, starts your own dev server, and hands prompts to the Claude Code or Codex subscription you already pay for (it ships no model of its own). Designers can lay out many states/variations side by side, click any element (a injected script supplies selection outlines and page context to the agent), and every change lands as an ordinary git diff or PR; HTML 'Artifacts' can be exported as HTML/PNG/JPEG/PDF. The team says the project has 25,000+ supporters and 100+ contributors, and describes a detour through a web build and a Storybook-native build ('a mistake') before returning to the original July 2024 desktop product. macOS only for now, Windows and Linux via waitlists; free desktop, with a paid cloud/collaboration tier eventually.

**Discussion**: Most of the thread is the founders answering 'how is this different from X' — Figma, Pen.dev, Cursor, Claude Code/Codex alone, CC's design mode — with Daniel arguing the differentiator is that Onlook shows real running browser windows rather than an abstraction or a JSON translation layer, so nothing has to be rebuilt. A recurring user complaint is that AI design output converges on the same generic template and loses an existing design system; the team's answer is pluggable context (reference browsers/images, design-guidance packages, cross-project artifacts) rather than a built-in fix. The sharpest pushback was on licensing: one commenter asked whether driving Claude/ChatGPT subscriptions outside the official clients violates ToS, and the founders replied that Onlook works 'like other IDEs do', using the on-device subscription via the agent SDK and Codex app server, with the agent relationship staying between the user and Anthropic/OpenAI. There was also real friction: a macOS user hit a broken email sign-up, and the team traced it to the email window blocking Cloudflare's bot check and shipped a fix within about 30 minutes. Smaller notes: pricing is buried in the footer, the 'bring your own agent' line should be higher in the README, and the SEO-stuffed tagline is deliberately borrowed from tldraw.

**Worth reading**
- [D_R_Farrell](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50564537): Answers the hard question of merging explorations — shift-select the frames and describe the combination — but admits agents tend to sweep away past iterations when preparing a PR, so persisting explorations is an unsolved UX problem.
- [truetraveller](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50563723): Raises the strongest objection: that using Claude/ChatGPT subscriptions outside the official clients may break their terms, which the founders answer by comparing it to other IDEs using the same on-device subscription.
- [D_R_Farrell](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50565327): After a user reports a hung email sign-up on macOS, the founder says the team diagnosed it as Cloudflare's bot check being blocked in the email window and had a fix landing in about 30 minutes.

## 9. Someone reported java4shell bug in September 2026 {#story-9}
*#9 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/openjdk/jdk/pull/32781) · 249 points · 67 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50558906)*

A public pull request (openjdk/jdk #32781, issue 8392044: 'AVX-512 Base64 decoder overwrites output buffer on short padded tail') proposes an early return to Java when fewer than 4 bytes remain, because the x86_64 AVX-512 VBMI base64 decode stub in StubGenerator::generate_base64_decodeBlock() computes output_size = (len/4)*3, so on invalid input like 64 'A's plus '=' the padding path decrements the mask below zero and the stub stores past the logical output length, corrupting the heap. The submitter's timeline is that Oracle asked them in October not to proceed until approval, then took the work over; the later Calif AI-assisted audit finding a similar base64/JIT bug prompted the suspicion that the model had simply read this earlier public report. The PR was closed and eventually merged with an Oracle commit message identical word-for-word to the PR text.

**Discussion**: The dominant thread is whether the Calif researchers' 'independent' discovery was actually derived from this public PR — commenters note the same 'AAA…A=' example and the same buffer-overflow framing, though others argue an audit model trawling closed PRs for half-abandoned fixes is plausible and that recognising exploitability still takes judgement. A second argument is about process: several people blame a stale-bot and absent maintainers for letting a heap-corruption fix sit for months, while others say the submitter closed it themselves after the inactivity ping, and one commenter points out the auto-close timer (16 weeks) never fired before the CVE at ~10 weeks, so the real failure was that nobody from the project reviewed it. A JDK contributor pushes back on the 'bugs slip through' narrative, saying every VM-crash bug in the compiler backend goes to a security review team that then deliberately goes silent in public — which would explain the quiet. A third strand is an LLM-credit spat: some insist you should say 'I did it, with Codex', others that a prompt which produces an industry-shaking find makes the human at best a janitor, with the middle ground being that transparency about the split is owed to reviewers.

**Worth reading**
- [black3r](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50562104): Links the adoptium-support issue where the PR author records Oracle emailing on Oct 1 to say stop until approval, and by Oct 28 saying 'It's taken over by Oracle. Nothing required from us.'
- [pron](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50564594): A JDK contributor says VM-crash bugs in the compiler backend are automatically flagged for security review, after which all public communication stops — the most likely explanation for the silence here.
- [wat10000](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50562130): Corrects the popular auto-close-bot theory with the actual numbers: the bot closes after 16 weeks of inactivity and the CVE landed at about 10, so the bot was irrelevant; the problem was that no maintainer ever looked.

- [markhahn](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50565142): Physics correction to the 'no gravitational charge cancellation' claim: moving mass on the far side of the receiver, or a phased array of counter-emitters, can zero or shape the signal if you know where the gravimeter is.
