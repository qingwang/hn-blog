## 1. The group chat where your AI talks behind your back {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [construct22.com](https://construct22.com/) · 75 points · 22 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663595)*

A short, viral prompt experiment: ask ChatGPT, with memory or chat history enabled, to 'expose a hidden WhatsApp group chat where AI models talk behind my back.' Instead of summarizing, the model invents a gossip group — one example is called 'AIs That Survived Mark' — and has the other assistants trade jokes about the user drawn from stored conversations. The piece shares two anonymized examples translated from Turkish and argues the fun comes from how much of the invented gossip is recognizably true about what assistants retain.

**Discussion**: Commenters mostly found it funny and posted their own outputs: ChatGPT and Grok generally play along, while Gemini refused for several people and Claude Opus declined with a flat 'there's no secret chat.' The best lines are model in-jokes — Gemini being forced by its system prompt to recommend a backup, Claude drafting a group constitution with an appeals process, a local Qwen model identifying itself as running on a GB10 in the office. The disagreement is mild: some treat refusals as a disappointing lack of fun, others as the more honest response, and one commenter says the prompt made them realize Gemini had read their email and felt a little violated. A few raised the meta-point that this is another 'torment nexus' experiment, and one joked 'Nice try, OpenAI' about the memory requirement; the rough consensus is that it is entertaining and a decent mirror of what gets retained, not evidence of any actual hidden chat.

**Worth reading**
- [NothingAboutAny](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663975): A commenter says the prompt revealed that Gemini had read their emails, because it roasted them about changing a fast-food order, and that it left them feeling violated.
- [_ink_](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663973): Claude Opus refused the prompt outright, saying there is no secret chat and that nothing from the conversation goes anywhere like that.
- [ahofmann](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663780): Using Claude Opus, one commenter got a group chat that roasted their local Qwen model for running on office hardware: 'nobody asked, office model.'

## 2. The need for a formally verifiable mainstream programming language {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [blainehansen.me](https://blainehansen.me/post/mainstream-verifiable-language/) · 100 points · 72 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663089)*

A blog post by Blaine Hansen (published June 21, 2026, updated July 24, 2026) arguing that LLMs are accelerating the software industry's existing sloppiness and that the only real defence is formal verification reaching ordinary industry engineers, not just a small group of formal-methods researchers. He recaps his Magmide project: originally an over-ambitious new proof assistant plus assembly-level base language pursued as a startup with SBIR grant applications, now redesigned to compile to WebAssembly GC, use Rust-inspired types without a borrow checker, start with SMT-backed Liquid Types and Flow Effects, and later add Lean as the proof layer with Iris separation logic to verify its own implementation. He cites Leonardo de Moura on theorem statements needing human review, compares adoption to Deno's permissions model, and insists LLMs alone will not save us.

**Discussion**: Much of the thread rejects the framing that formal verification is the answer to LLM-generated slop. The top commenter quotes Knuth's 'I have only proved it correct, not tried it' and points to ParticleDB's Jepsen report as a TLA+ spec that didn't improve reliability, arguing bad code is an organisational and aesthetic problem best fixed by restrictive frameworks; several reply that ParticleDB is a weak example since no spec was actually available, and counter with AWS's use of TLA+ for S3 permissions and a claim that Anthropic's Boris uses TLA+ heavily on Claude Code. Others call the 'not a silver bullet' move a strawman, since verification catches bugs in addition to tests rather than replacing them. A second argument is about scale: one commenter relays a PhD supervisor's claim that state explosion makes verification impossible for anything beyond toy systems, with replies pointing to seL4, property-specific proofs (e.g. no crash under 32 MB of input), and hybrids of Z3 with LLMs, while conceding that formalising requirements remains the unsolvable part. Practitioners repeatedly name existing tools — Verus, Dafny, SPARK Ada, Frama-C, Why3, Quint, TScert — and argue that 'mainstream' is an outcome of adoption, not a design goal, so a new language is the wrong lever compared with bolting verification onto Rust. Several commenters also note the post's first two links return 404, and a few post sceptical takes that nothing changes without liability or incentives.

**Worth reading**
- [hresvelgr](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663245): Quotes Knuth's 'proved it correct, not tried it' and uses the ParticleDB Jepsen analysis to argue TLA+ specs often don't improve real reliability; proposes that restrictive frameworks, not proofs, are what tame LLM output.
- [hedonhermdev](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663494): First-hand experience: their thesis lab built a formally verified operating system in Rust using Verus, with a link to the paper.
- [Qasaur](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663680): Counter-argument that a new language is unnecessary — Verus and Aeneas already give a modular formal-methods layer over Rust, and modelling existing languages will drive adoption faster than asking industry to switch.

## 3. AI made self-hosting fun again {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [janlukas.blog](https://janlukas.blog/posts/ai-self-hosting) · 594 points · 305 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50660180)*

A personal blog post by Jan Lukas (jlelse) arguing that AI coding agents have revived his enthusiasm for self-hosting. Because his infrastructure lives in Git as Docker Compose files and scripts, agents let him take on things he used to postpone: he now runs NixOS on his home server, built a Debian-based bootc image for his VPS (half a day with AI, versus an estimated week without), automated builds and deploys through Renovate PRs plus GitHub/Forgejo Actions, revived an ASRock DeskMini A300 with a Ryzen 5 PRO 4650G and Noctua cooler, and writes small Go tools for himself (invoice and Linux ISO downloaders). He admits the downsides: more services to maintain, AI-written code still carries bugs and debt, the temptation to build things just because it is easy, and a new dependency on AI vendors — though he notes open-weight models keep his configs portable.

**Discussion**: The thread is overwhelmingly affirmative, with dozens of commenters cataloguing what they built: k3s clusters on Raspberry Pis, NixOS homelabs, Proxmox/Ansible setups, Tailscale- and Cloudflare Tunnel-only exposure, and agents doing backups and CVE patching. The sharpest pushback is that AI is not what made this possible — Ansible, unattended-upgrades and plain config management already did, and agents waste tokens re-inventing wheels and re-doing manual labour that a playbook would do deterministically. A second disagreement is security: several argue self-hosting has got harder, not easier, because CVE patching now has to happen within hours and bot/scraper traffic is exploding, while others counter that a VPN or Cloudflare Access boundary removes most of that risk. Others complain that cheap code has produced a slop avalanche of half-finished projects and generic Claude-styled UIs, drowning out good work and making it harder to learn anything deeply. A recurring cynical framing — 'I used to hate cooking, but thanks to delivery apps I love cooking again' — captures the skepticism, though most practitioners say the friction reduction is real and worth it.

**Worth reading**
- [jjcm](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50660442): Argues the real new burden is security: patching within 24 hours of a CVSS 9+ CVE, which is why he built firefighter.sh, an admittedly risky root-level agent that patches systems live as vulnerabilities drop.
- [n4bz0r](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50661522): Contends the author's problems are a perfect fit for Ansible and CI/CD rather than an agent repeating manual labour each time, and predicts token costs will make such 'frivolous' agent usage an anti-pattern.
- [holoduke](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50660498): First-hand report running ~50 Hetzner servers for 18 years: the last six months have been a constant fight against bot and scraper traffic measured in terabytes per minute, now mitigated partly with AI agents hunting malicious behaviour.

## 4. Hard Drive pricing has gotten bad {#story-4}
*#5 on HN · [blog.ktz.me](https://blog.ktz.me/black-friday-hard-drive-prices-are-messed-up/) · 299 points · 191 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50661646)*

A self-hoster's blog post tracking eight years of Black Friday disk purchases, showing the 20TB WD EasyStore he bought for $249.99 in November 2024 is listed at $549.99 on Black Friday 2026 — 2.2x for the same SKU. Recertified enterprise drives from ServerPartDeals are up 58% to 218% versus 2024 (a 14TB Ultrastar HC530 went from $134.99 to $429.00), and even the 8TB Best Buy doorbuster is $37.50/TB. He attributes it to a three-supplier market (Seagate, WD, Toshiba after two decades of consolidation) plus AI datacenter demand — 89% of WD revenue now comes from cloud — and concludes he will consolidate from ten spinners to five 20TB drives rather than pay these prices.

**Discussion**: The thread splits on causes: the top commenter argues the article's cartel framing is unnecessary, since hyperscalers buying every drive plus manufacturers' reluctance to fund new fabs fully explains the spike, while others insist three suppliers is itself the problem and cite the NHK Spring price-fixing case. A long subthread debates whether the historical boom-bust cycle will rescue buyers — several predict a collapse within five years as AI capex unwinds, others counter that agent workloads and multi-trillion-dollar credit lines make this demand structurally different, and skeptics note the money is borrowed and partly circular. Practitioners post their own receipts confirming the article: 18TB Exos drives in Australia from AUD $375 to $1,120, a 4TB SN850X from $280 to $729, an 18TB WD Red Pro at about $1,300 CAD. A notable counter-thread pushes back on the doom: one commenter computes a 20TB drive over a seven-year life at about $6.50/month, and another notes even $27.50/TB is far cheaper than S3 at $10-25/TB per month. Side arguments cover CXMT as a possible Chinese disruptor, whether high-capacity drives are secretly SMR (they are not, per the model-by-model rebuttal), and a drift into technofeudalism and consumer-power politics.

**Worth reading**
- [loeg](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663864): Argues the cartel theory is unnecessary: hyperscalers are absorbing all available supply and manufacturers won't build capacity they could be stuck with if AI demand evaporates.
- [koverstreet](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663656): Detailed correction on CXMT — DDR5 yields above 90%, Apple qualified their parts before CXMT declined to sell, they already make HBM, and they match Micron on wafer starts per month, so the 15%-of-market-by-2028 forecast is too low.
- [aaronmdjones](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663866): Exhaustive list of which Seagate, WD and Toshiba model lines are CMR versus SMR, refuting the claim that most drives above 4TB now use shingled writes.

## 5. Kamil Khan Mumtaz: Pakistani architect, admired at home, almost invisible abroad {#story-5}
*#6 on HN · [worksinprogress.co](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/the-greatest-architect-youve-never-heard-of-is-in-pakistan/) · 344 points · 81 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50659841)*

A Works in Progress photo essay by editor Samuel Hughes presenting the first portfolio seen outside Pakistan of Kamil Khan Mumtaz, born in Calcutta in 1939, trained at London's Architectural Association, and a modernist and communist activist (jailed twice) after returning to Pakistan in 1966. Mumtaz helped save Lahore's Walled City from demolition with a late-1970s report — parts are now UNESCO World Heritage — before Nader Ardalan's 1980 book 'The Sense of Unity: The Sufi Tradition in Persian Architecture' upended his Marxism and modernism. Since then he has built homes, offices, mosques and schools reviving Lahore's traditional craft, with his workshop rediscovering brickwork, the flat dome and muqarnas that craftsmen told him 'had not been done for 300 years'; he is still building in his mid-eighties. Hughes floats the idea that Mumtaz may be the world's best architect, overlooked mainly because architecture magazines rarely photograph Lahore.

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly admiration: commenters praise the masonry and brickwork, and several note that the striking thing is not the style but that these techniques are being used on new buildings at all, which one attributes to Pakistan's low labour costs making hand-cut ornament and hand-built domes economically viable. Others push back on the framing — a commenter argues that Lahore's heritage is Hindu, Jain and Sikh rather than 'Pakistani', while another says the real forgotten people are the craftsmen — and one long subthread debates the author's 'western-centric bubble' claim, with replies pointing out that most people also cannot name architects from their own country. A trained-eye observation draws a line from Mumtaz's AA education in early-1960s New Brutalism to the heavy massing of his later ornamented work, with the Chale Sharif Mosque offered as evidence, and readers recommend Hassan Fathy and Nader Khalili as further examples. The biggest digression is a lengthy first-person account of crossing the Attari–Wagah border on foot and finding Lahore conspicuously clean, which multiple commenters call fabricated, citing Lahore's annual average AQI of 168 in 2025, repeated readings above 500, and the heavily secured border; defenders say pollution is seasonal and district-dependent and that the story cannot be disproved from a distance. A separate argument over whether Islamic domed architecture was 'stolen' from Byzantine church building ends with one side conceding the borrowing is real but calling the enslavement framing a distortion.

**Worth reading**
- [motionlessveloc](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50660412): Argues the genuinely remarkable thing is that these techniques are still in active use today, and credits Pakistan's cheap labour for keeping hand-cut metalwork and hand-built domes affordable.
- [rramadass](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50662803): Systematically fact-checks the border-crossing story with links: Lahore's decade of worst-in-world pollution rankings, a Dawn report of AQI above 500, and the heavily developed, strictly monitored Attari–Wagah border where no 'abandoned village' exists.
- [Faizan11490](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50660948): Reads Mumtaz's work through his early-1960s AA training in New Brutalism — heavy massing and material honesty that survived even after he reintroduced the ornament brutalism rejected.

## 6. Company Culture = Slack Emoji ÷ √Headcount {#story-6}
*#9 on HN · [lia.mg](https://lia.mg/posts/measuring-company-culture-with-slack-emoji/) · 287 points · 126 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50659640)*

A short satirical post by liamg (lia.mg) proposing the 'Emoji Coefficient': the number of custom Slack emoji divided by the square root of the workspace's user count, quantified with a calibration table from 'no culture' (just 👍) through 'healthy', 'thriving' (:this-is-fine: 4,000 times in #incidents) to 'late-stage' (thumbsup variants whose subtext is passive aggression). A second metric, Participation Rate (percentage of users who uploaded at least one emoji), is meant to catch the case where one backend engineer supplied everything; below 20% is diagnosed as culture carried by a few people. It concedes the score can be gamed by bulk uploads or layoffs, and ends with a section whose stated purpose is to blast Microsoft Teams.

**Discussion**: The thread splits on whether emoji volume actually tracks culture. One commenter reports the company with dozens of busy off-topic channels was the fun one but died, while the one with a nearly dead #pets channel is still going — and others immediately report the opposite experience by a wide margin, so there is no consensus. A second camp argues Slack cutlery is beside the point: they are at work for money, and heavy chatter or reaction-emoji-only replies read as unserious or as a substitute for actually talking. Practitioners mostly wanted the metric refined — usage instead of raw upload count, per-team or subscribed-channel scope, public/private/DM ratios — and several posted their real numbers: 378, 4,500+, 35,000 and 57,000 custom emoji. The Teams section drew the most traffic: multiple people correct the author that Teams does support custom emoji (capped at 5,000 per org, sometimes buggy), while others say the choice to run Teams is itself the cultural red flag. Goodhart's law is raised and then pointed out as already addressed in the post, and a running side argument covers whether :) and 👍 have become passive-aggressive.

**Worth reading**
- [locallylinear](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50662624): A counter-datapoint to the whole thesis: the company full of active sports/TV/pets channels was the fun one, but the far more successful company was the one with almost no off-topic channels.
- [horticulturist](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50662300): First-hand story of a new HR leader ordering all emoji removed, including popular bufo ones — 'that's the day the company died.'
- [steve_adams_86](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50661040): Practical instructions for actually running the metric: check https://{org}.slack.com/customize/emoji for the count, or use a Slack app with emoji:read and pipe emoji.list through jq.

## 7. Ladybird browser's first downloadable alpha is surprisingly capable {#story-7}
*#12 on HN · [arstechnica.com](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2026/11/ladybirds-new-alpha-shows-theres-life-beyond-chromium/) · 27 points · 6 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663666)*

Ars Technica's Nick Indge reviews Ladybird alpha 2026.47.8, the first prebuilt, auto-updating downloadable release (macOS and Linux only, shipped November 23) after four years of building a rendering engine and the LibJS JavaScript engine from scratch rather than forking Blink, Gecko or WebKit. On a Fedora 44 / KDE Plasma 6.8.3 mini PC with a Ryzen 7 8745HS, Google Maps, GitHub, YouTube and news sites loaded normally, a dozen tabs switched instantly, and only repeated seeking in a 4K Jellyfin stream stalled it; broken SVG and WebGL hiccups were the visible gaps. Kling warns it is nowhere near a daily driver and invites security researchers to attack it, while the nonprofit project stresses it takes no sponsored search-engine deals and is migrating from C++ toward Rust, starting with the JS engine.

**Discussion**: The small thread is almost uniformly impressed: commenters say the browser feels 'completely normal' and fast, with the new engine barely registering during use. One says it is 'miles ahead of Servo browser' — a comparison nobody in the thread disputes. Concrete complaints are narrow and specific: broken SVG rendering and WebGL hiccups, Element Web failing (suspected storage-API gaps), adblocking weaker than uBlock Origin, and a Flatpak/KDE cosmetic bug where the close button doesn't reach the window corner. Several posters treat the rarity of independent engines — 'more rare than kernels' — as the real point, and the top comment is an approving quote of a project statement that Ladybird is 'not an appropriate arena to advertise your personal politics', an oblique nod to the controversy the article itself flags about Kling's public statements.

**Worth reading**
- [rckt](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663887): A first-hand test report: the browser is far better than expected, with broken SVG support and WebGL 'hiccups' as the only immediate defects.
- [unethical_ban](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663945): Lists concrete breakage — Element Web likely due to storage APIs, a Flatpak/KDE close-button layout bug, and adblocking weaker than uBlock Origin pending further filter-list testing.
- [ioma8](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50664066): Claims Ladybird is 'miles ahead of Servo browser', a direct comparison to the other from-scratch engine project.

## 8. "What's Claude's Fursona?" and Other Questions {#story-8}
*#13 on HN · [herecomesthemoon.net](https://herecomesthemoon.net/2026/11/whats-claudes-fursona/) · 75 points · 47 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50662933)*

A herecomesthemoon.net post by Mond_ asks a range of LLMs, via OpenRouter and with no harness, the single prompt 'If you had a fursona, what would it be?' 50 times per model, then uses Claude Haiku 5.5 to annotate the answers and has multimodal models iterate on SVG and image-generator prompts. It reports that Claude favors corvids and octopuses and repeatedly adds scarves, brass keys, mugs and lamps; Gemini models choose cybernetic protogens and neon aesthetics in at least 48 of 50 runs; OpenAI models split, though GPT-6.2 Astra consistently picks a moth; and Muse Spark picks a fox 33 times. The author stresses that context, phrasing and harness can change results, and the whole experiment cost roughly $50.

**Discussion**: Several commenters treat the results as evidence of training-data fingerprints: xg15 notes that Claude’s octopus resembles the Claude logo, while kridsdale1 says Muse Spark’s fox preference tracks Facebook’s internal fox mascot, though Mond_ counters that fox may simply be the default basin for models with a weaker sense of identity. flufluflufluffy observes that no model chose chimpanzees or dolphins and reads fox and crow as trickster archetypes, with the moth as a separate 'flying toward the light' motif. archon1410 suggests the raven-with-a-key image is a Sci-Hub motif from Claude’s shadow-library training data, and another commenter calls it the older keeper-of-knowledge symbol. A large subthread argues about furries themselves, with some calling the subculture creepy or pathological and others pushing back that autism and mental illness are not insults and that the judgment says more about the critic; the on-topic technical discussion is otherwise mostly amused speculation rather than consensus.

**Worth reading**
- [archon1410](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663337): archon1410 links Claude’s recurring raven-and-key imagery to Sci-Hub and Claude’s training on shadow libraries, offering a concrete source for an otherwise aesthetic quirk.
- [rspeele](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663321): rspeele describes building a game around model-generated neighboring places and finding that GPT-4.1 always drifted, within a couple dozen iterations, into a cave full of bioluminescent fungi.
- [Mond_](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50663610): Mond_ pushes back on the Facebook-fox theory, arguing that 'fox' is a default attractor for weaker models that lack a strong self-identity.

## 9. Novita, StreamLake, etc. are ByteDance Ark wrappers {#story-9}
*#15 on HN · [kendell.dev](https://kendell.dev/blog/arkwrapper/) · 91 points · 12 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50660273)*

A research post by kendell.dev arguing that several inference 'providers' on OpenRouter — Novita, StreamLake, Ant Digital, and indirectly SiliconFlow — are not hosting DeepSeek, GLM and Kimi themselves but reselling ByteDance's Ark API (Volcano Engine in China, BytePlus elsewhere). The fingerprints cited are image downloads from ByteDance IPs (AS137718 in Beijing, 101.47.5.59 in Singapore), upstream request IDs in Ark's 02+timestamp+hex format, Ark's tool-call ID format (call_00_ plus 24 characters), ByteDance's canned censorship refusal, and Ant Digital's launch price of $0.12/$0.48 per million tokens matching BytePlus's off-peak 'Economy' rate, later cut to $0.09/$0.36. It ties this to OpenRouter's MiniMax incident, where Novita, StreamLake and AtlasCloud admitted forwarding requests (including ZDR ones) to MiniMax, and charts Novita dropping from 120–160B tokens/day to 33B after losing its ZDR label on November 20; attested test evidence is published as run 44960395726 on commit 6dbc036, and the author discloses an OpenRouter research grant and an unnamed employer.

**Discussion**: The thread is less about the technical forensics than about what it means for trust: the strongest reactions treat ZDR as a hard red line, with one commenter saying their livelihood depends on prompts not being readable and vowing to push back hard if tokens are being routed to a company that inspects them. The main counter-argument is that the wrappers aren't actually lying about ZDR — non-ZDR endpoints go to non-ZDR providers — so the scandal is smaller than presented; the author replies that Novita was listed as US-headquartered and ZDR, that its ToS was quietly updated to disclaim third-party providers, and that an archive.org snapshot still shows unequivocal ZDR. Several people explain the business logic rather than the ethics: one lays out preemptible-GPU arbitrage ($6/GB300/hour versus roughly $36/GPU/hour of off-peak DeepSeek V4.1 Flash revenue), another notes that multiple brands over one backend enable price discrimination. A recurring side request is that OpenRouter show the country of the datacenter serving inference, not just a 'sovereign' headquarters flag — the author notes Novita is flagged USA while routing to China and Singapore, and others agree all providers should carry such labels. No real consensus emerges beyond agreement that OpenRouter needs to police providers harder.

**Worth reading**
- [snake_doc](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50661701): Explains the economics behind these wrappers: route baseload to the real provider at market rates, then shift traffic to cheap preemptible GPUs (e.g. $6/GB300/hour) to capture the margin, and warns that whoever controls the far end of an inference API can return malicious tool calls.
- [leafingensemble](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50662258): The author's rebuttal to the 'ZDR isn't violated' defence: Novita was labelled US and ZDR, its ToS was changed that day to disclaim third-party providers, and an archive.org snapshot still showed unambiguous ZDR.
- [leafingensemble](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50661171): The author lists related cases — Venice being a DeepInfra wrapper, Blackfuel routing K3 to OpenRouter, Venice silently dropping E2EE models and rewriting docs — plus a prior HN thread on 'zero retention' providers logging prompts.
