## 2. How close can pure functional programming get to C? {#story-2}
*#4 on HN · [rtfeldman.com](https://rtfeldman.com/fp-vs-c) · 65 points · 7 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218195)*

A post by Richard Feldman on rtfeldman.com testing whether Roc, a young functional language still weeks away from its 0.1.0 release, can approach C on DEFLATE compression. He spent significant time optimizing Niclas Ahden's roc-deflate package and benchmarked it against libdeflate on the Silesia corpus across 144 cases, finding roc-deflate a median 8.9% slower on an AMD Ryzen 9 3900X and 2.6% slower on an M1 MacBook Pro, with a worst case 18% slower and a best case roughly 18% faster. His argument is that a single-digit median gap is a reasonable price for guaranteed memory safety, no side effects, no FFI and no build scripts, since C's performance ceiling remains higher but Roc offers stronger guarantees.

**Discussion**: Commenters largely accepted the result and treated it as an interesting data point rather than a language war. The most substantive thread argued that the real gap is not bounds checks but C/C++'s willingness to elide copies and mutate distant state directly, which matters because memory bandwidth is often the binding constraint — and one commenter tied this to why functional languages have historically struggled in supercomputing despite theoretical parallelism advantages. A dissenting question challenged the benchmark choice itself: libdeflate's output size is comparable to zstd but it takes far longer, so why treat it as the performance champion — answered by the suggestion that a zstd implementation with a correctness proof would be far harder, and DEFLATE is simply the tractable target for this kind of project. Others were friendly and curious, asking for resources on hardware-aware access patterns and for examples of well-known programs written in Roc.

**Worth reading**
- [jandrewrogers](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218599): Argues the key gap is C/C++'s extreme copy elision and direct mutation of remote state, which saves memory bandwidth at a maintainability and safety cost, and explains why FP languages often underperform in supercomputing.
- [jeffbee](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218676): Questions whether libdeflate is a meaningful performance benchmark at all, noting it produces zstd-like output sizes but takes roughly 40 times longer.
- [jdw64](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218681): Asks which well-known programs are built with Roc and how difficult its syntax is — a practical reality check on the ecosystem claims.

## 3. A practical guide to AI image generation, from diffusion to agents {#story-3}
*#7 on HN · [danielball.com](https://danielball.com/notes/ai_image_generation.html) · 45 points · 9 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50171379)*

Daniel Ball's guide to how image models work and how to use them, drawn from his own Python experiments calling several providers to make pixel-art sprites, photoreal card-table backgrounds and restaurant posters that don't look AI-generated. It argues most models are diffusion (latent, ~8x smaller internal canvas, seeded, cheap and local) while newer multimodal models like GPT Image, Gemini and Meta's Muse plan and paint in one model, which buys instruction-following, readable text and conversational editing at the cost of seed control and fixed output sizes. Practical advice: prices span more than 100x (a fraction of a cent up to 15 cents+, Nano Banana at $0.039), describe the camera and film rather than 「stunning」, put a thin interface in front of every provider, treat generation as a slow failing job, and let ordinary code handle exact sizing, palettes and layout.

**Discussion**: The thread barely argues with the article and mostly extends it. The top commenter asks why local Flux/Qwen runs look one to two years behind ChatGPT and take up to five minutes per image on a decent gaming PC, wondering whether it is hardware, configuration or simply the state of consumer-grade local models; the only reply points him to r/StableDiffusion prompts and ComfyUI workflows, so no diagnosis is reached. The substantive branch is about procedural graphics as an alternative for pixel art: instead of bitmaps, store short sequences of drawing instructions that an LLM writes, which sidesteps the grid alignment, palette and high-frequency noise failures of large generative models. The author of that project cites an interview with King's Quest creators Ken and Roberta Williams, who said storing full bitmaps per scene was too expensive in the 1980s, so they stored drawing instructions and rendered on demand; others plan to try it for backgrounds, tiles and sprites. A final commenter says asking Opus 5.5 to write Python for logos is more consistent and controllable than generative AI for that job, which fits the thread's rough consensus: use AI for the creative or code-generating part, keep exact output deterministic.

**Worth reading**
- [turbobutton](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218867): First-hand frustration that local Flux/Qwen output trails ChatGPT by one to two years and takes up to five minutes per image on a gaming PC, with the cause left unresolved.
- [vunderba](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50172003): Describes an LLM-driven procedural graphics approach for EGA-style scenes — storing drawing instructions instead of bitmaps — with links to agi-stylist and a style gallery.
- [vunderba](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50181470): Traces the idea to the King's Quest creators, who stored simple drawing instructions rather than full bitmaps because 1980s storage was too expensive.

## 4. Dessa: Conversation Model trained only on speech {#story-4}
*#8 on HN · [block.xyz](https://block.xyz/dessa) · 348 points · 132 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50213925)*

Block's small AI research lab — founded by former members of Toronto startup Dessa and reporting to Jack Dorsey — built Dessa, a full-duplex speech-to-speech model trained from scratch on raw human conversation with no text and no pretrained models, and calls it the first realistic live-conversation AI trained only on speech. It learned backchanneling, mid-sentence laughter, volume changes and interruption timing from audio alone, runs faster than real time locally on a MacBook Pro M4 Max or better, and was trained on a single NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 rack; the lab claims it picked up language from hundreds of times fewer words than LLMs. The post is a demo page of recorded human–Dessa calls (plus one with GPT-Live-1), with no weights, API or product, and an invitation for safety researchers to apply for early access.

**Discussion**: The thread splits sharply on the demos: some call the voice uncanny and the best they have heard, while others say the hand-picked clips sound tipsy, soap-operatic or nonsensical (「How's the winter stuff?」), and several demand open weights, an API or something runnable before believing it — a few compare it to Sesame's CSM hype that never shipped. The most substantive correction is that 「one GB200 NVL72」 means a 72-GPU rack worth roughly $4–6M, not a single GPU, which badly undercuts the 「indie AI」 framing even though the team is under ten people. Commenters who use ChatGPT's advanced voice mode dislike the simulated backchanneling and interruptions, saying 「uh huh」 kills their train of thought and the constant filler is exhausting rather than human. Tool use is another recurring doubt: with no text training the model cannot read a system prompt or emit tool calls, so it would have to be steered by voice or paired with a separate text LLM, and several argue the intelligence is clearly a generation behind the voice. The dominant worry is misuse — voice cloning plus natural conversational tics for grandparent, ransom and social-engineering scams — with people sharing real AI scam-call anecdotes; a minority also object to the AI-girlfriend framing of the third clip.

**Worth reading**
- [jedbrooke](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218436): Points out that GB200 refers to Nvidia's 30-plus-GPU rack-scale system, not a single GPU, so training on 「one GB200 NVL72」 is far less impressive than the phrasing suggests.
- [michelsedgh](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50217958): Notes that a model trained purely on audio cannot read text system prompts or emit tool calls, so agentic use requires spoken instructions or pairing it with a separate text LLM.
- [mrinterweb](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50216664): Lays out a concrete impersonation attack — clone a voice from social video, SIM-swap the number, call family or subordinates — and says a shared-memory challenge question is the only defense they can think of.

## 5. Bringing uv and ty together {#story-5}
*#10 on HN · [astral.sh](https://astral.sh/blog/uv-ty-integration) · 109 points · 24 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50172962)*

An Astral blog post announcing an experimental preview that wires uv (the package/project manager) into ty (its type checker and language server). Two concrete features: first-class PEP 723 inline-metadata scripts, where ty uses uv to build a per-script environment so each script is checked against its own declared dependencies in the CLI and editor; and `uv check`, a unified diagnostic command that syncs the environment, checks packages or a whole workspace (`--package`, `--all-packages`, `--script`), and reuses the ty version pinned in the lockfile. It needs uv 0.12.3+ and ty 0.0.76+; automatic environment setup stays opt-in because installing dependencies executes third-party code, and Ruff support in `uv check` plus unused-dependency checks are promised later.

**Discussion**: The thread splits on whether merging the tools is good. The top comment argues ty works fine today with pip, pip-tools or any other manager, and that uv brings opinionated quirks and its own lockfile many people dislike, so the tools are better left standalone; replies counter that integration is optional, that projects using ty without uv are a tiny minority, and that plenty of teams use uv but not ty simply to avoid arguing for another tool in CI. A sub-thread defends uv's opinionatedness as the cure for two decades of Python packaging pain, with old-timers noting uv learned from earlier tools rather than inventing the category. Strategically, several read this as Astral responding to pyrefly's momentum by making ty the default that arrives with uv, while others say ty should have been integrated with Ruff instead, or that uv will simply subsume both. Practitioners are warm on the script support — poor editor support for PEP 723 scripts was the reason one commenter never adopted them — but one flags ty's hesitant Django support as negating the benefit, and others worry about what OpenAI's acquisition of Astral means long term.

**Worth reading**
- [WhyNotHugo](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50217379): Argues ty is perfectly usable on non-uv projects and that uv's opinionated quirks and custom lockfile make blending the tools a net loss for users of pip-tools or other managers.
- [olive-n](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218857): Reads the integration competitively: pyrefly has been gaining traction, and shipping ty inside uv makes it the path-of-least-resistance default rather than a tool people seek out.
- [xavdid](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218030): First-hand account that the missing piece for PEP 723 scripts was exactly this: without a matching venv for editor completions, the feature was too painful to use beyond toy scripts.

## 9. C vs. C#: racing VLC's Gaussian blur {#story-9}
*#18 on HN · [martinfinkel.com](https://martinfinkel.com/blog/gaussian-blur-c-vs-csharp/) · 61 points · 12 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50176544)*

Martin Finkel ported VLC's gaussianblur.c filter line-by-line to C#, loaded as a native VLC 4 plugin via his VLCLR project and .NET Native AOT, and benchmarked both on the same 1080p clip on a 4-core 11th-gen Core i7 with byte-identical output. The straight port was about 23% slower per frame (RyuJIT recomputing Math.Min and keeping the accumulator in memory, versus clang hoisting both); after an October 12 update hoisting loop bounds into locals and switching to nint indices, it landed within 8% of the C and ahead at sigma 4. Applying the same algorithmic work to both (magic-number division, cache-friendly row accumulation, weight symmetry, separate border loops, no SIMD) made both 2.5x faster than current VLC; adding AVX2 intrinsics put them roughly on par at sigma 2 and 4, 9% apart at the largest blur, and about 30x faster than the shipped filter — around 140 fps at 1080p and sigma 8 on one core. He also filed merge requests for two unrelated VLC bugs: hardware decoding turning half the frame green with CPU filters, and the blur reading two bytes past each line's right edge.

**Discussion**: Commenters were most surprised that VLC's shipped filter uses no SIMD at all and runs on the CPU rather than the GPU, with several saying a GLSL or compute shader is the obvious place for this and one linking Aras Pranckevičius's recent post on fast blur with an animated radius. The strongest technical pushback came from a commenter pointing to O(n) recursive Gaussian algorithms as a bigger win than hand-tuning, which drew a detailed counter-argument that FFT and recursive methods have per-sigma overheads, force a dozen CPU-specific variants and constant maintenance, and that Teams-style background blur already shows the aliasing you get from subsampling at large sigma. A side thread weighed why anyone would want Native AOT, with startup time, native interop and smaller Docker images cited. On the headline number, one commenter argued 8% is a mountain in most industries, while another dismissed the comparison as meaningless for a single filter step whose CPU time barely affects revenue.

**Worth reading**
- [anderskaseorg](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218830): Points to a survey of O(n) Gaussian blur algorithms that are independent of blur radius, i.e. a bigger algorithmic lever than the SIMD work benchmarked here.
- [schobi](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218926): Explains why the theoretically faster O(n) methods are not automatically better: FFT overhead, sigma-dependent variant switching, aliasing when subsampling, and a dozen architecture-specific implementations to test and maintain.
- [Dylan16807](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218590): Pushes back on treating 8% as decisive, arguing that a few percent on one step of a pipeline barely moves revenue and that saved CPU time matters even less.

## 10. Show HN: Corgi, a helpful {agent, terminal} multiplexer {#story-10}
*#20 on HN · [corgi.computer](https://corgi.computer/) · 72 points · 18 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50214373)*

Corgi is a terminal multiplexer built for running coding agents, posted as a Show HN by its author eiopa, inspired by Herdr and used as the author's daily driver for a few weeks. It keeps agents and processes running in the background like tmux, shows what each agent is doing with an activity feed, adds F1 to jump to the next unread agent and Ctrl-T to jump into work, and offers a unified launcher with fast search/resume over sessions and inline token-usage tracking for Codex, Claude Code and Pi across multiple accounts. Terminal extras include click-to-copy links, ⌘V image paste with thumbnail previews (both requiring Ghostty Nightly), per-workspace CPU/memory/GPU tracking, and a `corgi --skill` command that teaches agents to drive it; the author notes he built it during a frustrating sick leave.
