## 1. Inkling 2 Mini {#story-1}
*#3 on HN · [thinkingmachines.ai](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/) · 915 points · 172 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50581260)*

Thinking Machines' release post for Inkling 2 Mini, a Mixture-of-Experts model with 26.4B total parameters and 3.4B active (5.4B effective weight activations per token because the middle layers are run three times per token). It claims top scores among open-weights models under 100B on agentic coding benchmarks — DeepSWE v1.1 48.2%, SWE-bench Verified 79.2% with bash only, SWE-bench Pro v2 77.9% — while trailing Qwen3.8-27B on knowledge and multi-turn tasks (MultiChallenge 50.0%, ComplexConstraints v1.1 33.4%). Most weights are trained and deployed natively in NVFP4 with the last four layers in MXFP8 for a ~20GB footprint, and a variable-loop research variant (TML-d24-varloop-research) backs experiments showing no-CoT time horizon rising from 16s at one pass to 37s at three, with shorter but still legible chains of thought and no rise in agentic-sabotage scores.

**Discussion**: The thread is mostly enthusiastic: commenters call the blend of a small MoE, native 4-bit training and looping a genuine step for local inference, and several explain the architecture's lineage (Universal Transformer, NVIDIA's Dory, TinyRecursiveModels, the DIY 'RYS' layer-repetition experiments), with one noting 3x is also the depth where hand-duplicated layers paid off in Qwen models. Early hands-on reports cluster around 10-20 tokens/s on M1 Pro/Max Macs, ~28 tok/s decode and ~480 tok/s prefill on a 7900XTX, and ~52 tok/s on an RTX 5090, with people trading llama.cpp PR numbers and a small patch worth roughly 30-35% speedup. Skeptics push back hard on benchmarks: multiple testers report the model burning 15-20k thinking tokens, inventing a WordPress hook and then stubbornly searching for it, and being 'even more talkative than Qwen' on Artificial Analysis token-use charts, while others argue it plainly replaces Qwen3.8-27B for coding since looping boosts reasoning but not memorized knowledge. There is rough agreement that speed is the open question — one commenter computes that an M1 Max should hit ~74 tok/s and sees only 18 — plus side debates on whether looping hurts monitorability and on how open-weight labs make money.

**Worth reading**
- [hedgehog](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50584949): Reports 27 tok/s decode on a Ryzen 395 via llama.cpp Vulkan and posts a two-line ggml_cont patch for the SiTU activation that he measures at about 30% faster, confirmed by a 7900XTX owner.
- [dofm](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50583503): Detailed hands-on failure: the Unsloth Q4 build spent all 19,200 allowed thinking tokens on a single-file WordPress plugin without finishing, invented a core hook, then kept searching for the nonexistent hook even after tool use was enabled.
- [meatmanek](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50584615): Does the memory-bandwidth math: with 400GB/s and ~5.4B effective active parameters an M1 Max should generate near 74 tok/s, but it gets ~18, suggesting the runtime is far from optimized (others dispute the active-parameter accounting).

## 2. ClawDOS – An AI coding agent for DOS {#story-2}
*#4 on HN · [ricapar.net](http://ricapar.net/ClawDOS/) · 32 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50586292)*

A hobby project by Ricapar, written almost entirely by Claude, that runs a full coding agent harness on an original IBM PC XT — 8088 at 4.77 MHz, 640K RAM, CGA — emulated in 86Box and also demoed on a real 5160. HARNESS.EXE is a 176 KB DOS binary (13,000 lines of C/C++ built with Open Watcom, FreeDOS 1.4 plus mTCP) that speaks the Claude Messages API itself, doing everything but TLS, which is offloaded to a Python/socat/stunnel tunnel on a second machine that can also hold the API key. It exposes 18 tools (read/write/edit files, run DOS programs and capture output, take CGA screenshots encoded as PNG on the 8088, poke memory and I/O ports, PC speaker music, notes in C:\MEMORY), ships a Turbo C 2.0 / TINYASM / WASM / GW-BASIC toolchain, and reports a sample 'write and run PRIMES.C' turn finishing 64 seconds after Enter.

**Discussion**: The thread is short and mostly appreciative rather than technical — 32 points and five comments, with no one questioning the claims or the practicality. The substantive thread is about a real gap: linzhangrun argues the market badly lacks agents for the huge installed base of WinXP/Win7-era machines, noting that nearly all current agents require Windows 10 1809 or later, so debugging old devices usually means fighting to get SSH working. That is seconded by first-hand experience: unleaded says they built a win32 agent this month and watched DeepSeek's chain of thought realise it was running inside NT 4.0 after a directory listing, then went off, found the DirectX SDK bundled with VS6 and one-shotted a 'write a 3D shooter' prompt. Others treat the whole genre as playful rather than useful — Tiberium calls running agents in odd environments (DOS, Linux on a feature phone, Windows 95) 'cute and mischievous' — and sans_souse simply says they love it. No corrections or criticism of ClawDOS came up.

**Worth reading**
- [unleaded](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50586895): A commenter who built a win32 agent this month describes DeepSeek's CoT noticing it was on NT 4.0 and then one-shotting a 3D shooter using the DirectX SDK shipped with VS6.
- [linzhangrun](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50586985): Concrete complaint behind the 'agents for old machines' idea: almost all agents demand Windows 10 1809+, so legacy debugging falls back to getting SSH working.

## 4. Clojure will affect the way you think about programming (2017) {#story-4}
*#7 on HN · [eli.thegreenplace.net](https://eli.thegreenplace.net/2017/clojure-the-perfect-language-to-expand-your-brain/) · 63 points · 21 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50585376)*

Eli Bendersky's 2017 blog post argues Clojure is the best 'next language' to learn, citing Alan Perlis's line that a language which doesn't affect how you think about programming isn't worth knowing. He walks through why it qualifies: it's a Lisp with uniform syntax and real macros (core.async is built on them), it favors persistent immutable data structures, maps/vectors and higher-order functions over objects, and it supports REPL-driven development. He also covers protocols as a Go-like alternative to inheritance, optional checking via clojure.spec and core.typed, STM/atoms/futures, seamless Java interop, ClojureScript, and notes he'd consider it his first Lisp usable in production after 15 years of dabbling.

**Discussion**: The thread splits on whether Clojure's ideas actually transferred. Several commenters credit it with permanent changes in how they model code — thinking in maps and functions, decomposing problems as data transformations — while one who learned it early says the lasting effect was a deep distaste for dynamic types and blind map/list usage, and that the ML family gave him a paradigm he kept. Others push back on the 'advanced language' framing: one argues TypeScript offers dynamic-plus-static typing, union types, hot reload, the biggest package ecosystem and works in mainstream IDEs without Emacs, so he never hit a ceiling that justified learning Clojure; a Clojurist replies that hot reload is not a real REPL, since you can't redefine one var in a live process or run tests without recompilation. The biggest argument is over relevance: one long-time Clojure user, a decade in, calls the article obsolete post-LLMs and says software is 'largely solved' so only distribution matters — a claim others call nonsense, pointing to vibe-coded outages and the flood of low-quality software, and arguing that being on-trend isn't the same as being right.

**Worth reading**
- [zergrush](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50586465): A self-described decade-long Clojure user argues the whole premise is outdated: LLMs mean you no longer need to learn a language's internals, Clojure's developer-experience advantage is gone, and 'software is largely solved' so only distribution matters.
- [bigstrat2003](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50586819): Direct rebuttal to the 'post-LLMs' claim: you still need to know how to program, vibe-coded outages show hand-written code often wins, and if software were solved we'd get a deluge of quality software rather than a deluge of crap.
- [vemv](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50587055): A concrete correction to the TypeScript comparison: TypeScript has no real REPL — you can't redefine a single var in a running program, and you can't run tests without triggering recompilation.

## 5. Valkey kills the skiplist, cuts sorted set overhead by up to 43% {#story-5}
*#9 on HN · [valkey.io](https://valkey.io/blog/from-skiplist-to-b+tree/) · 114 points · 40 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50534387)*

A Valkey engineering blog post explaining that Valkey 9.2 swaps the skiplist behind large sorted sets for 'fbtree', a B+ tree variant with 61-way fanout, SIMD comparison of 4-byte node 'features', linked leaves and cached edge leaves. In a 5-million-member test, per-member index overhead fell from 50.3B to 28.5B with sequential inserts (43%, ~109 MB) and to 32.0B with random inserts (36%, ~92 MB), while ZADD score-update throughput rose 105% and ZREM 76%; ZSCORE, ZRANDMEMBER and range scans barely moved because they hit the hashtable or are bound by result generation. Behaviour is unchanged except that OBJECT ENCODING now returns 'btree' instead of 'skiplist'; the numbers come from a pre-merge branch benchmarked on a Graviton3 c7g.metal box and 9.2 is still in release-candidate testing.

**Discussion**: The thread splits between a substantive data-structure debate and a loud argument over whether the article itself is AI-written — several commenters call it 'slop' with little useful signal, while others point to PR #4206 (and its suggested reading order for reviewers) as the better artifact, and the author's own summary comment was itself accused of being generated. On substance, commenters note skiplists remain easier for concurrent writers while B+ trees win on cache behaviour, one argues skiplists don't actually need backward pointers (a finger/stack search suffices), and another observes that memory latency has made 'heavyweight' high-fanout trees win repeatedly. The biggest counterpoint comes from antirez, who says he wrote a comparable Redis sorted-set memory optimization using different techniques, with savings that are sometimes larger, and urges users to benchmark both. Side threads cover Valkey versus Redis adoption (AWS reportedly 20–35% cheaper for Valkey) and a request for the benchmark code.

**Worth reading**
- [antirez](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50586785): antirez says he independently implemented a different sorted-set memory optimization for Redis with savings that can exceed Valkey's, and advises trying both.
- [dreamcompiler](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50586161): Argues skiplists don't need backward pointers at all — a search 'finger' kept on the stack gives near-equivalent reverse traversal, undermining one of the article's stated overheads.
- [tyre](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50584939): Points to the final PR (#4206), which includes a reviewing guide with suggested reading order and explicit scope boundaries — the part worth reading instead of the blog post.

Commenters swapped techniques — focusing on a finger held between nose and screen, or trying distance changes — and one noted that having one eye much stronger than the other can make stereograms effectively invisible. A few pushed for more modern formats, suggesting Gaussian splats for headsets, Nintendo 3DS and Looking Glass Go displays, and a 'wigglegram'.

**Worth reading**
- [alterom](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50584430): Explains that cross-eye versus parallel viewing is the usual culprit and links two guides, arguing the 3D image carries far more information than the flat one.

## 7. DreamCoder: Coding harness and IDE from the author of Mastering Emacs {#story-7}
*#13 on HN · [masteringemacs.org](https://www.masteringemacs.org/article/announcing-dreamcoder) · 191 points · 52 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50581110)*

Mickey Petersen, who writes Mastering Emacs, announces DreamCoder, an AI coding harness and IDE he spent 15 months building after concluding that terminal agents like Claude Code — and the near-identical GUI harnesses that copied them — are the wrong interface for serious work. The long post is mostly a history of AI and Emacs: McCarthy's Advice Taker, LISP and the abandonment of M-expressions for S-expressions, Russell's interpreter, Stallman and Steele's Emacs, and Teitelman's PILOT/ADVISE system in Interlisp that became Emacs's advice.el. Its thesis is that symbolic AI failed as a route to intelligence — 'a cast-iron system made of stern rules cannot spawn general intelligence' — but survived as pliable tools, and that a harness should be owned and inspectable rather than a black box.

**Discussion**: Commenters split sharply. Many praise the essay's history and the IDE's distinct visual design, and several agent-harness builders chime in with alternative architectures (a Clojure nREPL harness where each session is a namespace, smalldocs for agent-generated documents). The loudest objection is licensing: DreamCoder is proprietary, requires an account and EULA, is free for personal use but paid for businesses past five users, and detractors argue the Mastering Emacs author of all people shipping a closed IDE is disappointing — one notes the embedded system prompt was extracted within minutes; the author replies that the prompt is visible and editable in the UI and that he is not VC-funded. There is also friction over Claude subscription support: the author says Anthropic won't let third-party harnesses use subscriber accounts, while others report T3, Amp and OAuth-based tools doing exactly that, and he offers to add OAuth if the TOS allows. On the design, the browser stealing C-n and C-w is cited as why Emacs keybindings aren't supported, and skeptics say the whole case reduces to 'I have taste' with no metrics; a few also complain the post is all context and should have linked dreamcoder.ai directly.

**Worth reading**
- [everforward](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50583494): A developer describes his own Clojure/nREPL harness where a session maps to a namespace holding its tools and model, letting the agent and human share the same live JVM — and admits it is wildly insecure against prompt injection.
- [komali2](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50584713): A commenter objects that the harness is proprietary, needs registration and a EULA, and that the Emacs-world author releasing non-FOSS code is a letdown; he adds that the hidden system prompt was reverse-engineered almost immediately.
- [mickeyp](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50586251): The author concedes code review is now most of the job and his Review feature doesn't solve sifting 20,000 AI-written lines; he thinks the answer is a local CI-like checklist tailored to what a human would flag.

## 8. Ask HN: I have been unemployed for 2y. 15 years of experience, US. What to do? {#story-8}
*#15 on HN · text post · 119 points · 116 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50583761)*

A text-only Ask HN post from a senior software engineer in the DC/DMV area with 15 years of experience (a Fortune 50 employer plus several mid-size companies). He quit a ~4-year job at a 1,000+ person insurance-tech company in January 2025 after being tipped off that layoffs were coming, received little or no severance, and has had no SWE job offers since; his savings are nearly gone (4-5 months left), he is tutoring math, and he is about to take a manager job at a pizzeria. He asks how to explain the resume gap and whether there is a well-paying path outside or adjacent to software (his stack: C#/.NET/AWS).

**Discussion**: The overwhelming consensus is that cold applications are dead — ATS filters and application volume mean the only reliable route is people who already know you: former colleagues, referrals, internal recruiters, meetups, staffing agencies, and contract-to-hire. Several commenters say the gap barely matters to a human but matters enormously to the software screening you, and one hiring-side commenter describes receiving a thousand applications a day, mostly AI-generated spam, and advises lying about the gap — advice others call unethical, while the OP notes his own attempt at 'independent consultant' framing got recruiters to hang up because he had no big-name clients. A minority push back on the network-only thesis: one 25-year veteran says he changed jobs twice this year through ordinary LinkedIn applications, and others report success through staffing agencies and by widening scope to IT ops, public sector, defense work near DC, or second-tier tech hubs. A recurring side thread is trades: commenters who left software describe becoming a plumber, an electro-mechanic (a former SWE with a PhD), and heavy-duty diesel and industrial-electric apprentices, citing steady demand and more rewarding physical work. The thread also questions the OP's decision to quit rather than ride out the layoffs for severance and unemployment — he concedes in hindsight it was a mistake — and a couple of commenters doubt the post's sincerity, noting the throwaway account, no resume, and no post in the 'Who wants to be hired' thread.

**Worth reading**
- [noprocrastedaga](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50584139): A commenter who has recently been on both sides of the hiring market says a single opening drew ~1,000 applications a day, mostly ChatGPT-written spam and outright fraud, and argues the only rational response is to lie about the gap and approach people directly rather than through cold applications.
- [pmg101](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50586863): Direct counterpoint to the 'networking is the only way' consensus: 25 years of experience, changed jobs twice this year, both times through ordinary LinkedIn applications, with plenty of interviews.
- [huijzer](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50586387): First-hand account of leaving software (PhD and SWE background) for an electro-mechanic role at a ~100-person company — happier, calmer colleagues, and he now automates work for the head mechanics.

## 10. Backblaze Notice at Collection and Privacy Policy {#story-10}
*#19 on HN · [backblaze.com](https://www.backblaze.com/company/policy/privacy) · 168 points · 53 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50585593)*

This is Backblaze's consumer privacy notice, effective December 1, 2026, not a product announcement — the page's top banner currently promotes a multi-exabyte storage agreement with CoreWeave, which is what likely put it on the front page.
