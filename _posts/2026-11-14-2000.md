## 1. Lovable apps expose passport details and more than 200K private messages {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [crissyfield.de](https://www.crissyfield.de/research/lovable-en/) · 273 points · 84 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499017)*

A security researcher (HN user neathack) analyzed 159,041 Lovable-built projects scraped from Common Crawl between December 2025 and September 2026, in collaboration with Handelsblatt journalist Lina Knees. 34,545 of those projects used Supabase, and 24,664 returned Supabase records on page load without any login; by issuing further read-only requests (never past the first paginated page, the author says), the team found email addresses in 10,560 tables across 7,403 sites (30% of that subset). Named cases include a German lifestyle forum leaking 80,000+ profiles and 200,000+ chat messages, a provider exposing 1,000+ records with passport numbers, expiry dates and nationalities, 280,000 customer emails, plus contracts, invoices, photo albums and internal WhatsApp chats; the author notes the 30% figure is not a confirmed leak rate and the Common Crawl sample is not representative.

**Discussion**: The biggest fight is about research ethics, not the bug: jacquesm argues the author crossed from white hat to 「quite probably criminal」 by pulling enough data to assess severity rather than stopping after proving access, while the author replies that only first pages were fetched, the full dataset was disclosed to Lovable, and the work was done with a data-protection and ethical-hacking lawyer (a detail jacquesm says belongs in the post). Several commenters push the blame onto Supabase's historical defaults and the complexity of row-level security policies — one links a Supabase discussion thread claiming a fix landed October 30 but that existing projects may keep the old defaults — and a practitioner explains Lovable sits on Supabase Auth with browser-side database access, so getting RLS wrong is easy, and describes using Lovable only to prototype then ejecting and re-deploying after review. Others argue AI is incidental (bad access control predates it) and that what is new is scale and the absence of any technical gatekeeping, with the loudest thread demanding liability and GDPR consequences — countered by the author's point that it is the customers, not Lovable, who can be sued. A long sub-thread mocks the 「flying a passenger plane without a license」 analogy as security maximalism, and another debates whether engineering ever cared about quality before LLMs.

**Worth reading**
- [SOLAR_FIELDS](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499905): Argues Supabase shares culpability: it deliberately chose frictionless onboarding over security by default, and a GitHub discussion suggests the October 30 fix may not apply to existing projects.
- [dudus](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50500145): Practitioner explanation: Lovable puts Supabase Auth and a browser-side JS client in front of the database, so authorization depends entirely on hand-written RLS policies — the same failure mode as Firebase — and the commenter's team only uses Lovable to prototype, then ejects and redeploys elsewhere.
- [neathack](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499661): The author adds a figure missing from the post: an 85%-confidence classification model put roughly 4,000 sites (about 15.9% of Supabase-using Lovable projects, ~2.5% of all 159k analyzed) as leaking privacy-sensitive data.

## 2. US IRS free online tax filing open source repo {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/IRS-Public/direct-file) · 62 points · 18 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499083)*

The GitHub repo IRS-Public/direct-file is the source release of Direct File, the IRS's free interview-style federal e-filing service, published for Tax Year 2024 under the SHARE IT Act of 2024 (Public Law 118-187) and OMB M-16-21. It documents the Scala-based Fact Graph (an XML knowledge graph that reasons over partially completed returns, running on the JVM and transpiled via Scala.js for the browser), transmission to the IRS Modernized e-File (MeF) API, and a State API that passes MeF XML plus enriched JSON to third-party state filing tools; it was built in-house with USDS, GSA and vendors TrussWorks, Coforma and ATI, and the README states the repo is archived, unmaintained, has PII/FTI/SBU/NSS-related code removed, and should not be used in production.

**Discussion**: The thread is largely a referendum on why this exists and whether it matters: one camp asks whether the repo is even legitimate, notes that searching irs.gov surfaces no link to it, and points out the Direct File program was killed under DOGE; another quotes the very first pull request, where a commenter says development stopped in January 2025 and the code is public only because federal law requires it. Several people read the closed PRs as evidence of amateur Git use, with others pushing back that government developers may simply be new to Git or working in low-drive institutional cultures. On policy, one commenter expects Direct File to return and be made permanent after the recent congressional shift, while another doubts anything before 2028 given the team was laid off and the administration's Impoundment Control Act disputes. A recurring side argument is international comparison: Europeans note they mostly don't file at all because income is pre-reported, and one longtime US resident says American mockery of European digital services looks odd next to 90s-era US admin tooling, while others predict AI coding will do to TurboTax what this repo hints at.

**Worth reading**
- [ram1500natrluvr](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50500103): Quotes the text of PR #1 by elijah-wright, who says he learned from a former IRS project manager that Direct File development stopped in January and the code is only public because federal law requires it.
- [simonw](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50500188): Simon Willison notes a site:irs.gov Google search turns up nothing linking to the repo, and attributes that to DOGE having killed the program.
- [raverbashing](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50500164): Raises the practical unresolved question of whether the IRS MeF filing endpoint that accepts the JSON/XML payloads is still open and free to use.

## 3. PyPI's redesigned project pages with clearer security signals are now live {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [blog.pypi.org](https://blog.pypi.org/posts/2026-11-06-ui-updates-complete/) · 148 points · 52 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50497892)*

PyPI 完成了从 7 月开始的界面改版，新增专门的 security 标签页，显示每个 release 的 provenance 状态（全部文件都有 attestation、完全没有、部分文件有、文件来自不同构建系统或仓库、以及「以前有 attestation 现在没有」的回退警告），并可逐文件查看 PyPI Publish 和 SLSA Build Provenance attestation 及透明度日志链接。项目页面把 provenance 警告置顶，release files 表支持搜索/过滤并标出 late files（首个 release 后超过 14 天上传的文件，2026 年 7 月起已禁止），release history 也显示文件数、provenance 状态和 quarantined/yanked/archived/pre-release 状态。工作由 OpenSSF 资助、Kabu Creative 实现，遵循 OpenSSF Attestations Style Guide。

**Discussion**: 争论几乎全部集中在信任模型上：一批人认为这套机制把 Python 生态的安全押在 GitHub、GitLab、Google 等美国大型营利公司身上，是中心化与锁定，并对 PyPI 当年移除 PGP 支持耿耿于怀。PyPI 维护者 woodruffw 在帖中大量回帖澄清：微软没有为 Trusted Publishing 或 attestations 付钱，支持哪些平台是务实而非教条的选择；本地构建未来可能通过 email attestation 获得签名，而“一旦用了 Trusted Publishing 就永久不能回头”的说法是错的；Codeberg 之所以迟迟未接入，是底层 Forgejo 目前要把 OIDC 私钥落盘，达不到 PyPI 作为凭据代理所需的密钥管理标准，但他本人希望有非营利提供方加入。SQLAlchemy 作者 zzzeek 提供了最有分量的实战抱怨：只要有部分文件带 attestation，其余文件就会触发大红警告，而且上传这种文件时 PyPI 根本不发邮件通知。另有多人指出 attestation 只证明代码托管在大平台并用其 CI 构建，既不识别恶意项目，也不防仓库被入侵，因此有人呼吁把精力转向可复现构建和分布式审查；也有人调侃「根本没人会去看这些页面」。

**Worth reading**
- [zzzeek](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50498790): zzzeek 以实际维护者身份说明：同一项目里只要部分文件走 Trusted Publishing、其余文件不走，就会挂上大红警告，PyPI 却不发任何邮件通知（当年弃用 GPG 时可是每个签名都发信），而且他以为这是不可逆的开关。
- [woodruffw](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50498894): woodruffw 直接纠正上面两点：这不是永久开关，并给出讨论链接；同时表示 PyPI 一直欢迎更多 publisher provider，只要满足密钥管理与知名度要求。
- [woodruffw](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499442): woodruffw 强调如果把 attestation 当成「安全金牌」就搞错了前提——它只是对一组声明做密码学签名以便审计，并给出支持本地构建的 email attestation 讨论链接。

## 4. A Family Tree of Software Job Titles {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [zwischenzugs.com](https://zwischenzugs.com/2026/11/03/a-family-tree-of-software-job-titles/) · 70 points · 21 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499038)*

Ian Miell (zwischenzugs) built a Graphviz-rendered family tree of 104 software job titles running from 「Computer」 (a person, in the 1940s) down to 「AI Agent / Context Engineer」 (c. 2025), laid out in five-year rows and seven coloured family columns, with solid arrows for lineage and dashed ones for influence. Its arguments: coding began as low-status clerical work split off from 「planning」 in Goldstine and von Neumann's 1947 paper, titles repeatedly fission and re-merge (webmaster → front-end/back-end → full-stack; prompt engineer absorbed into AI engineer almost on arrival), and the 「forward deployed engineer」 is essentially IBM's 1960s pre-sales systems engineer or old-style consulting under a new name. Miell admits the dates are his own best guesses rather than sourced citations, and invites PRs against the repo.

**Discussion**: The thread mostly agrees that these titles carry little information: one commenter lists seven ops-flavoured titles held over a career and says only 「Cloud Infrastructure Engineer」 meant anything, arguing most organisations have no idea what real DevOps is and would not hire a 「DevOps Engineer」 if they did. Others push back mildly, noting that at sufficient scale someone genuinely does need to own the tooling, and that a developer can enjoy learning new skills without wanting the on-call burden of ops. Agile and Scrum are widely declared dead or vestigial — one commenter has dropped the certifications from their CV, comparing them to the 1990s webmaster — while a data specialist says analyst/engineer/scientist titles persist even as AI cools demand. A recurring practical complaint is that the diagram itself is unusable on the page (no zoom), answered with links to the standalone SVG, the hosted site and a ctrl+scroll tip. Others note omissions: operations/incident/problem managers and 「member of technical staff」 are missing, and one commenter reads the 「coding was the clerical bit」 framing as a line that flatters the planners.

**Worth reading**
- [mikepurvis](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50500081): A decade-long 「robot devops」 practitioner explains why it is uniquely hard: robot fleet ops, developer simulation/test-hardware tooling and deployed-unit support are three different disciplines, with unvirtualised hardware snowflakes and no fat data pipe.
- [stroebs](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499468): Lists the seven infrastructure titles they have held (DevOps Engineer through SRE Lead) and argues all but Cloud Infrastructure Engineer were meaningless euphemisms for a generalist trusted with the AWS root account.
- [kristianc](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499710): A sharp one-liner: the claim that 1947 coding was the clerical bit and planning the real work is a story that conveniently flatters the planners.

## 6. Why get an expensive Pi when I have an old phone? {#story-6}
*#6 on HN · [so.long.thanks.fish](https://so.long.thanks.fish/why-pi-when-i-have-an-old-phone/) · 427 points · 251 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50440709)*

A maker's blog post arguing that a used budget Android phone (a Redmi 13C 5G or Huawei Mate 30 5G, roughly ¥300–500 / £33–56 / $45–75 on Taobao as of November 2026) beats assembling a Raspberry Pi 4 with an Arducam 64MP camera, GPS HAT, RTC, UPS and 4G HAT, which the author prices at about ¥2,317 / $347 fully equipped versus the phone's ¥300–500. The phone already includes camera, GPS, storage, clock, battery, touchscreen and cellular, and its USB-C port can talk to the author's light-controller PCB; he has rooted the Redmi, tested its camera, got it sleeping and waking on schedule, and is building a rig to compare its 50MP JPEGs against the Arducam. The unanswered question is supply: sourcing a few hundred matching phones with healthy batteries and predictable cameras.

**Discussion**: Most commenters ignore the phone entirely and push an adjacent idea: used corporate mini PCs and broken-screen laptops are cheaper and far more capable than a Pi 5, with Lenovo Tiny, Dell Micro/OptiPlex, HP ProDesk and thin clients all named repeatedly, and several people report years of home-server use. The Pi's defenders argue it is a different class of device — GPIO headers, a maintained kernel and userspace, passive cooling, tiny size, one-command flashing — and that people comparing it to NUCs are solving a problem the Pi was never meant for. Battery safety is the biggest objection to the phone plan: multiple people describe swollen MacBook and phone batteries from 24/7 charging, with the counter-arguments being charge limiting (AccuBattery, AlDente), bypass-charging modes, or removing the battery and feeding the terminals from a bench supply. Practitioners report real deployments — OnePlus and Fairphone handsets running k3s clusters and Nerves (Erlang/Elixir) systems — while others warn that locked bootloaders, dead-end vendor kernels with no security updates, and device-to-device variation make fleets of phones a refurbishment operation, not a purchase. On the article's own open question about supply, the sharpest reply is that new hardware scales trivially and used hardware starts you at the wrong end of the bathtub curve.

**Worth reading**
- [Freak_NL](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50495561): Argues the real blocker is scaling: a Pi purchase of 100 units all works, while 100 used phones put you at the wear-out end of the bathtub curve, and suggests buying unsold two-to-three-year-old budget stock instead.
- [lawik](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50496756): Points to Citronics, which refurbishes and certifies Fairphone 2/3 boards for commercial reuse, plus a Nerves/Elixir toolchain for programming the phones and a community trading tips in the Nerves Discord.
- [psilvia](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50498706): First-hand report of turning spare Android phones into three k3s nodes (24 cores, ~30GB RAM) on vanilla Debian with a custom kernel, batteries capped at 80%, with a GitHub repo.

## 7. Seagate's new hybrid drive raises some old questions {#story-7}
*#7 on HN · [arstechnica.com](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2026/11/seagate-resurrects-the-ssd-hdd-hybrid-drive/) · 25 points · 10 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50453901)*

Ars Technica covers Seagate's new FireCuda Forge, a hybrid enclosure pairing a mechanical HDD with a user-supplied M.2 2280 NVMe SSD, managed by Seagate's new Alkemi platform. It ships in 8TB ($699) and 20TB ($899) versions — SSD not included — with the SSD capped at 1TB and required to hit 2,400MB/s read and 2,000MB/s write, over USB4, for a claimed 2,500MB/s read and 2,000MB/s write combined. The piece argues Seagate has repeated Apple Fusion Drive's 2012 pitch without answering the 2012 questions: whether data is cached or tiered, how data is placed on first boot, and whether one failed drive destroys the whole pool, since the Alkemi data sheet says only 「two-tier」.

**Discussion**: The thread mostly ignores the product and argues about tiering in general. Several ZFS users push back on the idea that heterogeneous storage layers just work: one notes ARC/L2ARC/SLOG tuning cliffs, RAM overhead for L2ARC mapping, and the difficulty of serving postgres, thousands of sqlite databases and an oversized duckdb on one box at once. A counterpoint comes from a commenter running 640GB of L2ARC on a spinning-rust desktop with a 93% hit rate who says responsiveness is fine and L2ARC failure is harmless. Others want metadata-only caching and note that bcachefs was close to delivering easy SSD/HDD hybrid setups before its troubles, while one laments the absence of real Linux hierarchical storage management. The loudest subthread is pricing: a Swedish commenter says the Exos 24TB drive cited at $699 in the US costs about $1,393 locally, roughly triple its price a year ago, and another says Amazon marketplace listings for drives are now mostly scams and his local supplier limits buyers to one disk each. One commenter compares the enclosure-managed Alkemi design to Drobo's VXWorks box hiding BeyondRAID behind a single block device.

**Worth reading**
- [ssl-3](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50500079): First-hand numbers: 640GB of L2ARC at 66% utilization with a 93% hit rate over a week, costing 664MB of RAM, on a machine whose primary storage is all spinning disks.
- [mikae1](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499903): Concrete regional price data: the Exos 24TB drive Ars cites at $699 in the US sells for about $1,393 (14,160 SEK) in Sweden, up from 4,160 SEK a year earlier.
- [bilegeek](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499869): Argues metadata-only caching is the right split, and links the openzfs 「Special Failsafe」 pull request that would make a metadata special device survivable.

## 8. Advancing science and engineering with Antigravity's /teamwork {#story-8}
*#8 on HN · [research.google](https://research.google/blog/advancing-science-engineering-with-antigravitys-teamwork-feature/) · 109 points · 28 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50496811)*

A Google Research blog post by VP/Fellow Vahab Mirrokni describing results from Antigravity's /teamwork multi-agent harnesses running on Gemini 4 Argon/Carbon/Hydrogen, which pair parallel agent exploration with falsifiers such as the Lean 4 checker, test suites and hardware counters, and partition context so fresh agents and adversarial critics work from clean state. Claimed results include a full proof of the 2013 Courtade–Kumar conjecture in information theory (formalized in Lean 4, ~50 million lines), an end-to-end machine-checked formalization of the Weil conjectures (~1.8M lines of Lean over 28,800 subagent invocations), first formalizations of the Birkhoff and 3D Bochner–Riesz conjectures, progress on Tarjan's 1985 Deque Conjecture, an O(n log n) multiplication algorithm whose C implementation matches GMP bit-for-bit up to a billion bits, and Deep Review feedback used by 578 opted-in STOC 2027 submissions (survey N=70: 90% found it largely factual, 30%+ said it caught substantive proof bugs). The tooling is being rolled out in Antigravity alongside /boost.

**Discussion**: The thread's dominant reaction is not to the math results but to the post itself: several commenters say the write-up buries its point under low-level detail, and one jokes that Google used its own model to draft it — a complaint others extend to a company-wide 『too much AI, not enough human』 pattern in Google prose. A second cluster argues Antigravity as a harness is behind Claude Code and Codex, citing the lack of an auto mode and heavy permission prompts; that draws a correction that auto mode has shipped for several versions and recently became the default, with one user saying they still had to pass --dangerously-skip-permissions to get work done but praising the Carbon model. ## 20. Formalizing US Tax Code (Using Lean) {#story-20}
*#25 on HN · [lexifina.com](https://lexifina.com/blog/formalizing-the-us-tax-code) · 101 points · 62 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50497900)*

A blog post from lexifina.com (part of the CC0 global-law project) describing an attempt to translate Title 26 of the US Code into Lean, the proof assistant, using the Office of the Law Revision Counsel's structured XML release current to 3 November 2026. An automated cross-reference check produced 257 findings and, after AI-agent review, 138 genuine drafting faults — dead references to repealed or moved provisions, typos, wrong level words, duplicated subsection numbers and unclosed parentheses — each shipped with a Lean certificate re-checked in about 2.5 minutes. The authors also modeled 2025 federal income tax (post-One Big Beautiful Bill Act) and compared it with PolicyEngine-US, Tax-Calculator and UsTaxes on 5,000 households: they agreed with PolicyEngine on 4,993 (the other 7 differ by ≤$11 in EITC), and reported bugs in the other tools, including Tax-Calculator granting EITC to separate filers without checking the spouses lived apart and UsTaxes dropping Schedule C self-employment income (14 bugs, affecting roughly one ordinary household in six). A search over 195,787 returns and ~30,000 multi-year plans rediscovered known strategies — separate filing to stay under the §199A threshold ($6,317/yr), claiming children on the lower earner's return ($3,090/yr), smaller itemized deductions to cut AMT ($2,070/yr), Roth conversions in gap years — but the post's headline claim is the drafting-error count and the SALT marriage penalty (up to $25,000 on two separate returns vs $10,000 joint for a couple earning $300,000 each).

**Discussion**: The liveliest dispute is political, not technical: several commenters object that calling loopholes 「bugs」 smuggles in a value judgement Lean cannot make, since nothing in the code distinguishes an intentional incentive from an accident, and others counter that the concrete misalignments it found (mismatched cross-references, contradictory rounding) are defects by any reading. A second thread argues over the SALT finding — one commenter calls the joint-filing penalty a big deal for two-earner couples around $300,000 each, others reply that such households are a tiny share of the population and that high earners in the Bay Area and New York should not get federal relief for state taxes; the thread also spends several messages correcting people who read it as $300,000 combined rather than $600,000. A lawyer commenter pushes back on the popular 「laws are vague on purpose」 line, saying vagueness is usually a legislative compromise or a deliberate appeal to spirit and that formalizing it into logic mostly does not make sense, while others defend formalization as a way to force legislatures to say what they mean. On the technical side, skeptics ask what Lean actually adds beyond a scripting language, and the best answer offered is proving invariants (「you cannot be taxed more than your income」) whose counterexamples would be loopholes; a few dismiss the whole thing as another vibecoded legal formalization destined to be abandoned, and one points out that the authors' own admitted miss on the 20% small-business deduction suggests plenty of low-hanging fruit remains. Commenters also flag Catala, a purpose-built legal DSL already used in a French government service, as prior art the post does not mention.

- [DannyBee](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499896): A lawyer argues law is vague mainly as legislative compromise or deliberate appeal to spirit, that it is a representation of societal judgement rather than a consistent logic system, and that formalizing it generally does not make sense.
