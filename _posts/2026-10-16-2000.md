## 1. eBay Restricts Listings of Radio Equipment, but Exempts Ham-Band-Only Gear {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [arrl.org](https://www.arrl.org/arrlletterissue?issue=2026-10-15#eBay-Restricts-Listings-of-Radio-Equipment_-But-Exempts-Ham-Band-Only-Gear) · 48 points · 22 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50122034)*

This is an item from the ARRL Letter of October 15, 2026, reporting that eBay has placed new restrictions on listings of radio transmitting equipment in response to FCC rules (Third Report and Order, July 23, 2026) that took effect October 13. Equipment subject to FCC authorization must now have its FCC ID submitted to the marketplace and posted with the listing, though posts already online, small sellers and used equipment are exempt. After ARRL asked for clarification, eBay said devices operating only on amateur frequencies need no FCC ID, but two-way VHF/UHF gear able to operate outside the ham bands cannot be imported, advertised or sold in the US without FCC certification — and the FCC has not changed the underlying rule that amateur gear (except commercial HF amplifiers) needs no authorization.

**Discussion**: The dominant reading of intent is that this targets the flood of cheap Baofeng-style radios that ship able to transmit on public-safety and commercial bands; a reply argues those aren't hacked, since older UV-5R units simply left the factory unlocked and are prized for it. Several commenters worry the policy is far broader than the headline suggests, noting that eBay's initial seller messages implied an FCC ID was needed for anything transmitting RF, and that eBay is taking a harder line than the FCC, whose ban exempts used gear. Others push back that the practical impact is small: most consumer electronics and test gear carry FCC IDs already, and the used-equipment exemption covers much of what changes hands on eBay. Commenters also flag knock-on risks for Meshtastic devices and ESP32-based SDR experiments, and a recurring skeptical note that the restriction is mostly theater — cheap SDRs and radio firmware, either via key-combo reset or community builds, keep out-of-band transmit easy.

**Worth reading**
- [sc68cal](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50122754): Points out the sought-after old UV-5Rs were never hacked — they just shipped without a factory lock, unlike current units.
- [scrumper](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50122473): Notes the FCC's own rule exempts used gear while eBay's listing requirement appears stricter than the regulation.
- [techjamie](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50122507): First-hand detail that Baofeng's ham-band-only firmware is defeated by a key-combo factory reset restoring roughly 136-172 MHz transmit.

## 2. 90% of US working hours are spent on tasks not covered well by AI benchmarks {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [mercor.com](https://www.mercor.com/research/labor-index/benchmark-map/) · 182 points · 154 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50121179)*

这是 Mercor Research 的“Labor Index / Benchmark Map”：把 29 个公开、agentic、经济上较有代表性的基准（含 Mercor 自家的 APEX 系列）用 LLM 裁判按 Wang 等人的方法打标到美国劳工部 O*NET 的 923 个职业、18,797 个任务上，再用 Hatgis-Kessell 等人的工时占比估计加权，得出约 90% 的美国工作时间花在基准覆盖极少的任务上。具体数字：只有 208/923 个职业存在至少 5 条基准提示覆盖的任务，22 个职业族中有 6 个完全没有；被 Eloundou 等人评为“AI 暴露”的任务上榜概率约为未暴露任务的 6 倍，而 O*NET 任务重要性最高的任务上榜概率是最低者的 6–10 倍。作者承认样本仅 29 个基准、结果属临时性，且用 APEX 对投行、法律、会计、软件工程四个职业做 O*NET 加权后，与简单平均得分差别不大，因为不到三分之一的 O*NET 任务有足够 APEX 覆盖。

**Discussion**: 头号评论把结论读成“资本只想自动化高薪好工作”，随后线程迅速偏离主题，变成一场关于“技术到底有没有带来通缩”的长辩论：一方举 10TB 硬盘、千兆宽带、iPad 说明比特类商品大幅降价，另一方反驳真正的支出大头——住房、医疗、教育、食品——长期涨价，是“小玩意变便宜、必需品变贵”。第二大类讨论集中在基准本身的可信度：有人指出“covered well”实际只是“covered at all”，13 条提示就算“覆盖良好”，按常人的定义标题数字应该超过 99.9%；一位在“覆盖良好”职业族里做 AI 方案的从业者说 90% 可能高估，因为基准覆盖的恰恰是价值最低、最容易量化的任务。还有大量一线经验帖：软件工程师说 80% 时间在协调“stakeholder”分歧而非写代码，AI 提高编码效率后时间被 Jira、QA、汇报填满（Baumol 成本病的小尺度版本），有人干脆转行做电工、飞行员；一条关于机器人捡垃圾的分支则争论硬件（电机、电池、成本）而非控制软件才是瓶颈。共识大致是：90% 这个数字本身少有人反对，但讨论更倾向于认为它衡量的是基准的覆盖偏差，而不是 AI 的真实劳动力冲击。

**Worth reading**

## 3. Apple ML Research: Muse Spark Open with MLX on Mac Studio {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [machinelearning.apple.com](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/mlx-distributed-inference) · 192 points · 95 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50118320)*

A post on Apple's Machine Learning Research blog showing how to run very large open-weight LLMs locally with the latest MLX release on Mac Studio, including across multiple machines over RDMA on Thunderbolt 5. The headline demo runs the new ~816B-parameter MoE model Muse Spark Open (NVFP4 4-bit, about 493GB of weights) on a cluster of four Mac Studios at over 36 tokens/second; Apple also benchmarks Mistral Medium 3.5 (128B) and Qwen 3.8 (27B) on M5 Ultra versus M3 Ultra, claiming up to 4.6× faster time-to-first-token and ~1.6× faster generation thanks to the M5 GPU Neural Accelerators and 1.2 TB/s (vs 819 GB/s) memory bandwidth, at roughly 300W peak. It details pipeline versus tensor parallelism via the jaccl backend (MLX v0.30.1+, macOS 26.2's RDMA driver), the mlx.launch, mlx_lm.benchmark and mlx_lm.server helpers, and shows pointing OpenCode at a local server.

**Discussion**: The dominant thread is strategic: several commenters argue Apple is content selling the shovels while Meta and others spend billions training open-weight models, and that Apple doesn't need its own frontier model — a view countered by others who note Apple's AI stack already lags Nvidia, AMD and Google on training and inference tooling, and that Siri shows the bet hasn't paid off. A second cluster questions the economics, comparing a $10k–$40k Mac Studio setup against OpenRouter or subscription credits, with the rough consensus that local makes sense mainly for privacy, contractual or legal constraints, and for the future promise rather than today's price-performance. Practitioners push back on the benchmarks: one notes Apple compares Meta's model at 4-bit NVFP4 against Qwen at native BF16, and says Muse Spark wouldn't even be in his top ten choices for a 512GB M5; another reports real numbers for models he actually runs on the hardware. Other subthreads cover the Meta–Apple partnership (the submitter links Meta's blog, a Zuckerberg/Ternus photo and Axios coverage), complaints about background macOS daemons like mediaanalysisd and duetexpertd heating machines, the 「fraction of the power used by a common toaster」 line, and a long tangent about em dashes and generative AI.

**Worth reading**
- [peri-cl](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50119761): Argues the benchmark favors Meta's model by running Muse Spark at 4-bit NVFP4 while Qwen is at native BF16, a quantization most users wouldn't pick, and says Muse Spark wouldn't make his own top ten for a 512GB M5.
- [liuliu](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50120882): First-hand numbers from a real M5 Ultra owner: a single 256GiB machine runs DeepSeek 4.1 Flash 0910 at roughly 3,200 tok/s prefill and 45 tok/s, and a 128GiB M5 Max runs GLM 5.3 Flash, with quality verified on MathArena Apex and GPQA Diamond.
- [antipaul](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50121186): The submitter supplies the context the article omits: Meta's own Muse Spark Open release post, a Zuckerberg/Ternus photo, and Axios reporting that Apple and Meta buried the hatchet, quoting Meta AI chief Alexandr Wang.

## 5. What on Earth Is Dekton? {#story-5}
*#6 on HN · [worksinprogress.news](https://www.worksinprogress.news/p/porcelain) · 91 points · 30 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50118828)*

An essay by Charles C. Mann in Works in Progress (Issue 26) that starts from his irritation at a costly induction cooktop whose cooking surface — a material called Dekton, which cost about five times the ~$30/sq ft tempered glass he expected — had to be bought separately. Dekton is an artificial slab material launched in 2013 by the Spanish company Cosentino, made by pressing a slurry of ground porcelain, glass and quartz at 25,000 metric tons and firing it at 1,200°C; the article argues it is essentially Porcelain 3.0, and spends most of its length on porcelain's history, from kaolin clay fired above 1,200°C in northern China through Tschirnhaus and Böttger's 1708–10 breakthrough at Meissen to Dekton, which took six years and almost $200 million to develop.

**Discussion**: Most of the thread is practical countertop experience rather than history. One commenter argues the article misses the real driver behind porcelain's rise for countertops: 6mm slabs suit induction magnets and wall/floor use, engineered quartz is being squeezed by silicosis litigation and looming bans (Australia, possibly California), and porcelain can mimic marble cheaply — though a reply counters that quartz's hand-sprinkled depth and translucency is why people still pay for it. Several owners confirm Dekton shrugs off red wine, sizzling pans and scratching, but the sharpest disagreement is over brittleness: one commenter watched a 3x3 inch Home Depot sample shatter over ~25 square feet after a short fall, and others note chips from dropped pans or cracks in 1.2cm slabs; a further correction from Cosentino's own docs warns it dislikes thermal shock. A side thread notes that hidden under-counter induction tops (as opposed to normal CERAN glass-top units) are the source of the author's confusion, and another commenter questions whether the article's description of atoms diffusing across particle boundaries is physically accurate.

**Worth reading**
- [zebrafruit](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50120575): Explains the industry economics the article skips: thin 6mm porcelain works with induction and on walls/floors, silicosis litigation and threatened quartz bans are closing the cost gap, and porcelain is cheap 「printer paper」 for any stone pattern.
- [icodestuff](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50122481): First-hand counter-evidence to the marketing: a 3x3 inch Dekton sample dropped from under three feet exploded across ~25 square feet, and he switched to high-quality quartz rather than risk dropping things on it.
- [throwaway219450](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50120758): Corrects the article's framing, noting CERAN low-expansion glass tops are nothing new and the real confusion is the trendy hidden under-counter induction burner sold without a surface — plus a Huygens Optics video on CERAN.

## 6. Big Fish: Exploiting a 37-Year-Old Kernel Bug in OpenBSD {#story-6}
*#7 on HN · [calif.io](https://calif.io/research/big-fish) · 125 points · 35 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50118977)*

A research post from Calif (calif.io) describing a local privilege-escalation exploit chain in OpenBSD that traces back roughly 37 years to the original BSD sources, where the shared-memory attachment counter shm_nattch was declared as a signed 16-bit short. Forking a process with SysV shared-memory attachments pushes the counter past 32,767 so it wraps negative (the write-up wraps it to -32,643 by mapping 127 segments, munmap()ing them and forking 259 processes), yielding a use-after-free via a race with IPC_RMID plus a separate conditional -1 out-of-bounds write; the authors got local root with KARL, SMAP and SMEP enabled and no information-disclosure bug, using a 16-byte struct ip_moptions allocated via IP_MULTICAST_TTL and a close() that flips the PTE self-map from user to supervisor. Fixes were in errata 026 and 018, and the post proposes safe-unlinking, randomizing the self-referential PTE map through .openbsd.randomdata (PML4 slot 320 + (random_byte & 127), giving bases 0xffffa00000000000–0xffffdf8000000000 at 512-GiB intervals), allocator base/order randomization, UBSan array-bounds sanitizer (21 issues reported privately to FreeBSD) and Alloc Tokens.

**Discussion**: A large share of the thread argues about the write-up itself: one commenter asserts every post on the site is AI-generated, another says this particular post reads as entirely human, and a security instructor replies that either way the exploit explanation is poor — just seven terse bullet points, weaker than his undergraduates' write-ups — though he praises the mitigations section; a third calls that a no-win complaint. On substance, commenters note the NetBSD code was rewritten and the bug accidentally fixed there in August 1999, and several push back on the premise that OpenBSD is an exceptionally audited codebase, noting only about a dozen active developers and that only the kernel and base userland are audited, not ports. The longest exchange disputes OpenBSD's security reputation: one commenter calls the 「only two remote holes in the default install」 claim close to meaningless because most of the base services (httpd, relayd, ldapd, unbound, smtpd, bgpd, vmm, nfs, dhcpd) are disabled by default, others counter that a small default attack surface is a virtue and that only sshd and pf listen, and a correction notes that Cisco routers mostly run Linux while Juniper uses FreeBSD/Linux, not OpenBSD. Separately, a commenter who pointed an AI at the small OpenBSD tools cat and find reports finding multiple bugs including a buffer overflow dating to the NetBSD/OpenBSD split, prompting the usual memory-safety debate: some say moving to safe languages like Rust is the only lasting fix, while others argue no language is safe, formal verification or added C memory-safety features are cheaper than rewriting, and Rust brings supply-chain risk.

**Worth reading**
- [thomashabets2](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50119990): Reports that an AI pointed at the small OpenBSD utilities cat and find found multiple bugs, including a buffer overflow that predates the NetBSD/OpenBSD split, with a link arguing everything in C is undefined behaviour.
- [stackghost](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50120393): Strong counter-argument that OpenBSD's security reputation is largely a fiction: the 「two remote holes」 claim is diminished by a default install that does nothing, and real high-security deployments run Windows and COTS routers.
- [raddan](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50121141): A college security instructor argues the exploit write-up is too terse to follow — seven bullet points — while conceding the mitigations discussion is stronger.

## 7. Designing more efficient quantum circuits with Claude {#story-7}
*#8 on HN · [anthropic.com](https://www.anthropic.com/research/designing-efficient-quantum-circuits) · 22 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50121939)*

Anthropic research post describing an experiment in which Claude (specifically a model called Claude Mythos 5) was given plain-language specs for eight quantum-computing primitives from chemistry, optimization, physics simulation and number theory, plus an automated evaluator that scored designs on qubits × gates. Working in a closed loop with minimal human input — 53 hours on one task — the model produced circuits that beat the best published designs, including a new approach to modular inversion for elliptic-curve point addition that avoids storing divsteps branch history by decoding it from the numbers themselves, and a redesign making the costliest step of a leading quantum optimization algorithm about 12x cheaper. The post ties this to FeMoco simulation (gates estimated down from ~50 trillion in 2017 to ~340 million, ~150,000x) and credits Andi Gu (Anthropic STEM Fellows), Alek Kemeny, Susanne Yelin and Mikhail Lukin, with a paper on arXiv.

**Discussion**: The thread is tiny (5 comments) and almost entirely meta rather than technical — nobody engages with the circuits themselves. One commenter jokes that this is 「universal paper clips」 territory: the AI building quantum computing to make itself smarter. The most substantive comment accuses Anthropic of overselling its role, quoting the arXiv paper's acknowledgments, which say Andi Gu conducted 「part of this work」 under the STEM Fellows Program and thank IARPA, the Army Research Office and an NSF grant for the Harvard-side work — versus the blog's framing that the work 「was led by」 Gu as part of the program; the commenter calls the commercial framing essentially hallucinated, and no one rebuts the point in-thread. A third comment supplies a TL;DR claiming reductions of up to 17x and 12x by reconstructing temporary data and swapping in a better algorithm, and a fourth just mocks that acronym. There is no rough consensus on the science, only mild skepticism about the marketing.

**Worth reading**
- [12923817](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50122599): Quotes the arXiv paper's acknowledgments (Gu conducted 「part of this work」 under the STEM Fellows Program; Harvard work funded by IARPA/ARO/NSF) to argue Anthropic's blog framing overstates its role and is effectively hallucinated.
- [whoishiding](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50122490): Compact TL;DR of the result: eight simulated circuits redesigned, operations cut by up to 17x and 12x via reconstructing temporary data and replacing an algorithm.

## 8. I Love Software Rendering {#story-8}
