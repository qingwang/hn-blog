## 1. What Cloudflare Doesn't Tell You {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [charlie.fish](https://charlie.fish/posts/2026/11/what-cloudflare-doesnt-tell-you/) · 63 points · 28 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50632883)*

A post by Charlie Fish arguing that Cloudflare presents modern protocol support to visitors — TLS, IPv6, post-quantum key exchange — while the Cloudflare-to-origin leg may be plaintext HTTP over IPv4 only, so users and site owners get a false picture of the whole path. He cites Cloudflare's own docs stating that for proxied records with both A and AAAA records, Cloudflare prefers the IPv4 address when connecting to the origin, and argues the dashboard should proactively warn admins and that CDNs should be able to expose origin TLS and IP-version metadata.

**Discussion**: The thread splits on the transparency proposal: several commenters say exposing origin metadata contradicts Cloudflare's job of shielding origins, with one describing locking a server down entirely behind a Cloudflare Tunnel and refusing to give that up for 'some cozy feeling of transparency', and another comparing the idea to SSH advertising that the admin password is a well-known default. On the technical claims, commenters push back that Cloudflare already nudges users toward Full (strict) origin TLS, that you can use Cloudflare's origin certificate or better generate your own so Cloudflare never holds the key, and that the tunnel route makes origin certificates moot. Several question why the IPv4 preference matters at all, answering that IPv4 saves 4 bytes of per-packet overhead, that IPv6 routing was historically worse at some ISPs, and that most operators see little value in IPv6 — while one reports running an IPv6-only origin on port 443 behind Cloudflare for a year without problems. The most common complaint is the headline: several say the real story is just that Cloudflare prefers IPv4 for origin connections.

**Worth reading**
- [Velocifyer](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50633137): Explains the IPv4 preference mechanically — it saves 4 bytes of per-packet overhead inside 1500-byte frames — and notes you can force IPv6-to-origin by keeping the proxied AAAA record and deleting the A.
- [bastawhiz](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50633643): Strong counter-argument to exposing origin connection state: it is like SSH announcing to every visitor that the admin password is a well-known default, and shaming negligent admins is not worth the leak.
- [dantillberg](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50633363): Draws the distinction the article blurs: plaintext origin TLS is a real privacy problem, but it is unclear why Cloudflare using IPv4 rather than IPv6 to reach the origin matters to anyone.

## 2. Active Intrusion Campaign Against Check Point Security Management Servers {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [greynoise.io](https://www.greynoise.io/blog/check-point-security-management-servers-intrusion-campaign) · 205 points · 71 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50630594)*

A GreyNoise report on a campaign it tracks as GreyNoise Threat 13 (GT13) against Check Point Security Management Servers, with confirmed activity from October 17 to November 2, 2026, hitting at least 22 organizations across finance, energy, government, transport and manufacturing, spread over 17 countries from the UK and Germany to India, Vietnam and the US. The actor used 99 IPs (ExpressVPN egresses and hijacked Check Point boxes), 1,400+ sessions and 30+ Python scripts, plus Nmap, Chisel, Impacket and Paramiko, chaining CVE-2026-16232 (SmartConsole auth bypass to Run Script) to copy firewall databases and internal CA material from 24 servers and mint a pivot certificate. A notable detail: they first broke into a Rejetto HFS server by harvesting Math.random() output from login cookies to reconstruct the V8 PRNG state and forge an admin session, later locking down their hop against CVE-2026-98432 using nftables, and GreyNoise believes LLMs and agentic tooling drove much of the automation.

**Discussion**: The thread splits three ways. The loudest reaction is disbelief that a Math.random()-derived session signing key is still exploitable, with one commenter noting he poked at the same vector in Burp Suite a decade ago; others push back that it is a language-design trap (crypto.getRandomValues needs a secure context, though localhost counts) and that any competent audit or static-analysis rule should catch it. A long subthread argues over the report's claim of LLM-driven automation: a doomer predicts swarms of criminals pointing models at critical infrastructure, and gets rebutted with the argument that offenders respond to risk and profit rather than capability, backed by FBI complaint and scam-compound figures, and by a security veteran who says attack rates are not actually spiking and that HN overhypes cybersecurity. Practitioners focus on the boring cause — the initial access almost certainly required management interfaces exposed to the internet, and the consensus advice is to keep management planes off the public internet and behind an overlay network, noting that switching to pfSense does not help since Netgate shipped its own critical PHP fix. Several commenters also flag that the attackers patched their own relay against a Linux IPv4 use-after-free while Ubuntu was slow to ship the same fix, and read that as a sign of how fast patching now has to move.

**Worth reading**
- [omh](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50633125): Asks the question the write-up leaves open: how did initial access actually happen, and did all 22 victims really have their Check Point management servers reachable from the internet?
- [ba1afd89f34cb23](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50633416): Argues that passing traffic through an appliance rarely lets you exploit it, so the real fix is keeping management planes off the internet and control planes locked down — with a caveat about dynamic VPN endpoints and WAN BGP peering.
- [sigmar](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50630972): Points out that the actor used nftables to shield its compromised hop from CVE-2026-98432 while Ubuntu was still dragging its feet on the same fix, as documented in a linked HN thread.

## 5. Mechanical Pencil: Spray Bottle {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [mechanical-pencil.com](https://mechanical-pencil.com/products/spray-bottle) · 196 points · 56 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50598555)*

An interactive illustrated explainer from mechanical-pencil.com, a site devoted to 'an illustrated celebration of the engineering around us,' that takes apart an ordinary trigger spray bottle. It notes the modern spray bottle emerged in the early 1970s and that essentially all designs, despite variations in nozzle and trigger, rely on the same basic one-way-valve mechanism; the page animates the trigger stroke, reload, cross-section and exploded views to show how fluid keeps being drawn up and sprayed pull after pull. The site is a commercial product page for the illustrated breakdowns, with a pointer to Instagram for future teardowns.

**Discussion**: Most commenters loved it as a rare example of hand-made, effortful web design, comparing it to Bartosz Ciechanowski's ciechanow.ski and to German kids' show Die Sendung mit der Maus, and several asked for follow-ups (microwave, plastic bottle design, birds' lungs). The real technical argument is about the ball check valve: readers say the animation never explains why the ball moves down against the straw when the trigger is pressed rather than being pushed harder into the upper seat, and jrflo, who partly concedes the animation is confusing, suggests the ball actually rests against the top of the straw and is lifted by suction on release, with gravity returning it; others note most real pumps add a spring, and that gravity sealing would mean the sprayer fails upside down (it does, though mainly because the dip tube sucks air). A secondary thread turns hostile over whether AI made the page: the_af and sudobash1 cite the author's FAQ describing physical disassembly, CAD modelling and hand animation, while 0c3ca83 insists the graphics look AI-generated; a separate argument about the incentives for disclosing AI use spirals into an unproductive 'everyone cheats' debate. Smaller practical notes: some modern sprayers like the Dawn Dish Spray keep spraying briefly after the trigger is released, and commenters guess at pressurised chambers; others complain sprayer heads have become less reliable, blaming plastic springs, child-proof locks and the straw-to-chamber joint.

**Worth reading**
- [jrflo](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50631926): Argues the animation's valve seating is misleading: the ball likely rests on top of the straw and is lifted by suction on the release stroke, with gravity (not a spring) reseating it as a cost saving, linking to the Wikipedia check valve article.
- [the_af](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50631050): Quotes the author's FAQ — 'I take the product apart. CAD it up. Illustrate each view. Then animate and lay it out for the web' — to rebut the claim that the site is AI-generated.
- [SamBam](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50631619): Says the diagram never actually shows an empty piston chamber and that the label 'the piston chamber is emptied' is generous, drawing a parallel to the common but imprecise 'vacuum sucks water up a straw' explanation.

## 7. 2035 came early {#story-7}
*#7 on HN · [fzakaria.com](https://fzakaria.com/2026/11/23/2035-came-early) · 866 points · 324 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50624497)*

A blog post by Farid Zakaria arguing that Gary Bernhardt's 2035-set talk 'The Birth & Death of JavaScript' has effectively arrived early, thanks to LLMs. His site trynix.dev, built on nixpkgs-multiverse, now boots GUI programs — solitaire, GL gears, xeyes, audio, Firefox — from over 300,000 distinct Nix package versions straight in the browser, after he swapped the slow qemu-wasm backend for unkernel.dev (Linux compiled to WebAssembly, published by Pooya Parsa on Nov 19, 2026, shared by Domen Kožar on Nov 20). unkernel runs the kernel at wasm speed and only emulates user programs, claiming 2x–32x speedups, plus Wayland compositing via wland, a Mesa/virgl build rendering through WebGL2, ALSA audio, and TCP-over-WebSocket networking via a Wisp relay; the finale is Firefox running on Linux-in-WASM inside a Firefox tab, loading trynix.dev and Firefox again, which took over an hour to render.

**Discussion**: The thread's most-discussed comment is from gbl08ma, who spent ~100 hours (largely prompting Claude) on a competing project, waarm64.com, and was two weeks from announcing when unkernel took all the attention; he says his is faster and more feature-complete but he has lost motivation. Dozens of replies encourage him to keep going, arguing competition validates a category; others note they no longer feel the same joy now that anyone can prompt the same thing. A large parallel argument is about the framing: many commenters object that almost none of this is JavaScript but WebAssembly, that the goalposts moved, and that the whole thing is old news given Fabrice Bellard's JSLinux from 2011 — countered by those who say asm.js/WASM was always the point of the talk. Others debate practical use: university Linux course environments, agent sandboxes, and just doing shell work on a locked-down Windows machine, versus skeptics who say it's a novelty with no production use case and that WASM is not a real sandbox when the engines and kernel are still C/C++. A separate long branch is a JavaScript-hate thread, where a retired former manager says JS drove him into management and LLMs gave him back the fun, drawing pushback about 'reverse centaurs' and about retirees not bearing the consequences.

**Worth reading**
- [gbl08ma](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50627172): The author of the competing waarm64.com project describes spending 100+ hours building it largely by prompting Claude, being two weeks from launch when unkernel landed, and losing the will to continue despite claiming better performance and a fuller feature set.
- [markasoftware](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50625792): A detailed technical teardown of the Wisp protocol unkernel uses for networking: it came out of a high-school project for bypassing school network filters (Scramjet), and its CONTINUE-based flow control lacks absolute offsets, so peers can't reliably know how many bytes are still accepted.
- [boxed](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50625490): Argues the prediction was about JavaScript but this is WebAssembly, so the accelerated timeline largely comes from moving the goalposts.

## 8. A training engine for your local model, using your idle compute {#story-8}

**Worth reading**

## 9. Splitting USB-C: USB 3 and USB 2 on one port {#story-9}
*#9 on HN · [frankchiarulli.com](https://www.frankchiarulli.com/blog/splitting-usb-c/) · 75 points · 58 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50629106)*

Frank Chiarulli describes Dongle-Pro v2, a board for his restorekit.org project that puts a Mac into DFU recovery mode over USB-PD, and the experiment behind it: instead of using a USB 3 hub chip like the GL3510 in his earlier 5 Gbps design, he keeps a cheap CH334P USB 2 hub on D+/D- and passes the SuperSpeed lanes straight through two CH9441P 2:1 muxes with no redriver. The board negotiates a full 10 Gbps USB 3.2 Gen 2 link while the dongle's hub and CH32X035G8U6 MCU enumerate at 480 Mb/s and 12 Mb/s on the same physical port, and after swapping to a HUSB311 PD PHY and cheaper ESD parts it costs $2.93 per board at qty 15 versus $5.03 for the USB 2-only Lite and $10.44 for Pro v1, on a 21 x 48 mm PCB. He also notes that macOS exposes the pairing via a UsbProtocolCompanion property and Linux via a port/peer sysfs symlink, so software can still tell which target is on which dongle.

**Discussion**: The thread's central pushback, led by arcticbull, is that this is not a supported configuration: USB 3 devices are required by spec to be able to fall back to USB 2 and the two links share one port power control and one logical port identifier, with a TI forum post quoted saying the arrangement enumerates and routes traffic but is not recommended. jsmith45 adds a concrete failure mode: hosts rely on ACPI/device-tree 2.0/3.x port pairing to cut power to the right port, so a split topology can cause power to be cut on the wrong port or the wrong device blamed for an over-current warning. The author accepts the criticism, says it was mostly an experiment with medium confidence it would even work, and is unsure whether to ship this SKU or the spec-compliant but pricier BOM; one commenter argues that for an expert tool 'who cares if it works'. Much of the rest of the thread drifts to USB-C generally: appreciation for USB-A's four pins, complaints that USB-PD negotiation produces constant 'why isn't this charging' mysteries (including a power bank that wakes up as a sink and drains the phone), an argument over whether multi-lane USB 3 is a parallel bus, and drum55's note that some motherboards wire a separate port to each side of a reversible connector instead of implementing flip detection.

**Worth reading**
- [jsmith45](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50631603): Explains that hosts map 2.0 and 3.x ports via ACPI/device tree for per-port power control, so splitting the topology can cut power to or blame the wrong device.
- [arcticbull](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50631968): Argues the arrangement is non-compliant per USB 3.2 and quotes a TI engineer saying simultaneous USB 2 and USB 3 on one downstream port is unsupported, while still calling the project cool.
- [fcjr](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50633221): The author thanks commenters for the TI link, confirms it was an experiment, and says he is unsure whether to ship this design or the spec-compliant but costlier BOM.

## 10. Show HN: Mason – a lightweight Postgres read-replica, built on columnar storage {#story-10}
*#11 on HN · [masondata.ai](https://masondata.ai/) · 49 points · 5 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50630294)*

Mason is an open-source, drop-in replacement for a Postgres read replica: a single Node.js process that syncs selected tables and columns from Postgres into DuckDB's columnar engine and rewrites incoming Postgres SQL into the form DuckDB runs fastest. The team evaluated 5,410 slow, hand-written queries from 159 real Postgres databases against pg_clickhouse (59.4% exact match), cedarDB (88.1%) and DuckDB (84.1%) before choosing DuckDB for its open-source license, and claims Mason now matches Postgres on 96% of those queries, erroring clearly on the remaining 4%. Its own benchmarks on an AWS m7g.xlarge show a count-by-status over 100M rows dropping from 216s to 0.46s (~467×) and a revenue-by-month join from 335s to 96.7s, while primary-key lookups are 2.8–6× slower than Postgres; it also ships RBAC, row-level security via foreign-key following, generated tables, an MCP server for AI agents, and Prometheus/Grafana metrics.
