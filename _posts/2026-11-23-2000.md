## 1. Nvidia Reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO {#story-1}
*#1 on HN · [cnbc.com](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/nvidia-jensen-huang-ceo.html) · 1,367 points · 2,518 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50611576)*

A CNBC report on Nvidia’s board reinstating Jensen Huang as president, CEO and board director effective immediately, 11 days after he resigned over reports of extramarital affairs, including one with a woman alleged to be a Chinese spy. The board said it acted unanimously after input from senior U.S. government officials and is confident his return poses no national security risk; Nvidia shares rose 33%, recovering roughly the ground lost since the scandal broke. An independent committee’s Wachtell-led investigation continues, and Huang is scheduled for congressional testimony on export controls in early December.

**Discussion**: Most commenters reacted with anger and cynicism, calling the reversal proof that wealth and political connections override corporate governance, national security concerns and basic accountability; many cited the 33% stock jump and speculated about insider trading or lawsuits. A vocal minority pushed back: they argued there is still no public evidence Huang broke export laws or that Alice Huang was a spy, noted that agencies had said as much, and said the board overreacted to leaks and media pressure. Several focused on key-person risk, arguing Nvidia’s dependence on Huang’s 30-year relationships with suppliers such as TSMC made a clean succession nearly impossible, while others compared the episode to Sam Altman’s 2023 OpenAI firing and return as evidence that formal boards are often overruled by investors or political power. Commenters split on whether the affairs and relationships with subordinates were disqualifying or irrelevant; some said the HR and harassment exposure is a serious shareholder liability. The rough consensus, even among those defending the reinstatement on the evidence, was that the episode badly damaged confidence in corporate governance and increased political risk for AI companies.

## 2. "Per ARM's Request, This Site Will Be Discontinued on 4 December 2026" {#story-2}
*#2 on HN · [arm.jonpalmisc.com](https://arm.jonpalmisc.com/) · 208 points · 33 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50617499)*

arm.jonpalmisc.com is an unofficial, searchable reference for the AArch64 instruction set and Armv8-A/Armv9-A system registers — Arm's ISA documentation rebuilt as a fast static site without the pop-ups, login gates and bloat of Arm's own developer portal. Its author, jonpalmisc, posted that Arm's lawyers notified him to take the site down or face legal action, citing use of Arm's trademarks and IP, and set 4 December 2026 as the shutdown date; he says he is looking for a way to keep existing bookmarks and links working. The site is a WebAssembly-powered search front end over content converted from Arm's manuals.

**Discussion**: The dominant reaction is irritation at Arm for going after a resource that only helps people use its chips; several commenters note the Streisand effect, predicting a dozen mirrors within a day (the author himself confirms people have already offered to host mirrors). The main counter-argument, and the closest thing to consensus on the legal question, is that this is not a fair-use dispute: the text is taken verbatim from Arm's copyrighted manuals and the copyright, patent and export-control notices were stripped, which is exactly what corporate lawyers object to. Commenters also compare the site to other long-standing rehosts of the same material and point out that Arm already publishes the same instruction descriptions on developer.arm.com under DDI0487 — just behind a tracking, login and terms-acceptance flow that people describe as slow and unpleasant. A secondary thread is practical preservation: the author says open-sourcing the code minus Arm's content is 'an option I am considering', an archive.org snapshot is circulating, and one commenter confirms a plain 'wget -m' works because the site is static.

**Worth reading**

## 3. Ladybird is now in alpha {#story-3}
*#3 on HN · [ladybird.org](https://ladybird.org/posts/alpha/) · 848 points · 230 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50614249)*

The Ladybird project announced its first alpha release, downloadable for macOS and Linux, running on an engine written from scratch rather than forked from Chromium, WebKit or Gecko. The post promises a rolling channel with automatic updates, in-browser broken-site reporting and opt-in crash reports, support for WebDriver and the Firefox DevTools protocol, and asks security researchers to attack it: 'we make no promises about security today.' It also thanks sponsors and donors and asks web developers to try their sites and report what breaks.

**Discussion**: The dominant reaction is surprise at how usable it already is: many commenters are posting from Ladybird itself, reporting YouTube, Google Maps, SVG-heavy apps and their own sites rendering correctly, with complaints limited to jerky scrolling and lag on media-heavy pages. A second thread contests the memory story — several boast 50–100MB, but one commenter who benchmarked dozens of browsers says Ladybird used more than ungoogled Chromium for a single-page session, noting tabs are separate processes and measurements are muddled by shared libraries. Security is the sharpest split: some argue an alpha nobody targets is low-risk and sandboxing it (bubblewrap, firejail, separate users) is enough, while others insist a browser runs untrusted code next to your SSH keys and cookies, and one commenter says a quick LLM pass over LibTLS turned up a missing timer.start() call and an over-wide filesystem grant. Independence from Google is widely praised, though a few point out a non-profit still answers to its sponsors, and the heavy LLM involvement (agents filling in Web Platform Tests, the Rust port) draws both enthusiasm and worries about review and copyright provenance.

**Worth reading**
- [breakwaterlabs](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50617073): A commenter reports that pointing a recent model at Ladybird's LibTLS and sandbox code surfaced a missing timer.start() in TLSv12.cpp and a commit fixing an over-wide filesystem write grant, arguing the security posture isn't what a browser needs, while noting LibTLS only covers some paths such as DNS-over-TLS.
- [simonw](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50614718): Quotes the project's October 2026 newsletter: Ladybird passed Safari Technology Preview on Web Platform Tests (2,184,722 subtests, +75,650 in a month), with most of the gain coming in five days after AI coding agents were pointed at WPT.

## 4. GLM-5.4: Toward generalized coding with our first loop transformer {#story-4}
*#4 on HN · [z.ai](https://z.ai/blog/glm-5.4) · 905 points · 308 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50609685)*

A Z.ai blog post announcing GLM-5.4, which Zhipu describes as its first 'loop transformer' — a model that reuses a block of layers across multiple passes instead of stacking all layers once. Because the article text could not be fetched, details come from the post as quoted by commenters: the loop architecture was validated across 1B, 4B, 30B and 750B base models, with the claim that a ~750B looped model matches a 2–3T parameter model, and that a looped 30B model beat its unlooped twin at identical parameter count on ~10% more training compute. Weights are promised as an open release only after 'safety hardening', the post includes no benchmark table, and it teases a much larger successor 'ready soon'.

*Article not read: page had almost no readable text (probably needs JavaScript or a login)*

**Discussion**: The thread splits into three arguments. Technically, several people corrected the popular framing that looping saves memory bandwidth: the looped weights still have to be fetched from HBM on every pass, so looping buys more compute per parameter (less VRAM capacity) rather than less bandwidth per token, and helps mainly at high concurrency. Others questioned whether this is really a new generation at all: one commenter says GLM-5.4 is a pretrained model converted to looped form rather than trained looped from scratch, and predicts a wave of cheap benchmark-grabbing conversions. On safety, a camp argues looping is 'the forbidden technique' that pushes reasoning into hidden states and destroys chain-of-thought monitorability; others reply that the model still emits ordinary CoT, that the change is equivalent to a deeper model, and that closed labs already hide their traces anyway. The most repeated practical complaint is 'marathon thinking' — Chinese models including DeepSeek and MiniMax burning 10+ minutes on trivial edits — though defenders say thinking budgets can be lowered, that the issue is really tokens/sec, and that US models simply don't show their reasoning. A recurring meta-critique is the missing benchmarks, which some read as evidence the results aren't impressive and others as deliberate restraint.

**Worth reading**
- [loubbrad](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50611012): Corrects the thread's most-upvoted explanation: looping does not reduce memory bandwidth, since reused layer weights must still be re-read from HBM each pass — it trades memory capacity for compute, not bandwidth for compute.
- [lolokazaki123](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50611759): Claims GLM-5.4 is a pretrained model converted to looped form rather than trained looped from scratch, and predicts a wave of similar cheap conversions chasing benchmark gains.
- [Tadpole9181](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50616657): Counters the safety panic with specifics: only the middle 28 layers run twice, emulating a 106-layer model in 78 layers, and the model still outputs a plain human-language CoT, so nothing extra is hidden away.

## 5. NariQ: BF16-level Qwen3.8-27B in a 10 GB file {#story-5}
*#5 on HN · [huggingface.co](https://huggingface.co/nari-labs/Qwen3.8-27B-NariQ-GGUF) · 49 points · 8 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50611402)*

nari-labs released NariQ v1 GGUFs of Qwen3.8-27B at 3, 2.5 and 2 bits per weight — the 3-bit file is roughly 10 GB — using a trellis-coded post-training quantization calibrated on real coding-agent traces and built on QTIP/GSQ-RCO work. Their benchmarks claim the 3-bit and 2.5-bit files match BF16 on long-horizon agentic tasks: DeepSWE 41.0 and 41.9 fully solved tasks vs 39.8 for BF16 (out of 113), Terminal-Bench 2.1 63.8/60.2 vs 62.6, AIME 98.3/96.5 vs 97.7, with the 2-bit file clearly weaker (DeepSWE 28.6, Terminal-Bench 52.4). The catch is a required llama.cpp fork with new tensor types that stock llama.cpp, Ollama and LM Studio cannot load; it is tuned for SM120 (RTX PRO 6000) and runs at only ~3.8 tok/s on an M3 Pro, with AMD and Intel GPUs unsupported and GeForce cards untested.

**Discussion**: The thread splits into practical usability and technical scrutiny of the format. On usability, the Metal number dominates: one commenter argues 3.8 tok/s makes it pointless next to a much smaller model running at ~69 tok/s, since a 1000-token reply would take four minutes, while another asks whether it can be squeezed onto a 16 GB Mac and wants a comparison against Bonsai 2 27B. The sharpest pushback is technical — mmastrac argues NariQ looks like a derivative of exl3's mul1 codebook, that its two changes (6-bit digits, skipped E4M3 rounding) each move error by only ~0.1%, and that the pmcgx codebook in exlr8 beats it by 1.1–3.1% at low bitrates, offering to run a direct head-to-head on Qwen3.8-27B. Others ask the more basic question of what information a 3-bit file actually loses and whether it is simply redundancy, which nobody resolves. No commenter reports running the weights on their own workload, so the benchmarks are neither confirmed nor refuted here.

**Worth reading**
- [mmastrac](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50617252): mmastrac (working on exlr8) claims NariQ is derived from exl3's mul1 codebook, pastes side-by-side codebooks, and says a Claude-assisted head-to-head on pseudo-weights shows pmcgx has 1.1–3.1% less error, offering to quantize Qwen3.8-27B for a real comparison.
- [vorticalbox](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50617744): Argues the 3.8 tok/s Metal speed makes this impractical versus a smaller model at ~69 tok/s, since a 1000-token reply would take about four minutes.
- [Retro_Dev](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50617761): Raises the core concern that benchmark parity at 3 bits cannot cover everything in the training data, asking what information is actually being discarded.

The captured text also includes quote-tweet-style commentary, part of it in Chinese, framing Amodei as having told a congressional hearing that open-weight AI is heading somewhere extremely dangerous because once weights are released you can no longer monitor users, cut off access, or enforce red lines. **Discussion**: The thread splits between people who read Amodei's safety stance as a regulatory moat and those who insist the safety camp is sincere — pointing to Geoff Hinton leaving Google and safety staff taking pay cuts, countered by the observation that every trillion-dollar AI CEO now talks about safety and that LeCun himself walked away from Meta. ## 8. Spacecraft reaches Mercury on second-ever mission to enter its orbit {#story-8}
*#8 on HN · [theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/nov/20/spacecraft-mercury-second-ever-mission-orbit-bepicolombo) · 205 points · 68 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50587439)*

The Guardian reports that BepiColombo, the €1.6bn joint ESA/JAXA mission, has arrived at Mercury after an eight-year, roughly 6bn-mile cruise and is firing its thrusters to become only the second spacecraft ever to orbit the planet, after NASA's MESSENGER. In early December the stack splits into ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter, which will map the surface and probe the planet's oversized core, and JAXA's magnetospheric orbiter, which will study the magnetic field and sodium exosphere; science begins in April, with about three years of operations before the probes run out of fuel and crash. The piece stresses how hard Mercury is to reach (the sun's gravity accelerates the craft, which must constantly brake) and quotes ESA's Geraint Jones and Leicester's Emma Bunce.

**Discussion**: The thread splits into three conversations. One group works out the mission's hardware and its ultimate fate: the stack carries a fuel-laden transfer module (around 1,400 kg of propellant) because gravity assists alone are not enough, and several commenters press on why the orbiters will eventually spiral into the surface — the best answer being that Mercury's lumpy gravity plus the sun's tidal pull stretch the orbit until its low point intersects the ground, with solar radiation pressure as a secondary perturbation. A second, larger argument addresses how hard Mercury orbit is in delta-v terms: a direct Hohmann transfer needs roughly 13 km/s (about 5.5 leaving Earth plus 7.5 braking at Mercury) while BepiColombo has only about 5 km/s of capability, hence the years of Earth, Venus and Mercury flybys; one commenter notes it is marginally cheaper in delta-v to send a probe to impact Alpha Centauri than to circularize at Mercury (21.5 vs 21.7 km/s). A third thread notes an 'unexpected challenge' during orbit insertion that the Guardian piece never mentions, with links to ESA's own post saying the spacecraft is safely in orbit 'despite' it, and criticizes ESA's communications as slow and relentlessly positive. Side threads cover why non-NASA missions get so little coverage (European readers agree it is the same for them), a long Kerbal Space Program detour about patched-conic versus n-body modeling, and whether a private company like SpaceX could do this — most say only if someone funds the science, and that the delta-v, not the will, is the blocker.

**Worth reading**
- [dantillberg](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50614344): Explains the real cause of the probes' eventual demise: Mercury's irregular gravity field and the sun's varying pull elongate the orbit at both ends until periapsis hits the surface — not drag or energy loss.
- [Tuna-Fish](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50615157): Striking delta-v comparison: starting from Earth's surface, impacting Alpha Centauri (21.5 km/s) is cheaper than circularizing around Mercury (21.7 km/s) without gravity assists.
- [teamonkey](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50617171): Points to ESA's own status post confirming BepiColombo is safely in Mercury orbit 'despite an unexpected challenge', filling in the insertion anomaly the article omits.

## 9. Nospeaker ─ Make your MacBook's trackpad talk using its haptic motor {#story-9}
*#9 on HN · [github.com](https://github.com/os6/nospeaker) · 220 points · 123 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50610318)*

Cody Collier's nospeaker is a single-file Swift program (no dependencies) that drives a Force Touch trackpad's haptic motor at up to 4,000 times a second to produce audible sound, so a muted MacBook can 'speak' through the glass. It uses the private MultitouchSupport framework — MTActuationCreateFromDictionary to describe a vibration as sine tones and MTActuationActuate to play it (Apple's public API only exposes three fixed taps, and the names were learned from the open-source Tactile project). macOS's own `say` output is analyzed every 10 ms by linear prediction into the two strongest formants plus loudness and pitch, which the motor plays as two tones restarted on each pitch beat; measured output is usable from 98 Hz to 3,951 Hz with the cleanest, loudest notes around 1 kHz. It needs a Force Touch trackpad and macOS 12+ on Apple Silicon or 13+ on Intel, was tested only on an M5 Max MacBook Pro, never exceeds the motor's normal maximum strength, and ships with a curl-pipe-to-sh installer plus a notarized zip.

**Discussion**: Most of the 120+ comments ignore the software and marvel at the underlying illusion — several long-time MacBook users only learned from the thread that the trackpad has no physical click, and others describe the 'rigor mortis' feeling when a crashed or dead machine's pad stops clicking. That consensus splinters into a hardware argument: one camp calls Apple's trackpad head and shoulders above the market, while others say ThinkPad pads work better with dry hands, that dragging and accidental heavy presses are annoying, or that Dell XPS 16 and Asus ExpertBook Ultra pads now use the same tech and feel equivalent. On durability, doubters worry 4,000 actuations a second will wear out the motor and void the warranty, and the counter-argument points to Steam Controller haptics that already play sounds and to the Taptic Engine being structurally close to a speaker. Only one commenter claims to have run it: soci reports it is startlingly loud and, as an unintended bonus, speaks in the Mac's system language (Catalan in their case). A separate nostalgia wing trades links to floppy-drive and PC-speaker music (Commodore 1541, Monkey Island 2, Sim City 2000 on a soundcard-less 486), and several people complain the README has no demo video and post X/Instagram and YouTube-short links instead.

**Worth reading**
- [soci](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50617654): The only commenter who says they actually ran it: it is surprisingly loud, speaks in the Mac's configured system language (Catalan for them), and they suspect it is damaging the motor.
- [kccqzy](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50615681): Pushes back on the 'haptics were a cost-down' theory, arguing the old physical trackpads on 2011–2012 MacBook Pros felt uneven, collected dust and had inconsistent release, so the haptic pad was a genuine quality improvement.
- [Rohansi](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50612670): Offers evidence against imminent motor failure: the Steam Controller uses similar haptic motors, officially plays a few sounds, and has been pushed to play full music.

## 14. What's the difference between an integer and a pointer? (2018) {#story-14}
*#16 on HN · [blog.regehr.org](https://blog.regehr.org/archives/1621) · 92 points · 78 comments · [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50587286)*

A 2018 blog post by John Regehr, written as an alternate introduction to a then-forthcoming research paper, arguing that in performance-oriented systems languages (C, C++, Rust) pointers must not be reasoned about as pointer-sized integers. He shows a small C program where a pointer fabricated via integer arithmetic and a cast is treated by both GCC and Clang at -O3 as aliasing the original heap cell — but changing the computed offset to the observed literal value -96 makes GCC stop believing the store through p clobbers x, even though x and p have the same value. He notes the C standard neither requires nor forbids this, and points to the LLVM pointer-provenance paper being presented at OOPSLA that November.

**Discussion**: The thread splits over how much intptr_t/uintptr_t actually guarantees. One side (sparkie, HarHarVeryFunny, kevin_thibedeau) argues the standard only promises that a pointer→integer→pointer round trip compares equal, not that the result can be dereferenced, and that intptr_t is optional precisely because pointers may be segmented, tagged or multi-word, making them not bit vectors at all; the other side (uecker, afdbcreid) replies that this is a far-fetched reading of the wording, that an implementation that can't support the round trip simply shouldn't define the types, and that a footnote says the mapping is 'intended to be consistent with the addressing structure of the execution environment'. A related subthread argues over GCC's -O3 behavior: some call it a semantic change under optimization, while others say the compiler is entitled to it because C's semantics are the standard's, not the programmer's intuition. Practical notes include CHERI, Harvard architectures, segmented 8086, ARM MTE's top-byte tags, and amd64 pointer canonicalization making pointers behave more like sign-extended integers than unsigned ones. Martin Uecker himself appears to say the ISO document TS 6010 is not yet implemented by GCC or Clang, and Rust's official strict-provenance API is cited as the one place where these rules are now actually spelled out.

**Worth reading**
- [quelsolaar](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50616248): A member of the WG14 memory-model study group argues the example is not valid standard C — an integer is only guaranteed to convert back to a valid pointer if it was derived from a pointer and left unmodified — and notes that banked or hash-plus-offset pointers like CHERI's would break this even when the arithmetic is done on integers.
- [tialaramex](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50616470): Points out that Rust now formally documents strict provenance (Aria's 'Tower of Weakenings' API) while C remains murkier, that LLVM still miscompiles programs by giving integers provenance, and that provenance exists in every real systems language because without it pointers strangle obvious optimizations.
- [tialaramex](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50616561): Cites WG14 defect report 260 directly as evidence the C standards committee does not agree that pointer-to-integer conversion is merely implementation-defined in the way the prior commenter claims.

- [GuB-42](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50617306): A counter-argument to the outrage: Original pods are already only 5-6 g versus 7 g for a traditional Italian espresso, so managing a decent cup under 5 g would be genuinely impressive and arguably less wasteful.
- [dotancohen](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50605682): First-hand account from someone whose client was the local Nestlé distributor: they described obsessive engineering — nitrogen-packed capsules, very high machine pressure, special seals, minimal stray water between cycles — and says he trusts them to get the coffee itself right.
